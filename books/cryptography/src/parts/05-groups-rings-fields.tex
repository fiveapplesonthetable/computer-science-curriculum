\chapter{Groups, Rings, and Fields}

By now you have spent two chapters (Chapters~3 and~4) living inside the integers modulo $n$: adding, multiplying, finding inverses, hunting for primes. The same moves keep coming back. Addition mod $n$ behaves like ordinary addition. The nonzero residues mod a prime behave like ordinary multiplication. Inverses appear when $\gcd(a,n) = 1$. These patterns are instances of three \emph{algebraic structures}---sets paired with operations that obey a fixed list of rules---called groups, rings, and fields.

Naming these structures lets us prove a fact once from the rules alone and reuse it across different settings, from modular arithmetic in RSA and Diffie--Hellman to points on a curve in elliptic-curve cryptography (ECC). One question drives most of the chapter: when you repeat an operation inside a finite set until it loops back to its starting point, how long can that loop be? Answering it explains why RSA decryption recovers the original message and how to choose safe parameters for Diffie--Hellman and elliptic curves.

\chapterline

\section{Why crypto needs algebra at all}
\label{ch05:why-algebra}

Cryptography relies on arithmetic that is \emph{easy one way and hard the
other}. You want a function that legitimate parties can evaluate quickly and an
adversary cannot invert. Plain arithmetic over the integers $\Z$ cannot provide this: addition,
multiplication, and integer exponentiation are all fast to undo.

The remedy is to do arithmetic inside a \emph{finite} set that wraps around. Fix a modulus $n$ and a base $g$. Sending a secret integer $x$ to $g^x \bmod n$---the map $x \mapsto g^x \bmod n$---scrambles values enough that recovering $x$ from $g^x \bmod n$ (the discrete logarithm problem from Chapter~4) has no known fast algorithm for large, well-chosen $n$. Wrap-around creates the gap between the forward direction (fast modular exponentiation) and the backward direction (recovering the exponent).

Once you commit to finite, wrap-around arithmetic, you need to know which
familiar rules survive. Can you always divide? When does an inverse exist? How many
times can you repeat an operation before it cycles back to where it started? The theorems of groups,
rings, and fields answer those questions and give us the language in which security claims can be stated and proved.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Think of an algebraic structure as a \emph{game with rules}. A group is the minimal game where you
can combine two elements, undo any move, and never leave the board. A ring adds a second
operation so you have both addition and multiplication. A field adds the guarantee that
you can \emph{divide} by anything nonzero. Cryptographic schemes are built inside these structures, and security arguments rest on what their rules permit or forbid.
\end{intuitionbox}

\section{Groups}
\label{ch05:groups}

\subsection{The definition, and why each axiom earns its place}

To study an operation on its own, we write $\ast$ as a placeholder for whatever rule combines two elements (such as addition mod $n$ or multiplication mod $n$). A \emph{binary operation} $\ast : G \times G \to G$ takes any ordered pair $(a,b)$ of elements from $G$---the set of all such pairs is written $G \times G$---and produces a single result $a \ast b$.

\begin{definition}[Group]
\label{ch05:def-group}
A \emph{group} is a set $G$ together with a binary operation $\ast : G \times G \to G$
satisfying four foundational rules, called \emph{axioms}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Closure.} For all $a,b \in G$, the result $a \ast b$ belongs to $G$.
  \item \textbf{Associativity.} For all $a,b,c \in G$, $(a \ast b)\ast c = a \ast (b \ast c)$.
  \item \textbf{Identity.} There is an element $e \in G$ (the \emph{identity element}) with $e \ast a = a \ast e = a$ for every $a \in G$.
  \item \textbf{Inverses.} For every $a \in G$ there is an element $a^{-1} \in G$ (the \emph{inverse} of $a$) with
        $a \ast a^{-1} = a^{-1} \ast a = e$.
\end{enumerate}
If in addition $a \ast b = b \ast a$ for all $a,b \in G$, the group is \emph{abelian}
(or \emph{commutative}: the order of the two inputs does not change the result). Every group in this book is finite (has finitely many elements) and abelian unless we say otherwise. The number of elements in $G$ is written $\abs{G}$ and is also called the \emph{order of the group}.
\end{definition}

Each axiom earns its place. Closure keeps every combination inside $G$, so you can chain moves indefinitely without leaving the set. Associativity lets
you write $a \ast b \ast c \ast d$ without parentheses, which makes
\emph{repeated} application of the operation (and hence exponentiation) unambiguous. Identity gives you a
``do nothing'' element, the starting value that $g^0$ refers to. Inverses make every step reversible, which is why encryption built from a
group operation can be undone by whoever holds the right key.

\begin{focusbox}
What is \emph{not} required matters too: there is no second operation, no notion of ``bigger''
or ``smaller,'' and no division outside the single operation's inverse. The definition is minimal on purpose.
Because there are only four axioms, many different objects qualify---integers under
addition, coprime residues under multiplication, points on an elliptic curve, rotations
of a cube---and any theorem proved from the four axioms holds for all of them at once.
\end{focusbox}

\subsection{The two groups you already know}

You have met two groups already without the name.

\textbf{Additive group $\Zn{n}$.} Take the residues $\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}$ with the
operation ``add, then reduce mod $n$.'' The identity is $0$; the inverse of $a$ is $(n-a) \bmod n$
(so $0$ is its own inverse, and $a \ne 0$ has inverse $n-a$). Closure and associativity come straight from ordinary
addition. This group has $n$ elements for every integer $n \ge 1$.

\textbf{Multiplicative group $\Zns{n}$.} Take the residues that are \emph{coprime} to $n$,
with the operation ``multiply, then reduce mod $n$.'' The identity is $1$. Why throw away the non-coprime residues? Because of axiom~4 (inverses). Recall from Chapter~4
that $a$ has a multiplicative inverse mod $n$ if and only if $\gcd(a,n)=1$. To make
\emph{every} element invertible, we keep only the coprime residues. The size of this group is Euler's totient $\varphi(n) = \abs{\Zns{n}}$.

\begin{example}[$\Zns{15}$ by hand]
\label{ch05:ex-zns15}
The residues coprime to $15 = 3\cdot 5$ are
$\{1,2,4,7,8,11,13,14\}$, so $\varphi(15)=8$. Check closure and inverses on a few products:
\[
  2 \cdot 8 = 16 \equiv 1 \pmod{15}, \qquad
  4 \cdot 4 = 16 \equiv 1 \pmod{15}, \qquad
  7 \cdot 13 = 91 = 6\cdot 15 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{15}.
\]
Each pair is mutually inverse ($4$ is its own inverse). By contrast, $5 \notin \Zns{15}$ because
$\gcd(5,15)=5 \neq 1$: indeed $5 \cdot 3 = 15 \equiv 0 \pmod{15}$, and an element that multiplies a nonzero value to $0$ cannot have an inverse.
\end{example}

\begin{trapbox}
A common slip: thinking $\Zns{n}$ is all of $\{1,\dots,n-1\}$. That holds only when
$n$ is prime. For composite $n$ you must drop every residue sharing a factor with $n$.
Forgetting this causes ``why is my inverse code returning garbage''
bugs: the modular inverse does not exist for those inputs.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Group notation: additive vs. multiplicative}

The same abstract group can be written in two notations, and cryptography uses both:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Concept} & \textbf{Additive notation} & \textbf{Multiplicative notation} \\
\midrule
Combine $a$ and $b$ & $a + b$ & $a \cdot b$ (or $ab$) \\
Identity element & $0$ & $1$ \\
Inverse of $a$ & $-a$ & $a^{-1}$ \\
Repeat $k > 0$ times & $k \cdot a = \underbrace{a + \cdots + a}_{k\text{ terms}}$ & $a^k = \underbrace{a \cdot a \cdots a}_{k\text{ factors}}$ \\
Zero / negative steps & $0 \cdot a = 0,\quad (-k)\cdot a = k\cdot(-a)$ & $a^0 = 1,\quad a^{-k} = (a^{-1})^k$ \\
Typical setting & Elliptic curves, $(\Zn{n}, +)$ & $\Zns{p}$, $\Zns{n}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Elliptic-curve cryptography uses additive notation, so the secret-key operation is multiplying a curve point $P$ by an integer $k$ (called a \emph{scalar}), meaning adding $P$ to itself $k$ times. Protocols in $\Zns{p}$ use multiplicative notation, so the corresponding operation is raising $g$ to an integer power $g^k$. Both notations describe the same repeated group operation.

\begin{walkbox}
Quick check before moving on: in the additive group $\Zn{6}$, what is the inverse of $4$?
You need $x$ with $4 + x \equiv 0 \pmod 6$, so $x = 2$. In the multiplicative group
$\Zns{7}$, what is the inverse of $4$? You need $4x \equiv 1 \pmod 7$; testing gives
$4 \cdot 2 = 8 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$, so the inverse is $2$. Same word ``inverse,'' two different
operations.
\end{walkbox}

\section{Order of an element, and cyclic groups}
\label{ch05:order}

\subsection{Order of an element}

Pick a group element $g$ and start applying the operation to itself: $g, g^2, g^3,
\dots$ (in multiplicative notation). Because the group is finite, this list cannot go on
forever without repeating. The first time it lands back on the identity is the element's
\emph{order}.

\begin{definition}[Order of an element]
\label{ch05:def-order}
The \emph{order} of $g \in G$, written $\ord(g)$, is the smallest positive integer $k$
such that $g^k = e$. (In additive notation: the smallest $k>0$ with $k\cdot g = 0$.)
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Orders in $\Zns{7}$]
\label{ch05:ex-orders-7}
Here $7$ is prime, so $\Zns{7} = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$ has $\abs{\Zns{7}} = 6$ elements. Trace the powers $g^k \bmod 7$ for $g = 2, 3, 6$:
\[
\begin{array}{l|cccccc}
g & g^1 & g^2 & g^3 & g^4 & g^5 & g^6 \\\hline
2 & 2 & 4 & 1 & 2 & 4 & 1 \\
3 & 3 & 2 & 6 & 4 & 5 & 1 \\
6 & 6 & 1 & 6 & 1 & 6 & 1
\end{array}
\]
Reading off the first column where $1$ appears gives $\ord(2)=3$, $\ord(3)=6$, and $\ord(6)=2$; also
$\ord(1)=1$ since $1^1 = 1$. Every order in the list ($1, 2, 3,$ and $6$) divides $6$, the size
of the group. Section~\ref{ch05:lagrange} proves this always happens.
\end{example}

\begin{intuitionbox}
The powers of $g$ form a \emph{cycle}: a closed loop that returns to the identity and then
repeats. The order is the length of that loop. A short loop means $g$ reaches only a few elements
by exponentiation; a long loop means $g$ sweeps through many elements before returning to $e$.
Cryptographic protocols usually want long loops so that $g^x$ ranges over a large set and hides the secret $x$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Cyclic groups and generators}

When a single element's loop covers the \emph{entire} group, that element is a generator
and the group is cyclic.

\begin{definition}[Cyclic group, generator]
\label{ch05:def-cyclic}
A group $G$ is \emph{cyclic} if there exists an element $g \in G$, called a \emph{generator}, such that
every element of $G$ equals $g^k$ for some integer $k$. For a finite group $G$, this holds if and only if $\ord(g) = \abs{G}$.
We write $G = \langle g \rangle$, read ``the group generated by $g$.''
\end{definition}

In Example~\ref{ch05:ex-orders-7}, the element $3$ has order $6 = \abs{\Zns{7}}$, so its
powers $3,2,6,4,5,1$ run through all six elements. Thus $\Zns{7}$ is cyclic with generator
$3$, as Figure~\ref{ch05:fig-cycle} shows. The element $2$, by contrast, has order $3$ and
generates only the smaller set $\{1,2,4\}$, which forms a group of its own inside $\Zns{7}$ (a \emph{subgroup}, defined in Section~\ref{ch05:lagrange}).

\begin{example}[$\Zn{6}$ is cyclic too]
\label{ch05:ex-z6-cyclic}
In additive notation, ``powers of $g$'' means integer multiples mod $6$. Take $g=1$ in $\Zn{6}$:
$1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 0$ covers all six residues, so $1$ is a generator. Take $g=5$: $5, 4, 3, 2, 1, 0$ also covers all
six, so $5$ generates too. By contrast, $g=2$ gives $2, 4, 0, 2, 4, 0, \dots$, reaching only $\{0,2,4\}$: order
$3$, not a generator. The generators of $\Zn{6}$ are $1$ and $5$, the residues
coprime to $6$. (In general, $k$ generates the additive group $\Zn{n}$ if and only if $\gcd(k,n)=1$; see Exercise~\ref{ch05:ex-additive-gen}.)
\end{example}

\begin{whybox}
Why cyclic groups suit cryptography. In a cyclic group $\langle g \rangle$ of size
$q$, every element equals $g^x$ for a unique exponent $x \in \{0,1,\dots,q-1\}$. The map $x \mapsto g^x$
is therefore a bijection (Chapter~4) between $\{0,1,\dots,q-1\}$ and the group. Going \emph{forward}
($x \mapsto g^x$) takes $O(\log q)$ multiplications via square-and-multiply. Going
\emph{backward} ($g^x \mapsto x$, the discrete logarithm) has no known efficient
algorithm in well-chosen groups. That one-way bijection, combined with the exponent rule $(g^a)^b = g^{ab}$, lets two
parties combine their secrets in Diffie--Hellman without revealing them.
\end{whybox}

\begin{historybox}
The word \emph{group} comes from \'Evariste Galois, who in 1832 (the night before a fatal duel at age twenty) studied permutations of polynomial roots to determine which equations can be solved by \emph{radicals}, meaning formulas built from arithmetic and $n$-th roots ($\sqrt{\phantom{x}}, \sqrt[3]{\phantom{x}}, \dots$). Rejected by the French Academy during his lifetime, his manuscripts were published by Joseph Liouville in 1846 and founded abstract algebra. In 1976, Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman's paper ``New Directions in Cryptography'' built public-key key exchange on cyclic groups, working in $\Zns{p}$ for a large prime $p$. Later researchers found faster discrete-log attacks in $\Zns{p}$ called \emph{index-calculus} methods, which exploit how integers factor into small primes. That pushed modern protocols toward elliptic-curve groups, where elements are curve points rather than integers, index calculus does not apply, and the same security requires much smaller keys. The group axioms and protocol equations stayed identical; only the underlying group changed.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Not every group is cyclic}

$\Zns{n}$ is cyclic for some moduli $n$ and not for others. As noted in Chapter~4, $\Zns{n}$ is cyclic if and only if
$n \in \{1, 2, 4, p^k, 2p^k\}$ for an odd prime $p$ and $k \ge 1$. The smallest non-cyclic example is
$\Zns{8} = \{1,3,5,7\}$.

\begin{example}[$\Zns{8}$ has no generator]
\label{ch05:ex-zns8}
Square every element modulo $8$:
\[
  1^2 = 1, \qquad
  3^2 = 9 \equiv 1, \qquad
  5^2 = 25 \equiv 1, \qquad
  7^2 = 49 \equiv 1 \pmod{8}.
\]
Every element squares to $1$, so every non-identity element has order $2$. No
element has order $4 = \abs{\Zns{8}}$, so no generator exists (Figure~\ref{fig:ch05-cyclic-vs-klein}). Algebraists call this four-element pattern the \emph{Klein four-group}: it behaves like a pair of independent light switches $(b_1, b_2) \in \{0,1\}^2$ combined with XOR, where flipping any combination twice returns to $(0,0)$.
\end{example}

This distinction matters in protocols. If a scheme assumes $g$ generates a large group and you hand it a
non-cyclic group (or an element of tiny order), $g^x$ covers far fewer values than expected and the security guarantee breaks. Choosing the modulus and generator so that you work inside a large prime-order cyclic subgroup is a standard requirement, made precise using Lagrange's theorem next.

\section{Subgroups and Lagrange's theorem}
\label{ch05:lagrange}

\subsection{Subgroups}

A \emph{subgroup} is a subset that is itself a group under the same operation.

\begin{definition}[Subgroup]
\label{ch05:def-subgroup}
A nonempty subset $H \subseteq G$ is a \emph{subgroup} (written $H \le G$) if it is closed
under the operation and under inverses: for all $a,b \in H$, both $a \ast b \in H$ and
$a^{-1} \in H$. (Because $H$ contains at least one element $a$, it also contains $a^{-1}$ and therefore $a \ast a^{-1} = e$, while associativity is inherited from $G$.)
\end{definition}

Every element $g \in G$ produces a subgroup automatically. The set of its powers,
\[
\langle g \rangle = \{e, g, g^2, \dots, g^{\ord(g)-1}\},
\]
is the \emph{cyclic subgroup generated by $g$}, and its size is $\abs{\langle g \rangle} = \ord(g)$. In $\Zns{7}$, for instance, $\langle 2 \rangle = \{1,2,4\}$ has
$3 = \ord(2)$ elements.

\subsection{Lagrange's theorem}

Lagrange's theorem relates the size of any subgroup to the size of the whole group.

\begin{theorem}[Lagrange]
\label{ch05:thm-lagrange}
If $G$ is a finite group and $H \le G$ is a subgroup, then $\abs{H}$ divides $\abs{G}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
Why Lagrange's theorem holds (the coset argument; see Figure~\ref{ch05:fig-cosets}). For a fixed $a \in G$, define the
\emph{left coset} $aH = \{a \ast h : h \in H\}$, obtained by combining $a$ on the left with every element of $H$. Two facts do the work.

\emph{Every coset has the same size as $H$.} The map $h \mapsto a \ast h$ from $H$ to
$aH$ is reversible (combine on the left with $a^{-1}$ to undo it), so it is a bijection and
$\abs{aH} = \abs{H}$.

\emph{Distinct cosets do not overlap.} Suppose $aH$ and $bH$ share an element. Then for some $h_1, h_2 \in H$,
\[
  a \ast h_1 = b \ast h_2
  \implies b^{-1} \ast a = h_2 \ast h_1^{-1} \in H.
\]
Writing $h_0 = h_2 \ast h_1^{-1} \in H$ gives $a = b \ast h_0$. As $h$ ranges over $H$, the products $h_0 \ast h$ range over all of $H$ (since $H$ is closed under $\ast$ and $h_0^{-1} \in H$), so $aH = (b \ast h_0)H = bH$. Thus any
two cosets are either identical or \emph{disjoint} (share no elements).

Every element $a \in G$ lies in the coset $aH$ (because $e \in H$ gives
$a = a\ast e \in aH$), so the distinct cosets partition $G$ into non-overlapping blocks of equal size $\abs{H}$. If there are $m$ blocks, then
$\abs{G} = m\cdot\abs{H}$, so $\abs{H}$ divides $\abs{G}$. The count of blocks $m = \abs{G}/\abs{H}$ is
called the \emph{index} of $H$ in $G$, written $[G:H]$.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{The consequences cryptography uses}

Lagrange's theorem is a counting statement; its corollaries are what protocols apply directly.

\begin{corollary}[Order divides group size]
\label{ch05:cor-order-divides}
For any $g$ in a finite group $G$, $\ord(g)$ divides $\abs{G}$.
\end{corollary}
Since $\langle g\rangle \le G$ is a subgroup of size $\ord(g)$, Theorem~\ref{ch05:thm-lagrange} gives $\ord(g) \mid \abs{G}$ at once. That explains Example~\ref{ch05:ex-orders-7}: in a group of
$6$ elements, the only possible element orders are the divisors $1, 2, 3,$ and $6$---never $4$ or $5$.

\begin{corollary}[Identity at the group size]
\label{ch05:cor-glag}
For any $g$ in a finite group $G$ of order $n=\abs{G}$, we have $g^{n} = e$.
\end{corollary}
Write $k = \ord(g)$. By Corollary~\ref{ch05:cor-order-divides}, $n = k m$ for some integer $m$, so
$g^{n} = (g^{k})^{m} = e^{m} = e$. Raising any element to the size of the group returns the identity.

Specializing $G$ to the unit group $\Zns{n}$, whose size is
$\varphi(n)$, recovers Euler's theorem; specializing
further to a prime modulus $p$ recovers Fermat's little theorem.

\begin{corollary}[Euler and Fermat from Lagrange]
\label{ch05:cor-euler-fermat}
If $\gcd(a,n)=1$ then $a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$ \textup{(Euler)}. If $p$ is prime
and $p \nmid a$ then $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$ \textup{(Fermat)}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Chapter~4 proved Euler's and Fermat's theorems by multiplying all units together and canceling. Here both follow from a single structural fact---element order divides group size---with no product manipulation. A result that first looked specific to modular arithmetic holds in \emph{every} finite group, elliptic curves included.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{example}[Euler in action]
\label{ch05:ex-euler}
Take $n=15$, where $\varphi(15)=8$ (Example~\ref{ch05:ex-zns15}). Euler's theorem guarantees
$a^{8}\equiv 1 \pmod{15}$ for every $a$ coprime to $15$. Check $a=2$: $2^4 = 16 \equiv 1 \pmod{15}$, so
$2^8 = (2^4)^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{15}$. Here $\ord(2)=4$, which divides $8$. You
do not need the full exponent $8$ for $a=2$, since $4$ already returns to $1$, but $8$
works for \emph{every} element of $\Zns{15}$ at once.
\end{example}

\begin{connectionbox}
Square-and-multiply (Chapter~3, and analyzed in the companion algorithms book) computes $g^x \bmod n$ in $O(\log x)$ modular multiplications, so a $2048$-bit exponent takes only a few thousand multiplications. Lagrange's theorem provides an independent shortcut: you may reduce the exponent modulo $\ord(g)$ (or modulo $\varphi(n)$) before exponentiating. Writing $x = q\,\ord(g) + r$ with $r = x \bmod \ord(g)$ gives
\[
  g^x = \bigl(g^{\ord(g)}\bigr)^q \cdot g^r = e^q \cdot g^r = g^{x \bmod \ord(g)}.
\]
Together, fast exponentiation and exponent reduction mod $\varphi(n)$ keep RSA decryption exponents bounded below $\varphi(n)$.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{From one operation to two: rings}
\label{ch05:rings}

A group gives you one operation. Ordinary arithmetic has two (addition and
multiplication), linked by distributivity. A \emph{ring} is the structure with two compatible operations.

\begin{definition}[Commutative ring with identity]
\label{ch05:def-ring}
A \emph{ring} is a set $R$ with two binary operations $+$ and $\cdot$ such that:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $(R,+)$ is an abelian group, with additive identity $0$ and additive inverses $-a$;
  \item multiplication $\cdot$ is associative, is commutative ($a\cdot b = b\cdot a$), and has an identity element $1$;
  \item multiplication distributes over addition: $a\cdot(b+c) = a\cdot b + a\cdot c$ for all $a,b,c \in R$.
\end{itemize}
More precisely, these axioms define a \emph{commutative ring with identity}; because every ring in this book is commutative and has a $1$, we say ``ring'' for short.
\end{definition}

The main example is $\Zn{n}$ \emph{with both operations}: add mod $n$ and multiply mod
$n$. Every $\Zn{n}$ is a ring. So is the set of integers $\Z$, and so is the set of polynomials (expressions such as $x^3 + x + 1$) whose coefficients come from a ring. Chapter~6 uses that polynomial ring to build $\F_{2^8}$, the $256$-element byte field inside AES, from polynomials with coefficients mod $2$.

\begin{focusbox}
In a ring, multiplication is \emph{not} required to
have inverses. In $\Zn{n}$ you can always add, subtract, and multiply, but you cannot
always divide. The elements you \emph{can} divide by are the \emph{units}: those
with a multiplicative inverse, namely the residues coprime to $n$. In any ring $R$, the set of units forms a group under multiplication; for $R = \Zn{n}$ that unit group is
$\Zns{n}$ from Section~\ref{ch05:groups}.
\end{focusbox}

\subsection{Zero divisors: the wrinkle that composite moduli introduce}

In ordinary integers, $a\cdot b = 0$ forces $a=0$ or $b=0$. In a general ring that rule can
fail, which is why composite moduli require extra care.

\begin{definition}[Zero divisor]
\label{ch05:def-zerodiv}
A nonzero element $a \in R$ is a \emph{zero divisor} if there is a nonzero element $b \in R$ with $a\cdot b = 0$.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Zero divisors mod $12$]
\label{ch05:ex-zerodiv-12}
In $\Zn{12}$, we have $3\cdot 4 = 12 \equiv 0 \pmod{12}$, even though neither $3$ nor $4$ is $0$; similarly $2\cdot 6 \equiv 0$ and $8\cdot 3 \equiv 0 \pmod{12}$. The full set of zero divisors in $\Zn{12}$ is $\{2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 10\}$---all nonzero residues sharing a factor with $12$.

In general, for any nonzero residue $a \in \{1,\dots,n-1\}$ with $d = \gcd(a,n) > 1$, setting $b = n/d$ gives $0 < b < n$ (so $b \not\equiv 0 \pmod n$) and $a\cdot b = (a/d)n \equiv 0 \pmod n$, making $a$ a zero divisor. Conversely, a zero divisor can never be a unit: if $a\cdot b = 0$ with $b \ne 0$ and $a$ had an inverse $a^{-1}$, then
\[
\begin{aligned}
  b &= 1 \cdot b = (a^{-1}\cdot a)\cdot b \\
    &= a^{-1}\cdot(a\cdot b) &&\text{(associativity)} \\
    &= a^{-1}\cdot 0 = 0, &&\text{(since $a\cdot b = 0$, and $x\cdot 0 = 0$ in any ring)}
\end{aligned}
\]
contradicting $b \ne 0$. Thus every nonzero residue in $\Zn{n}$ is \emph{either} a unit ($\gcd(a,n)=1$) \emph{or} a zero divisor ($\gcd(a,n)>1$), as Figure~\ref{fig:ch05-ring-anatomy} illustrates.
\end{example}

$a$ is a unit. Skipping that check is a recurring implementation bug, and avoiding zero divisors altogether is a major reason protocols favor prime moduli.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Fields: rings with division}
\label{ch05:fields}

A field is a ring in which you can divide by every nonzero element, so addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division all follow the rules of ordinary school algebra.

\begin{definition}[Field]
\label{ch05:def-field}
A \emph{field} is a commutative ring $F$ with $1 \ne 0$ (ruling out the one-element ring $\{0\}$) in which \emph{every} nonzero element
has a multiplicative inverse. Equivalently, $(F,+)$ is an abelian group and
$(F^{*},\cdot)$ is also an abelian group, where $F^{*} = F\setminus\{0\}$ denotes $F$ with $0$ removed.
\end{definition}

Familiar infinite fields include the rational numbers $\Q$ (fractions $a/b$ with $a,b \in \Z$ and $b \ne 0$), the real numbers $\R$, and the complex
numbers $\C$. Cryptography runs on \emph{finite} fields, starting with the prime fields.

\begin{theorem}[Prime fields]
\label{ch05:thm-prime-field}
For an integer $n \ge 2$, the ring $\Zn{n}$ is a field if and only if $n$ is prime. When $n = p$ is prime, we write $\F_p$ for $\Zn{p}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
Why primality is the exact dividing line. If $n = p$ is prime, every residue
$a \in \{1,\dots,p-1\}$ satisfies $\gcd(a,p)=1$, so every nonzero element is a unit with a multiplicative
inverse, satisfying Definition~\ref{ch05:def-field}. If $n$ is composite, write $n = ab$ with
$1 < a,b < n$; then $a$ and $b$ are zero divisors ($ab = n \equiv 0 \pmod n$), and Example~\ref{ch05:ex-zerodiv-12} showed that a zero divisor cannot have an inverse, so $\Zn{n}$ is not a field.
\end{whybox}

\begin{example}[$\F_5$ in full]
\label{ch05:ex-f5}
Take $\F_5 = \{0,1,2,3,4\}$. Figure~\ref{ch05:fig-f5} shows the multiplication table on its nonzero elements $\F_5^{*} = \{1,2,3,4\}$. Read off each inverse by locating the single $1$ in its row: $1^{-1}=1$, $2^{-1}=3$, $3^{-1}=2$, and
$4^{-1}=4$. Every nonzero element has an inverse, as a field requires. Moreover, tracing powers of $2$ mod $5$
gives $2^1=2$, $2^2=4$, $2^3=8\equiv 3$, $2^4=16\equiv 1 \pmod 5$, so $\ord(2)=4$ and $2$ generates $\F_5^{*}$.
\end{example}

\end{focusbox}

\section{A worked tour: the structures inside $\Zn{n}$}

Even though $9 = 3^2$ is a prime power, the modular ring $\Zn{9}$ is \emph{not} a field: $3\cdot 3 = 9 \equiv
0 \pmod{9}$ makes $3$ (and $6$) zero divisors.

Its \textbf{additive group} $(\Zn{9},+)$ has $9$ elements and is cyclic, generated by $1$
(or by any residue coprime to $9$, namely $1,2,4,5,7,8$).

Its \textbf{group of units} is $\Zns{9} = \{1,2,4,5,7,8\}$, the residues coprime to $9$, of size
$\varphi(9)=6$. To test whether $\Zns{9}$ is cyclic, compute successive powers of $2$ modulo $9$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  2^1 &= 2, & 2^2 &= 4, & 2^3 &= 8, \\
  2^4 &= 16 \equiv 7, & 2^5 &\equiv 7 \cdot 2 = 14 \equiv 5, & 2^6 &\equiv 5 \cdot 2 = 10 \equiv 1 \pmod{9}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Those powers visit $2,4,8,7,5,1$---all six units---so $\ord(2)=6$ and $2$ generates $\Zns{9}$.
(This agrees with the criterion in Section~\ref{ch05:order}: $9 = 3^2$ is an odd prime power,
so $\Zns{9}$ is cyclic, unlike $\Zns{8}$ in Example~\ref{ch05:ex-zns8}.)

Verify Lagrange's theorem inside $\Zns{9}$. Because $\abs{\Zns{9}} = 6$, element orders must divide $6$ and so lie in $\{1,2,3,6\}$. Check $\ord(8)$: $8 \equiv -1 \pmod{9}$, and $(-1)^2 = 1$, so $\ord(8)=2$.
Check $\ord(4)$: $4^1 = 4$, $4^2 = 16 \equiv 7 \pmod{9}$, and $4^3 \equiv 7\cdot 4 = 28 \equiv 1 \pmod{9}$, so $\ord(4)=3$. Both $2$ and $3$ divide $6$. Moreover, the subgroup $H = \langle 4\rangle = \{1,4,7\}$ and its coset $2H = \{2,8,5\}$ partition $\Zns{9}$ into two disjoint blocks of size $3$, matching Theorem~\ref{ch05:thm-lagrange}.
\end{walkbox}

\section{Toy code: groups by hand}
\label{ch05:code}

The fastest way to make these definitions concrete is to compute orders and find
generators in code. The snippet below works in any $\Zns{n}$.

\begin{implementbox}
\textbf{Learning only---never use this in real cryptography.} It is variable-time (its runtime depends on the inputs, which can leak secrets)
and uses tiny numbers for clarity. Production systems use vetted libraries with constant-time arithmetic and
large, validated parameters.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
from math import gcd

def units(n):
    "The group (Z/nZ)* : residues coprime to n."
    return [a for a in range(1, n) if gcd(a, n) == 1]

def order(a, n):
    "Smallest k > 0 with a^k = 1 mod n. Assumes gcd(a, n) = 1."
    k, x = 1, a % n
    while x != 1:
        x = (x * a) % n
        k += 1
    return k

def generators(n):
    "Elements whose order equals |(Z/nZ)*| = phi(n); empty if non-cyclic."
    U = units(n)
    phi = len(U)
    return [a for a in U if order(a, n) == phi]

# Check Lagrange: every order divides phi(n).
n = 9
U = units(n)                       # [1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 8]
phi = len(U)                       # 6
orders = {a: order(a, n) for a in U}
assert all(phi % orders[a] == 0 for a in U)   # Lagrange holds
print("phi =", phi)
print("orders =", orders)          # {1:1, 2:6, 4:3, 5:6, 7:3, 8:2}
print("generators =", generators(n))          # [2, 5]

# A non-cyclic group has no generator at all:
print("generators of 8:", generators(8))      # []  (Z/8Z)* is not cyclic
\end{lstlisting}
The assertion turns Lagrange's theorem into a runtime check: for any $n \ge 2$,
every unit's order divides $\varphi(n)$. The empty generator list for $n=8$
reproduces Example~\ref{ch05:ex-zns8}.
\end{implementbox}

\section{A pitfall: small subgroups and where Lagrange bites back}
\label{ch05:pitfall}

Lagrange's theorem constrains every subgroup, and an active attacker can turn that constraint against an implementation that skips input checks. The hazard is the \emph{small subgroup}.

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Small-subgroup confinement.} Diffie--Hellman in $\Zns{p}$ is meant to run inside a
large prime-order subgroup. For any odd prime $p$, however, $\Zns{p}$ has even size $p-1$ and always contains the order-$2$ subgroup $\{1, p-1\}$, since
$(p-1)^2 \equiv (-1)^2 = 1 \pmod p$. More generally, every divisor of $p-1$ corresponds to a subgroup of $\Zns{p}$ of that size.

Suppose Alice computes a shared secret as $K = Y^a \bmod p$ from a value $Y$ received over the network. If Alice does not validate $Y$, an impostor (Mallory or an active Eve) can send $Y = p - 1$ of
order $2$. Then $K = Y^a \bmod p$ takes only two possible
values ($1$ or $p-1$) regardless of Alice's secret $a$, revealing whether $a$ is even or odd---its \emph{parity}
(Figure~\ref{ch05:fig-smallsub}).
If $p-1$ has other small prime factors $3, 5, \dots$, repeating the trick with elements of those orders leaks $a$ modulo each small
factor, after which the Chinese Remainder Theorem (Chapter~4) reconstructs $a$.

\textbf{The fix follows from Lagrange.} Choose $p = 2q+1$ where $q$ is also prime (such a $p$ is called a \emph{safe
prime}). Then $\abs{\Zns{p}} = p - 1 = 2q$, whose only divisors---and by Lagrange, only possible subgroup sizes---are $1, 2, q,$ and $2q$. Work inside the order-$q$ subgroup, and \emph{validate} every received value $Y$ by checking $1 < Y < p-1$ and $Y^q \equiv 1 \pmod p$: since $q$ is prime, $Y^q \equiv 1 \pmod p$ forces $\ord(Y) \in \{1, q\}$, and $Y \ne 1$ forces $\ord(Y) = q$.

Elliptic-curve protocols apply the same idea. When a curve group $G$ has size $h \cdot q$ for a large prime $q$ and a small integer $h$, the ratio $h = \abs{G}/q$ (the subgroup index $[G:H]$) is called the \emph{cofactor}. Curves are chosen so $h$ is tiny ($1$, $4$, or $8$), and implementations validate received points or clear the cofactor before deriving keys.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
Safe-prime subgroups are standardized for Transport Layer Security (TLS) and Internet Protocol Security (IPsec). RFC~7919 defines
fixed, audited safe-prime groups (\texttt{ffdhe2048}, \texttt{ffdhe3072}, \dots, standing for finite-field Diffie--Hellman ephemeral with a $2048$- or $3072$-bit modulus) so
implementers do not generate weak moduli with small subgroup factors.
TLS~1.3 favors elliptic-curve groups such as Curve25519 (an elliptic-curve Diffie--Hellman group over the prime field $\F_{2^{255}-19}$), designed by
Daniel Bernstein with cofactor $h = 8$ and scalar clamping so small-subgroup inputs cannot leak secret key bits. Across all of these standards, the rule is the same: \emph{know the order of your group, and ensure every accepted input acts inside the large prime-order subgroup.}
\end{applicationbox}

\section{How the three structures power the rest of the book}
\label{ch05:roadmap}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch05-hierarchy} summarizes how groups, rings, and fields nest by adding operations and inverses, and where each one appears in later chapters.

\textbf{Groups} provide the discrete-logarithm setting: a cyclic group $\langle g \rangle$ where $x \mapsto g^x$ is fast to compute and hard to invert. That structure underlies Diffie--Hellman key exchange
(Chapter~18), ElGamal encryption and the Digital Signature Algorithm (DSA) in Chapter~20, and elliptic-curve cryptography
(Chapter~21), where the group elements are points on a curve instead of modular residues.

\textbf{Rings} provide $\Zn{n}$ with both addition and multiplication, the setting for RSA
(Chapter~19): encryption computes $m \mapsto m^e \bmod n$ for a \emph{composite} modulus $n=pq$, and
decryption relies on Euler's theorem (Corollary~\ref{ch05:cor-euler-fermat}) inside $\Zn{n}$. Here the composite modulus and its zero divisors are required because security rests on the hardness of factoring $n$.

\textbf{Fields} provide arithmetic where every nonzero element is invertible and no zero divisors exist. Prime fields
$\F_p$ supply the coordinates for elliptic curves. Binary extension fields
$\F_{2^k}$ supply the byte-level mixing in AES ($\F_{2^8}$) and the authentication polynomial in
Galois/Counter Mode (GCM, $\F_{2^{128}}$), where polynomial coefficients are single bits added modulo $2$ (XOR, with no carries between bit positions). Chapter~6 constructs $\F_{2^k}$ by reducing polynomials modulo an \emph{irreducible} polynomial---one that cannot be factored into lower-degree polynomials---mirroring how reducing the integers $\Z$ modulo a prime $p$ produces $\F_p$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Each structure matches a specific cryptographic job: reach for a \emph{cyclic group} when you need one reversible operation whose discrete logarithm is hard, a \emph{composite ring} $\Zn{pq}$ when hiding the prime factorization hides the unit group's size $\varphi(n)$, and a \emph{finite field} when you need exact division and linear algebra with zero collisions.
\end{intuitionbox}

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch05:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Group or not?]
\label{ch05:ex-group-or-not}
For each set and operation, decide whether it forms a group; if not, name an axiom that fails.
(a) The even integers under addition.
(b) The odd integers under addition.
(c) $\{1,2,3,4\}$ under multiplication mod $5$.
(d) $\{0,1,2,3\}$ under multiplication mod $4$.
(e) The set $\Zns{n}$ under multiplication mod $n$, for any integer $n \ge 2$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Orders by hand]
\label{ch05:ex-orders-hand}
Work entirely mod $11$. (a) Compute the order of every element of $\Zns{11}$. (b) List all
generators. (c) Confirm that each order you found divides $10$, and explain which theorem
guarantees this in advance.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Cyclic or not]
\label{ch05:ex-cyclic-or-not}
Determine whether $\Zns{n}$ is cyclic for $n = 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 16$. For each cyclic case
exhibit a generator; for each non-cyclic case, show that every element's order is strictly
less than $\varphi(n)$ and identify the largest order that occurs.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Subgroups and Lagrange]
\label{ch05:ex-subgroups}
In $\Zns{13}$ (which has $12$ elements): (a) Find the cyclic subgroup generated by $3$ and
state its size. (b) Find a subgroup of size $4$ and one of size $6$. (c) Explain why no
subgroup of size $5$ can exist, citing Lagrange's theorem.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Euler from Lagrange]
\label{ch05:ex-euler-proof}
Using only Corollary~\ref{ch05:cor-glag} ($g^{\abs{G}} = e$), prove Euler's theorem:
if $\gcd(a,n)=1$ then $a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$. Then specialize to deduce Fermat's
little theorem. State precisely which group you are applying the corollary to and why $a$
belongs to it.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Zero divisors and cancellation]
\label{ch05:ex-zerodiv}
(a) List all zero divisors in $\Zn{18}$. (b) Show $\gcd(a,18) > 1$ characterizes
them among nonzero residues. (c) Exhibit a concrete failure of cancellation: find $a \ne 0$ and $x \ne y$ in
$\Zn{18}$ with $a x \equiv a y \pmod{18}$. (d) Explain why the same cannot happen in $\F_{19}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Fields and non-fields]
\label{ch05:ex-fields}
(a) Prove that for $n \ge 2$, $\Zn{n}$ is a field if and only if $n$ is prime, filling in every step of
Theorem~\ref{ch05:thm-prime-field}. (b) Build the full multiplication table of $\F_7$ and
read off the inverse of each nonzero element. (c) Explain in one sentence why no field of
order $6$ exists.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Break a careless Diffie--Hellman]
\label{ch05:ex-small-subgroup}
Let $p = 23$, so $p - 1 = 22 = 2 \cdot 11$. (a) Find an element of order $2$ and an
element of order $11$ in $\Zns{23}$. (b) Suppose Alice computes $K = Y^a \bmod 23$ without
validating $Y$, and Eve sends the order-$2$ element as $Y$. Show that $K$ reveals
$a \bmod 2$. (c) State the safe-prime fix and the validation check that defeats this
attack, connecting it to the small-subgroup discussion in
Section~\ref{ch05:pitfall}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Generators of the additive group]
\label{ch05:ex-additive-gen}
Prove that $k$ generates the additive group $\Zn{n}$ if and only if $\gcd(k,n)=1$. (Hint:
the multiples of $k$ are $\{0, k, 2k, \dots\} \bmod n$; relate the number of distinct
values to $\ord(k)$ and to $\gcd(k,n)$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Count the generators]
\label{ch05:ex-count-gen}
A cyclic group of order $n$ has exactly $\varphi(n)$ generators. (a) Verify this for
$\Zns{7}$ ($n=6$) by listing its generators and counting. (b) Verify for $\Zns{9}$
($n=6$). (c) Use the formula to predict, without enumeration, how many primitive roots
$\Zns{p}$ has when $p = 2q+1$ is a safe prime; evaluate it for $p=23$.
\end{exercise}
