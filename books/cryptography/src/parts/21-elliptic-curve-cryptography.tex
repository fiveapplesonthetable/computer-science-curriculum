\chapter{Elliptic Curve Cryptography}

By now you have met two families of public-key cryptography. RSA leans on the hardness
of factoring; Diffie--Hellman and ElGamal lean on the discrete logarithm problem in a
multiplicative group $\Zns{p}$. Both work and remain widely deployed, yet both require
large keys: a standard RSA modulus is $3072$ bits, and a comparable finite-field
Diffie--Hellman prime is the same. The reason is the same in both cases. The fastest
known classical attacks --- the general number field sieve for factoring and index
calculus for discrete logarithms --- run in \emph{sub-exponential} time: slower than any
polynomial, but far faster than brute-force search, forcing us to pad key lengths to stay
ahead of them.

Elliptic-curve cryptography (ECC) answers a direct question: is there a group where the
discrete logarithm problem is \emph{harder}, because index-calculus methods do not apply
and the only attacks left are \emph{generic} algorithms --- ones that use only the group
operation and treat group elements as opaque labels? In such a group, keys can be much
shorter for the same security. Elliptic curves provide that group. A $256$-bit
elliptic-curve key matches the security of a $3072$-bit RSA key, and that twelvefold
reduction is why phones, browsers, and messaging apps such as Signal run on curves today.

\section{From a multiplicative group to a curve}
\label{ch21:motivation}

Recall the shape of Diffie--Hellman. Fix a cyclic group $\G$ of prime order $q$ with a
generator $g$. Alice picks a secret integer $a$ and publishes $g^a$; Bob picks $b$ and
publishes $g^b$; the shared secret is $g^{ab} = (g^a)^b = (g^b)^a$. Security rests on the
discrete log problem: given $g$ and $g^a$, recover $a$.

Nothing in that description mentions $\Zns{p}$ specifically. Diffie--Hellman needs
\emph{any} finite cyclic group in which:

\begin{itemize}
  \item the group operation is cheap to compute,
  \item exponentiation (repeated operation) is cheap via square-and-multiply, but
  \item the discrete logarithm is hard.
\end{itemize}

The multiplicative group $\Zns{p}$ satisfies the first two. The trouble is the third:
$\Zns{p}$ has too much structure. Its elements are integers, and the index-calculus
attack exploits the fact that small integers factor into small primes, building linear
relations among discrete logs and solving the whole thing in sub-exponential time.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Index calculus exploits multiplicative relations among small integers. In $\Zns{p}$ the
elements can be lifted to ordinary integers $1,2,3,\dots$, where $6 = 2\cdot 3$ links
the discrete logarithm of $6$ to those of $2$ and $3$. Integers that factor completely
into small primes are called \emph{smooth}; collect enough relations among smooth integers
and you can solve a linear system for all the small logs, then recover any target
logarithm. Points on an elliptic curve have no analogous notion of ``factoring into
smaller points,'' so there is no smooth subset from which to build relations.
\end{intuitionbox}

So we need a group whose elements are not integers and admit no factoring story, yet
still have a clean, fast group law. Elliptic curves supply one: the elements are points
$(x,y)$ satisfying a cubic equation, and the group operation is geometric --- draw a
line, find where it meets the curve.

\begin{historybox}
Elliptic curves are nineteenth-century mathematics, originally studied through elliptic
integrals (which compute arc lengths of ellipses --- hence the name; the curves
themselves are cubic curves, not ellipses). Their cryptographic use came much later. In
$1985$ Neal Koblitz (University of Washington) and Victor Miller (IBM) independently
proposed using elliptic-curve groups for discrete-log cryptography. For two decades ECC
was treated as specialized and patent-encumbered while RSA dominated. Expiring patents,
mobile devices that could not spare the cycles for $3072$-bit RSA, and the $2013$
disclosures revealing a likely backdoor in the NIST-standardized random generator
\texttt{Dual\_EC\_DRBG} (Dual Elliptic Curve Deterministic Random Bit Generator) shifted
practice decisively. That incident eroded trust in unexplained parameter choices and drew
attention to Daniel J. Bernstein's Curve25519 ($2005$), whose constants follow from transparent
public criteria; the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF), which publishes internet
protocol standards, adopted it across core protocols, and it now secures most web traffic.
\end{historybox}

\section{The curve and its points}
\label{ch21:curve}

Over a field $K$ whose characteristic is not $2$ or $3$ (so $2 \neq 0$ and $3 \neq 0$ in
$K$, allowing division by $2$ and $3$), an elliptic curve in \emph{short Weierstrass form}
is the set of solutions $(x,y) \in K \times K$ to
\begin{equation}
\label{ch21:weierstrass}
y^2 = x^3 + ax + b, \qquad a,b \in K,
\end{equation}
together with one extra point ``at infinity,'' written $\mathcal{O}$, explained below. We
require the cubic $x^3 + ax + b$ on the right to have no repeated roots (no factor
$(x - r)^2$, even over an extension field), which is equivalent to the
\emph{nonsingularity} condition
\begin{equation}
\label{ch21:discriminant}
4a^3 + 27 b^2 \neq 0.
\end{equation}
Up to the factor $-16$, the expression $4a^3 + 27b^2$ is the \emph{discriminant} of the
cubic --- a polynomial in the coefficients that equals zero if and only if two roots
coincide. When $4a^3 + 27b^2 = 0$, the curve is \emph{singular}: it develops either a
\emph{cusp} (a sharp pinch where two branches meet with a shared tangent) or a
\emph{self-crossing} (a point where the curve crosses itself with two distinct tangents).
At a singular point, the group law breaks and the discrete logarithm problem becomes easy,
so curve parameters must always satisfy~\eqref{ch21:discriminant}.

every point an inverse.

\subsection{The point at infinity}

The extra point $\mathcal{O}$ is a genuine element of the group, included so that adding
any two points on the curve always produces a point in the set. Picture $\mathcal{O}$ as
sitting infinitely far up (and down) the $y$-axis, the single point where all vertical
lines meet: every vertical line passes through $\mathcal{O}$. It serves as the identity
element of the curve group --- the counterpart of $1$ in $\Zns{p}$ or of $0$ under
addition.

\begin{focusbox}
A clean way to make $\mathcal{O}$ rigorous is \emph{projective coordinates}. Write
points as triples $[X:Y:Z]$ with $(X,Y,Z)\ne(0,0,0)$, where $[X:Y:Z]$ and
$[\lambda X:\lambda Y:\lambda Z]$ represent the same point for any nonzero $\lambda \in K$.
An ordinary planar point $(x,y)$ --- called an \emph{affine} point to distinguish it from
points at infinity --- is written $[x:y:1]$, and substituting $x = X/Z$, $y = Y/Z$ turns
the curve into $Y^2 Z = X^3 + aXZ^2 + bZ^3$. Points at infinity have $Z=0$, which forces
$X^3=0$ and thus $X=0$, leaving the single projective point $[0:1:0]$ (since $[0:\lambda:0]
= [0:1:0]$). That single point is $\mathcal{O}$. You can work in affine coordinates $(x,y)$
and remember ``vertical lines hit $\mathcal{O}$''; projective coordinates show why no
hand-waving is involved.
\end{focusbox}

\section{The group law: geometry first}
\label{ch21:grouplaw-geom}

The following geometric rule turns the set of points into a group; Section~\ref{ch21:grouplaw-alg}
translates the same rule into coordinate algebra.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Chord-and-tangent addition.} To add two points $P$ and $Q$ on the curve:
draw the straight line through $P$ and $Q$. Because the curve has degree three, that line
meets it in three points when counted with multiplicity (counting a point of tangency
twice, and an inflection point where the tangent crosses the curve three times); call the
third intersection $R'$. Reflect $R'$ across the $x$-axis to get $R$, and define
$P + Q = R$.
\end{focusbox}

Figure~\ref{ch21:addition-fig} illustrates this rule for two distinct points $P$ and $Q$.

Three special cases complete the definition for an affine point $P = (x_P, y_P)$:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Adding $\mathcal{O}$.} For any point $P$, $P + \mathcal{O} = P$. The
        line through $P$ and $\mathcal{O}$ is the vertical line through $P$; its third
        intersection with the curve is the mirror point $(x_P,-y_P)$, and reflecting that
        point across the $x$-axis returns $P$. Thus $\mathcal{O}$ is the identity.
  \item \textbf{Inverses.} The reflection $-P = (x_P, -y_P)$ is the inverse of $P$. The
        line through $P$ and $-P$ is vertical, so its third intersection with the curve is
        $\mathcal{O}$, which reflects to itself: $P + (-P) = \mathcal{O}$.
  \item \textbf{Doubling.} To compute $2P = P + P$ when $P=Q$, replace the chord with the
        \emph{tangent} line to the curve at $P$ (the limiting position of the chord as
        $Q$ approaches $P$), as shown in Figure~\ref{ch21:doubling-fig}. Because tangency
        counts twice, the tangent meets the curve in one remaining point $R'$; reflecting
        $R'$ across the $x$-axis gives $2P$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{whybox}
Why does this geometric recipe satisfy all four group axioms? Closure, the identity
$\mathcal{O}$, and the inverse $-P$ come straight from the definition. Commutativity holds
because the line through $P$ and $Q$ is the same line as the line through $Q$ and $P$, so
$P+Q=Q+P$.

The one non-obvious axiom is \textbf{associativity}: $(P+Q)+S = P+(Q+S)$. Geometrically
the two sides draw different intermediate lines, yet they always land on the same final
point. A geometric proof rests on B\'ezout's theorem, which states that two cubic curves
intersect in nine points (counted with multiplicity), together with the fact that any
cubic passing through eight of those nine intersections must also pass through the ninth;
applying that rule to the two triples of construction lines forces $(P+Q)+S$ and $P+(Q+S)$
to match. Thus the points of the curve form an abelian group with identity $\mathcal{O}$,
and the same algebraic identities hold when we replace $\R$ by a finite field $\F_p$.
\end{whybox}

\section{The group law: algebra}
\label{ch21:grouplaw-alg}

Geometry gives us visual intuition; real cryptographic software needs explicit formulas.
We translate ``draw the line, find the third intersection, reflect'' into coordinate
algebra. The arithmetic works in any field $K$ whose characteristic is not $2$ or $3$ ---
over $\R$ for geometric intuition, and over finite field $\F_p$ for real cryptography.
The coordinate formulas are identical; only the arithmetic rules change.

Let $P=(x_1,y_1)$ and $Q=(x_2,y_2)$ be points with neither equal to $\mathcal{O}$.

\subsection*{Case 1: $P \neq \pm Q$ (a genuine chord)}

The line through $P$ and $Q$ has slope
\[
m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}.
\]
Substituting the line equation $y = m(x-x_1)+y_1$ into the curve $y^2 = x^3+ax+b$ and
moving everything to one side produces a cubic equation in $x$:
\begin{align*}
0 &= x^3 + ax + b - \bigl(m(x - x_1) + y_1\bigr)^2 \\
  &= x^3 - m^2 x^2 + (a + 2m^2 x_1 - 2m y_1)x + \bigl(b - (y_1 - m x_1)^2\bigr).
\end{align*}
The three roots of this cubic are the $x$-coordinates of the three intersection points:
$x_1$, $x_2$, and the unknown third intersection $x_3$. Because the cubic has leading
coefficient $1$, it factors over its roots as
\[
(x - x_1)(x - x_2)(x - x_3) = x^3 - (x_1 + x_2 + x_3)x^2 + \cdots.
\]
Matching the coefficients of $x^2$ (\emph{Vieta's formula} for the sum of the roots) gives
\[
x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = m^2, \qquad\text{so}\qquad x_3 = m^2 - x_1 - x_2.
\]
The $y$-coordinate of $R'$ on the chord is $y' = m(x_3 - x_1) + y_1$. Reflecting across
the horizontal axis to obtain $P + Q = (x_3, y_3)$ negates $y'$:
\begin{equation}
\label{ch21:add-x}
x_3 = m^2 - x_1 - x_2, \qquad y_3 = m(x_1 - x_3) - y_1.
\end{equation}

\subsection*{Case 2: $P = Q$ with $y_1 \neq 0$ (the tangent, ``doubling'')}

When $P = Q$, the chord becomes the tangent line at $(x_1, y_1)$. Over $\R$, implicit
differentiation of $y^2 = x^3 + ax + b$ gives $2y\,dy = (3x^2+a)\,dx$, so the tangent
slope is
\[
m = \frac{3x_1^2 + a}{2y_1}.
\]
Over a finite field $\F_p$ there are no limits, yet the exact same formula holds
algebraically: expanding $x^3 + ax + b - \bigl(m(x - x_1) + y_1\bigr)^2$ in powers of
$(x - x_1)$ shows that $(x - x_1)^2$ divides the cubic if and only if $2y_1 m = 3x_1^2 + a$.
Because tangency is a double root ($x_2 = x_1$), the root-sum equation becomes
$x_1 + x_1 + x_3 = m^2$, giving
\begin{equation}
\label{ch21:double-x}
x_3 = m^2 - 2x_1, \qquad y_3 = m(x_1 - x_3) - y_1.
\end{equation}

\subsection*{Case 3: $x_1 = x_2$ with $y_1 = -y_2$ (vertical line)}

If $x_1 = x_2$ and $y_1 = -y_2 \neq 0$, the two points are vertical reflections of each
other ($Q = -P$). The line between them is vertical and meets the curve at the point at
infinity, so $P + (-P) = \mathcal{O}$. Similarly, if $P = Q$ and $y_1 = 0$, the tangent
line is vertical ($2y_1 = 0$ in the denominator), giving $2P = \mathcal{O}$.

\begin{formalbox}
Summarizing for $P,Q \neq \mathcal{O}$ with $P \neq -Q$:
\[
m = \begin{cases}
\dfrac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1} & P \neq Q,\\[1.2em]
\dfrac{3x_1^2+a}{2y_1} & P = Q \text{ and } y_1 \neq 0,
\end{cases}
\qquad
\begin{aligned}
x_3 &= m^2 - x_1 - x_2,\\
y_3 &= m(x_1 - x_3) - y_1.
\end{aligned}
\]
When computing over $\F_p$, every addition, subtraction, multiplication, and squaring
is evaluated modulo $p$. Division by $D \not\equiv 0 \pmod p$ means multiplication by the
modular multiplicative inverse $D^{-1} \bmod p$, computed via the extended Euclidean
algorithm (or Fermat's little theorem, $D^{p-2} \bmod p$).
\end{formalbox}

\subsection{Projective coordinates: eliminating modular inversions}
\label{ch21:projective}

The affine formulas above carry a steep performance cost: every slope $m$ divides by
$x_2 - x_1$ or $2y_1$. Over a $256$-bit prime field $\F_p$, modular inversion is the
slowest field operation. A field multiplication takes only a few CPU cycles, whereas a
modular inverse via the extended Euclidean algorithm or $z^{p-2} \bmod p$ costs the
equivalent of roughly $80$ to $256$ field multiplications.

If scalar multiplication $kP$ (adding $P$ to itself $k$ times) ran directly in affine
coordinates, each bit of a $256$-bit scalar $k$ would require one or two inversions ---
around $384$ modular inversions per handshake, becoming the main bottleneck of key exchange.

\begin{intuitionbox}
\textbf{The projective insight.} Instead of dividing at every step, store the
accumulated denominator in an auxiliary coordinate $Z$, just as you add fractions by
tracking a common denominator and reducing once at the end. Hundreds of expensive
inversions turn into a few extra multiplications per step plus \emph{one} inversion when
converting the final answer back to $(x,y)$.
\end{intuitionbox}

Two projective coordinate systems are standard in cryptographic libraries:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Standard projective coordinates:} Represent the affine point $(x, y)$ as
        a triple $[X : Y : Z]$ with $Z \neq 0$, where $x = X/Z$ and $y = Y/Z$. Clearing
        denominators in~\eqref{ch21:weierstrass} gives the \emph{homogeneous} cubic (every
        term has total degree $3$ in $X, Y, Z$, so scaling all three coordinates by
        $\lambda$ multiplies both sides by $\lambda^3$):
        \[
        Y^2 Z = X^3 + a X Z^2 + b Z^3.
        \]
        The identity element $\mathcal{O}$ is $[0 : 1 : 0]$.
  \item \textbf{Jacobian projective coordinates:} Give $Z$ different exponents in $x$ and
        $y$, setting $x = X/Z^2$ and $y = Y/Z^3$. Substituting into~\eqref{ch21:weierstrass}
        and multiplying through by $Z^6$ gives
        \[
        Y^2 = X^3 + a X Z^4 + b Z^6.
        \]
        Here $\mathcal{O}$ is $[1 : 1 : 0]$ (with $[\lambda^2 : \lambda^3 : 0]$ equivalent
        for any $\lambda \neq 0$). Jacobian coordinates are favored on short Weierstrass
        curves because point doubling takes $4$ field multiplications and $4$ field
        squarings ($4M + 4S$), and adding two distinct points takes $12M + 4S$ --- with
        \textbf{zero} modular inversions.
\end{itemize}

\[

\]
Trading hundreds of inversions for one speeds up scalar multiplication by roughly an
order of magnitude.

\section{Curves over a finite field}
\label{ch21:finitefield}

Everything above used a field only through $+,-,\times,\div$. Swap $\R$ for the finite
field $\F_p$ (the integers mod a prime $p$) and the definitions carry over verbatim. The
curve
\[
E(\F_p) = \set{(x,y) \in \F_p \times \F_p : y^2 \equiv x^3 + ax + b \pmod p} \cup \set{\mathcal{O}}
\]
is now a \emph{finite} set of points, and the chord-and-tangent law makes it a finite
abelian group. There is no continuous curve anymore --- only a scatter of grid points
(Figure~\ref{ch21:scatter-fig}) --- yet the algebra is unchanged.

\begin{example}[A tiny curve by hand]
\label{ch21:tinycurve}
Take $p = 13$ and the curve
\[
E : y^2 \equiv x^3 + 3x + 8 \pmod{13}.
\]
Check nonsingularity: $4a^3 + 27b^2 = 4\cdot 27 + 27\cdot 64 = 108 + 1728 = 1836$, and
$1836 = 141\cdot 13 + 3$, so $4a^3 + 27b^2 \equiv 3 \not\equiv 0 \pmod{13}$, confirming
that $E$ is nonsingular.

To list the points, for each $x \in \{0,\dots,12\}$ compute the right-hand side
$f(x)=x^3+3x+8 \bmod 13$, then check whether it is a quadratic residue (a square) modulo
$13$. Because $(13 - y)^2 \equiv (-y)^2 = y^2 \pmod{13}$, squaring $1, \dots, 6$ gives all
nonzero squares modulo $13$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
1^2 &\equiv 1, & 2^2 &\equiv 4, & 3^2 &\equiv 9,\\
4^2 &\equiv 3, & 5^2 &\equiv 12, & 6^2 &\equiv 10 \pmod{13}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The nonzero squares modulo $13$ are $\{1,3,4,9,10,12\}$, and each has two square roots
$y$ and $13 - y$. Tabulating all $13$ values of $x$:

\begin{walkbox}
\textbf{Add two points by hand.} On the curve of Example~\ref{ch21:tinycurve}, take
$P=(1,8)$ and $Q=(2,3)$. First compute the chord slope modulo $13$:
\[
m = \frac{3-8}{2-1} = -5 \equiv 8 \pmod{13}.
\]
Then substitute into~\eqref{ch21:add-x}:
\begin{align*}
x_3 &= m^2 - x_1 - x_2 = 64 - 1 - 2 = 61 \equiv 9 \pmod{13}, \\
y_3 &= m(x_1 - x_3) - y_1 = 8(1 - 9) - 8 = -72 \equiv 6 \pmod{13} \quad (\text{since }-72 + 6\cdot 13 = 6).
\end{align*}
So $P+Q = (9,6)$. Check that $(9,6)$ lies on $E$: $x^3+3x+8 = 729 + 27 + 8 = 764 =
58\cdot 13 + 10$, so $x^3 + 3x + 8 \equiv 10 \pmod{13}$, matching $y^2 = 36 \equiv 10
\pmod{13}$.
\end{walkbox}

\begin{walkbox}
\textbf{Double a point by hand.} Take $P=(1,8)$ and compute $2P$. Since $2y_1 = 16 \equiv 3
\pmod{13}$ and $3 \cdot 9 = 27 \equiv 1 \pmod{13}$, we have $(2y_1)^{-1} \equiv 9 \pmod{13}$,
giving the tangent slope
\begin{align*}
m &\equiv (3x_1^2 + a)\,(2y_1)^{-1} \\
  &\equiv (3\cdot 1^2 + 3)\cdot 9 = 54 \equiv 2 \pmod{13}.
\end{align*}
Substituting into~\eqref{ch21:double-x} gives
\begin{align*}
x_3 &= m^2 - 2x_1 = 4 - 2 = 2, \\
y_3 &= m(x_1 - x_3) - y_1 = 2(1 - 2) - 8 = -10 \equiv 3 \pmod{13}.
\end{align*}
Thus $2P = (2,3) = Q$. Because $Q = 2P$, we have $P + Q = P + 2P = 3P$, so the sum $(9,6)$
from the previous box is $3P$.
\end{walkbox}

\begin{labbox}
Keep going from $P=(1,8)$. You found $2P=(2,3)$ and $3P=(9,6)$. Compute $4P$ by adding
$P$ to $3P$, then $5P,6P,\dots$ until you return to $\mathcal{O}$. You will find that
$P$ generates a cyclic subgroup whose order divides $9$. Here $P$ has order $9$, so
$\{\mathcal{O},P,2P,\dots,8P\}$ is the entire group --- $P$ is a generator. Tracing the
full cycle by hand once makes the cyclic structure concrete.
\end{labbox}

\subsection{How many points? Hasse's theorem}
\label{ch21:hasse}

The group $E(\F_p)$ had $9$ points when $p=13$. Is that typical? A quick heuristic predicts
about $p + 1$ points: as $x$ ranges over the $p$ elements of $\F_p$, the value $f(x)$
behaves roughly like a random element of $\F_p$, which is a nonzero square about half the
time (giving two $y$'s) and a non-square about half the time (giving none), for an average
of $1$ affine point per $x$ plus $\mathcal{O}$. Hasse's theorem proves that the true count
never strays far from $p + 1$.

\begin{theorem}[Hasse, 1933]
\label{ch21:hasse-thm}
For any elliptic curve $E$ over $\F_p$,
\[
\#E(\F_p) = p + 1 - t, \qquad \abs{t} \le 2\sqrt{p}.
\]
The integer $t$ is called the \emph{trace of Frobenius} (arising from the Frobenius map
$(x,y) \mapsto (x^p, y^p)$ of Chapter~6, whose fixed points are the points with
coordinates in $\F_p$).
\end{theorem}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch21-hasse-window} illustrates Hasse's interval
$[\,p+1-2\sqrt p,\; p+1+2\sqrt p\,]$, which has width $4\sqrt p$ centered at $p+1$. For
$p=13$, the interval is $[14 - 7.21,\; 14 + 7.21] = [6.79,\; 21.21]$, allowing integer
sizes $7$ through $21$; our curve from Example~\ref{ch21:tinycurve} sits at $\#E(\F_{13})
= 9$ ($t = 5$). For a $256$-bit prime $p \approx 2^{256}$, the half-width $2\sqrt{p}
\approx 2^{129}$ is tiny next to $p+1$, so $\#E(\F_p)$ agrees with $p$ in its top $127$
bits --- a relative deviation below $2^{-127}$.

\begin{whybox}
Hasse's theorem guarantees two things curve designers need. First, the group is large
(roughly $p$ points), giving a large discrete-log search space. Second, a polynomial-time
point-counting algorithm (Ren\'e Schoof's $1985$ algorithm, which computes $t \bmod \ell$
for small primes $\ell$ without listing the points) can determine $\#E(\F_p)$ for
$256$-bit primes. Knowing the exact order is essential: secure ECC requires $\#E(\F_p) =
h \cdot n$ where $n$ is a large prime and the \emph{cofactor} $h = \#E(\F_p)/n$ is a tiny
integer (such as $1$, $4$, or $8$). If $\#E(\F_p)$ instead factors into small primes, the
Pohlig--Hellman attack (Section~\ref{ch21:pitfalls}) splits the discrete logarithm into
small sub-problems and breaks the curve.
\end{whybox}

\section{Scalar multiplication and the ECDLP}
\label{ch21:ecdlp}

In a multiplicative group we wrote the repeated operation as exponentiation $g^a$. On a
curve the operation is written additively, so repeating it means \emph{scalar
multiplication}:
\[
kP = \underbrace{P + P + \cdots + P}_{k \text{ times}}.
\]
This is the additive analog of $g^k$. Just as $g^k$ is computed by square-and-multiply,
$kP$ is computed by \emph{double-and-add}: write $k$ in binary and scan the bits from
most significant to least significant.

\begin{example}[Double-and-add]
\label{ch21:dbladd}
To compute $13P$, write $13 = 1101_2$. Starting with accumulator $R = P$ at the leading
$1$-bit and scanning the remaining bits $\mathtt{101}$ from left to right, double $R$ at
each step and add $P$ whenever the bit is $1$:
\[
P \;\xrightarrow{\,\mathtt{1}\,}\; 2P + P = 3P
  \;\xrightarrow{\,\mathtt{0}\,}\; 6P
  \;\xrightarrow{\,\mathtt{1}\,}\; 12P + P = 13P.
\]
That uses three doublings and two additions instead of twelve additions. For a $256$-bit
scalar, double-and-add performs at most $255$ doublings and on average $128$ additions ---
a few hundred curve operations, versus roughly $2^{256}$ for repeated addition by $P$.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}[Elliptic-Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem]
\label{ch21:ecdlp-def}
Let $E$ be an elliptic curve over $\F_p$ and let $P \in E(\F_p)$ be a point of prime
order $n$. Given $P$ and $Q = kP$ for an unknown integer $k \in \{0,\dots,n-1\}$, the
\textbf{elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem (ECDLP)} is to recover $k$.
\end{definition}

Computing $kP$ from $k$ is fast via double-and-add. Reversing it --- recovering $k$ from
$P$ and $Q = kP$ --- has no known efficient classical algorithm on a well-chosen curve.
ECC's security rests on that gap, just as Diffie--Hellman's in $\Zns{p}$ rests on the
finite-field discrete logarithm.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Think of scalar multiplication as a deterministic walk that hops wildly across the grid.
Stepping through $P, 2P, 3P, 4P, \dots$ visits coordinates that show no visible pattern
in $k$. Given a target point $Q$, no algebraic formula reads off the step count $k$
directly. The fastest generic search is Pollard's rho cycle-finding algorithm, which
needs about $\sqrt{n}$ group operations rather than $n$ --- and when $n \approx 2^{256}$,
$\sqrt{n} \approx 2^{128}$ remains far out of reach.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Why ECC keys are small: generic versus special attacks}
\label{ch21:keysize}

In \emph{any} finite cyclic group of prime order $n$, the discrete logarithm can be solved
by generic algorithms --- baby-step giant-step or Pollard's rho --- in about $\sqrt{n}$
group operations. These algorithms treat the group as a black box, calling only the group
law and equality tests, so they run identically on $\Zns{p}$, on an elliptic curve, or on
any other group of order $n$. Victor Shoup proved in $1997$ that $\sqrt{n}$ is an absolute
lower bound: no generic algorithm can solve the discrete logarithm in fewer than
$\Omega(\sqrt{n})$ group operations.

The gap between $\Zns{p}$ and an elliptic curve comes from what happens \emph{outside} the
generic model:

\begin{itemize}
  \item In $\Zns{p}$, elements are represented by integers, and \textbf{index calculus}
        (and its faster cousin, the number field sieve for discrete logs) exploits prime
        factorization of smooth integers to run in \emph{sub-exponential} time
        \[
        L_p[1/3] = \exp\!\Bigl(c\,(\ln p)^{1/3}(\ln\ln p)^{2/3}\Bigr),
        \]
        where $c > 0$ is a small constant ($c \approx 1.92$ for the general number field
        sieve) and $\ln p$ grows linearly with the bit-length of $p$. Because $L_p[1/3]$
        is much smaller than $\sqrt{p}$, $p$ must be inflated to about $3072$ bits before
        $L_p[1/3]$ reaches $2^{128}$ operations.
  \item On a general elliptic curve over $\F_p$, \textbf{no index-calculus attack is
        known}. Points do not factor into ``prime points,'' leaving no smooth subset from
        which to collect linear relations. The fastest known classical attack remains the
        generic $\sqrt{n}$ search, so $n$ only needs $256$ bits to make $\sqrt{n} \approx
        2^{128}$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{whybox}
Standard symmetric security targets $2^{128}$ operations (matching AES-128). Setting the
generic attack cost $\sqrt{n}$ to $2^{128}$ gives $n \approx (2^{128})^2 = 2^{256}$, so a
curve over a $256$-bit prime field achieves $128$-bit security with $256$-bit keys.

Setting the sub-exponential cost $L_p[1/3]$ to $2^{128}$ in $\Zns{p}$ (or for an RSA
modulus) requires $p \approx 2^{3072}$ --- twelve times the key bits and far heavier
arithmetic per handshake. Figure~\ref{fig:ch21-keysize-chart} and the table below compare
required key lengths across standard security levels.
\end{whybox}

\begin{connectionbox}
The $\sqrt n$ cost of Pollard's rho comes from the \emph{birthday bound} (Chapter~7). The
algorithm steps deterministically through points of the form $a_i P + b_i Q$ until two
steps land on the same point; by the birthday paradox, a collision occurs after roughly
$\sqrt n$ steps, giving a linear congruence $a_i + b_i k \equiv a_j + b_j k \pmod n$ that
solves for $k$. Here the sample space is the cyclic group of size $n$.
\end{connectionbox}

\subsection{When the security collapses: pitfalls}
\label{ch21:pitfalls}

ECC is only as strong as the chosen curve and its implementation. Three classes of
failures have broken real deployments.

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Pohlig--Hellman: composite group order.} Suppose the base point's order $n$
factors into pairwise coprime prime powers $n = q_1^{e_1} q_2^{e_2} \cdots q_r^{e_r}$.
The Pohlig--Hellman algorithm solves for $k \bmod q_i^{e_i}$ separately (in roughly
$e_i \sqrt{q_i}$ curve operations for each prime $q_i$) and reassembles $k \bmod n$ via
the Chinese remainder theorem. A curve of order $8 \cdot (\text{$253$-bit prime})$ is as
strong as its $253$-bit prime factor, whereas a curve whose order is a product of small or
medium primes falls quickly. \emph{Fix:} choose a curve whose order is $h \cdot n$ for a
large prime $n$ and a tiny cofactor $h = \#E(\F_p)/n$ (typically $h \in \{1,2,4,8\}$).
\end{attackbox}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Anomalous and supersingular curves.} Two rare curve families carry extra algebraic
structure that bypasses the $\sqrt{n}$ generic floor. \emph{Anomalous} curves over $\F_p$
satisfy $\#E(\F_p) = p$ (trace $t = 1$); the Smart--Satoh--Araki attack maps them to the
additive group $(\F_p, +)$, solving the ECDLP in polynomial time. \emph{Supersingular}
curves (over a prime field $\F_p$ with $p \ge 5$, those with trace $t = 0$, so
$\#E(\F_p) = p + 1$) succumb to the Menezes--Okamoto--Vanstone (MOV) and Frey--R\"uck
attacks, which use a \emph{pairing} --- a bilinear map sending pairs of curve points into
a small extension field $\F_{p^k}^{*}$ --- to transfer the ECDLP into $\F_{p^k}^{*}$,
where sub-exponential index calculus applies. \emph{Fix:} use a vetted standard curve,
never home-grown parameters.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Invalid-curve and small-subgroup attacks.} Look back at the short Weierstrass
addition formulas in Section~\ref{ch21:grouplaw-alg}: neither slope formula uses the
constant term $b$. Any point $(x_0, y_0) \in \F_p^2$ lies on \emph{some} curve
$y^2 \equiv x^3 + ax + b' \pmod p$, namely the one with $b' = y_0^2 - x_0^3 - ax_0 \bmod p$.
If a server receives a point from the network and skips checking $y^2 \equiv x^3 + ax + b
\pmod p$, an attacker can submit a low-order point on a weak curve $E_{a,b'}$, observe the
server's response, and learn the server's secret scalar modulo that small order. Repeating
across several small primes reconstructs the full private key via the Chinese remainder
theorem. This attack broke deployed TLS and Bluetooth stacks. \emph{Fix:} validate that
every received point satisfies the curve equation and is not $\mathcal{O}$ (and check
subgroup membership when $h > 1$), or use X25519, whose design prevents the attack.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{trapbox}
The lesson common to all of these is: \textbf{do not invent your own curve, and do
not skip point validation.} The mathematics of secure curve selection (point counting,
cofactor analysis, twist security, avoiding anomalous/supersingular families) is
specialist work. Use NIST P-256, Curve25519, or another standardized curve, and validate
every point you receive.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Elliptic-Curve Diffie--Hellman (ECDH)}
\label{ch21:ecdh}

With a hard ECDLP in hand, Diffie--Hellman carries over to the curve with no change in
protocol structure (Figure~\ref{ch21:ecdh-fig}). Replace the multiplicative group
$\Zns{p}$ by the cyclic curve subgroup $\langle P\rangle = \{\mathcal{O}, P, 2P, \dots,
(n-1)P\}$ of prime order $n$, replace modular exponentiation $g^a$ by scalar
multiplication $aP$, and you have \textbf{Elliptic-Curve Diffie--Hellman (ECDH)}.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The ECDH protocol.} Public parameters: a curve $E$ over $\F_p$ and a base point
$P \in E(\F_p)$ of prime order $n$.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Alice chooses a uniformly random secret scalar $a \getsr \{1,\dots,n-1\}$ and
        sends $A = aP$.
  \item Bob chooses a uniformly random secret scalar $b \getsr \{1,\dots,n-1\}$ and
        sends $B = bP$.
  \item Alice computes $S = aB = a(bP) = (ab)P$.
  \item Bob computes $S = bA = b(aP) = (ab)P$.
\end{enumerate}
Both arrive at the same curve point $S = (ab)P$. They derive symmetric session keys by
feeding the $x$-coordinate $x_S$ of $S$ into a key-derivation function such as HMAC-based
HKDF: $k = \operatorname{HKDF}(x_S)$.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{whybox}
Eve sees $P$, $A=aP$, and $B=bP$. To compute $S = (ab)P$ she must either recover $a$ from
$A$ or $b$ from $B$ (solving the ECDLP) or solve the \emph{Computational Diffie--Hellman}
(CDH) problem on the curve: compute $(ab)P$ from $(P, aP, bP)$ without finding either
scalar. On standard curves no method faster than solving the ECDLP is known, costing
about $2^{128}$ operations on a $256$-bit curve. Like finite-field Diffie--Hellman,
unauthenticated ECDH cannot stop an active man-in-the-middle attacker from substituting
her own points, so protocols pair it with digital signatures or pre-shared keys
(Chapter~23).
\end{whybox}

\begin{example}[ECDH on the toy curve]
\label{ch21:ecdh-toy}
Reuse $E: y^2 \equiv x^3+3x+8 \pmod{13}$ with base point $P=(1,8)$ of order $9$. Suppose
Alice chooses $a=2$ and Bob chooses $b=3$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Alice sends $A = 2P = (2,3)$ (computed earlier).
  \item Bob sends $B = 3P = (9,6)$.
  \item Alice computes $aB = 2\cdot(3P) = 6P = (9,7)$.
  \item Bob computes $bA = 3\cdot(2P) = 6P = (9,7)$.
\end{itemize}
Both obtain $S = 6P = (9,7)$. In production, $n$ is a $256$-bit prime rather than $9$, so
$a$ and $b$ are chosen from roughly $2^{256}$ possibilities, while the algebraic steps
stay identical.
\end{example}

\begin{implementbox}
A toy ECDH in Python, written for clarity, not safety. The arithmetic mirrors the
formulas of Section~\ref{ch21:grouplaw-alg} exactly.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
# TOY elliptic-curve Diffie-Hellman -- FOR LEARNING ONLY.
# No constant-time arithmetic, no point validation, no side-channel
# protection, tiny insecure parameters. NEVER use this for anything real.

p, a, b = 13, 3, 8                 # curve y^2 = x^3 + 3x + 8 over F_13
O = None                           # the point at infinity (identity)

def inv(z):                        # modular inverse via Fermat (p prime)
    return pow(z % p, p - 2, p)

def add(P, Q):
    if P is O: return Q
    if Q is O: return P
    x1, y1 = P; x2, y2 = Q
    if x1 == x2 and (y1 + y2) % p == 0:
        return O                   # P == -Q  ->  infinity
    if P == Q:
        m = (3 * x1 * x1 + a) * inv(2 * y1) % p     # tangent slope
    else:
        m = (y2 - y1) * inv(x2 - x1) % p            # chord slope
    x3 = (m * m - x1 - x2) % p
    y3 = (m * (x1 - x3) - y1) % p
    return (x3, y3)

def scalar_mul(k, P):              # double-and-add
    R, base = O, P
    while k:
        if k & 1: R = add(R, base)
        base = add(base, base)
        k >>= 1
    return R

P = (1, 8)                         # base point, order 9 on this curve
a_sec, b_sec = 2, 3                # Alice's and Bob's secret scalars
A = scalar_mul(a_sec, P)           # Alice's public point
B = scalar_mul(b_sec, P)           # Bob's public point
print("Alice:", scalar_mul(a_sec, B))   # a*(bP)
print("Bob:  ", scalar_mul(b_sec, A))   # b*(aP)  -- same point
\end{lstlisting}

Running this prints the same shared point for both parties. The warning in the
comments matters: a real ECDH must use constant-time scalar multiplication (to resist the
timing attacks of Chapter~37), must validate incoming points (to resist the
invalid-curve attack above), and must use a vetted curve with a cryptographic field size.
\end{implementbox}

\section{Curve25519 and X25519}
\label{ch21:curve25519}

The most widely deployed curve today is not one of the original NIST curves but
\textbf{Curve25519}, designed by Daniel J. Bernstein in $2005$. It underlies the
\textbf{X25519} key-exchange function used in TLS 1.3, Signal, SSH, WireGuard, and many
other protocols. Curve25519 displaced the older NIST curves for concrete cryptographic
engineering reasons.

Curve25519 is written in \emph{Montgomery form},
\[
E : y^2 = x^3 + 486662\,x^2 + x \quad \text{over}\quad \F_p, \qquad p = 2^{255} - 19,
\]
an alternative cubic equation whose chord-and-tangent law enables fast arithmetic on
$x$-coordinates alone. Because $2^{255} \equiv 19 \pmod p$, upper bits that overflow $255$
bits fold back into range by multiplying by $19$ instead of running multi-word division,
making field arithmetic fast in software. The curve has order $8n$ for a $252$-bit prime
$n$ (cofactor $h = 8$).

\begin{aestheticbox}
X25519 is fast, and equally important, it eliminates common \emph{implementation traps}
by building defenses into the function definition itself:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Montgomery ladder.} Instead of tracking one point and adding only on
        $1$-bits, the Montgomery ladder tracks an adjacent pair $(R_0, R_1) = (mP, (m+1)P)$
        and executes \emph{one addition and one doubling on every bit} of the scalar
        (Figure~\ref{fig:ch21-montgomery-ladder}), using constant-time arithmetic swaps
        rather than conditional branches. Execution time does not depend on the secret
        scalar's bits.
  \item \textbf{$x$-coordinate only and twist security.} X25519 transmits and computes on
        $x$-coordinates alone, shrinking public keys to $32$ bytes. Every $32$-byte string
        decodes to an integer $x$, which is reduced modulo $p$. When $f(x) = x^3 + 486662x^2 + x$
        is a square in $\F_p$, $x$ is the $x$-coordinate of a point on Curve25519; when
        $f(x)$ is a non-square, dividing by a fixed non-square $d \in \F_p$ makes $f(x)/d$
        a square, so $x$ lies on the companion curve $d\,y^2 = x^3 + 486662x^2 + x$, called
        the \emph{quadratic twist}. Bernstein chose the constant $486662$ so that both
        Curve25519 and its quadratic twist have large prime-order subgroups (\emph{twist
        security}, with twist order $4n'$ for a $253$-bit prime $n'$). Because both curves
        are safe, there is no ``is this point on the curve?'' check to forget, eliminating
        invalid-curve attacks.
  \item \textbf{Cofactor clearing via clamping.} Before multiplying, X25519 \emph{clamps}
        the $32$-byte secret scalar: it clears the bottom three bits (making the scalar a
        multiple of $8$), clears bit $255$, and sets bit $254$ to $1$. Being a multiple of
        $8$ annihilates any component of order dividing $8$ or $4$ on the curve or its
        twist, neutralizing small-subgroup attacks, while fixing bit $254$ ensures the
        ladder always starts at the same bit position.
  \item \textbf{Transparent constants.} Every parameter ($p = 2^{255}-19$ and $A = 486662$,
        the smallest integer satisfying the security and efficiency conditions) follows
        from stated public rules, leaving no room to hide a backdoor --- a major advantage
        after the $2013$ \texttt{Dual\_EC\_DRBG} scandal.
\end{itemize}
\end{aestheticbox}

\label{fig:ch21-montgomery-ladder}
\end{figure}

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Using X25519 (the right way).} As an application programmer you never touch the
group law. The interface is two functions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Generate a $32$-byte random secret $a$; compute the public key
        $A = \texttt{X25519}(a, \text{basepoint})$.
  \item On receiving the peer's public key $B$, compute the shared secret
        $S = \texttt{X25519}(a, B)$, then run $S$ through a key-derivation function (HKDF)
        to get your symmetric keys.
\end{itemize}
That is the whole API. The library handles clamping, the ladder, and reduction. This is
the model you should follow everywhere in cryptography: use a vetted, narrow,
hard-to-misuse interface, and never reach for the primitives directly.
\end{focusbox}

\subsection{Edwards curves and complete addition formulas}
\label{ch21:edwards}

Short Weierstrass curves carry an implementation hazard: \emph{exceptional cases}.
Look back at the addition formulas of Section~\ref{ch21:grouplaw-alg}. If $P \neq \pm Q$,
you compute a chord slope; if $P = Q$ with $y_1 \neq 0$, you compute a tangent slope; if
$P = -Q$, you return $\mathcal{O}$; and if either input is $\mathcal{O}$, you return the
other input. In software, any \texttt{if} statement whose condition depends on secret data
invites a timing side-channel attack: an adversary measuring execution time or probing CPU
branch predictors and instruction caches can infer which branch ran and reconstruct the
secret scalar.

In $2007$, Harold Edwards introduced an alternative curve equation that removes those case
splits. An \textbf{Edwards curve} over a field $K$ (of characteristic not $2$) is the set
of solutions to
\begin{equation}
\label{ch21:edwards-eq}
x^2 + y^2 = 1 + d\,x^2 y^2, \qquad d \in K \setminus \{0, 1\}.
\end{equation}
Plotted over $\R$ with $d < 0$ (Figure~\ref{ch21:edwards-fig}), the curve forms a smooth,
four-fold symmetric closed loop around the origin. Its identity element is the ordinary
affine point $(0, 1)$ --- no point at infinity is needed when $d$ is not a square --- and
the inverse of $(x, y)$ is $(-x, y)$, reflection across the vertical $y$-axis.

The addition law on an Edwards curve is symmetric in its two inputs:
\begin{equation}
\label{ch21:edwards-add}
(x_1, y_1) + (x_2, y_2) = \left( \frac{x_1 y_2 + y_1 x_2}{1 + d\,x_1 x_2 y_1 y_2}, \;
\frac{y_1 y_2 - x_1 x_2}{1 - d\,x_1 x_2 y_1 y_2} \right).
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Completeness of Edwards addition, Bernstein--Lange 2007]
\label{ch21:thm-edwards-complete}
 Let $K$ be a field of characteristic not $2$ and $d \in K \setminus \{0,1\}$. If $d$ is
not a square in $K$ (for $K = \F_p$, $d$ is a quadratic non-residue modulo $p$), then the
denominators $1 \pm d\,x_1 x_2 y_1 y_2$ are \textbf{never zero} for any points
$(x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2)$ satisfying~\eqref{ch21:edwards-eq} over $K$.
\end{theorem}

This property is called \textbf{completeness}. Equation~\eqref{ch21:edwards-add} works
unchanged for \emph{every} pair of curve points:
\begin{itemize}
  \item It adds distinct points $P \neq Q$.
  \item It doubles points when $P = Q$: setting $(x_2, y_2) = (x_1, y_1)$ produces $2P$.
  \item It adds the identity $(0, 1)$: substituting $(x_2, y_2) = (0, 1)$ returns $(x_1, y_1)$.
  \item It adds inverses: $(x, y) + (-x, y)$ evaluates directly to $(0, 1) = \mathcal{O}$
        because $x^2 + y^2 = 1 + d x^2 y^2$ makes the numerator and denominator of the
        second coordinate equal.
\end{itemize}

There are no exceptional points, no separate doubling routine, and no case branches. Every
point addition executes the exact same sequence of field operations (and in projective
Edwards coordinates, with zero modular inversions inside the loop).

\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{Two coordinate models for Curve25519.}
Introducing a coefficient $a$ in front of $x^2$ gives \emph{twisted Edwards curves},
$a x^2 + y^2 = 1 + d x^2 y^2$. The Montgomery curve Curve25519 used in X25519 is
\emph{birationally equivalent} --- related by a reversible change of variables written as
quotients of polynomials --- to the twisted Edwards curve
\[
-x^2 + y^2 \equiv 1 - \frac{121665}{121666}\,x^2 y^2 \pmod{2^{255}-19},
\]
where $\frac{121665}{121666}$ denotes $121665 \cdot 121666^{-1} \bmod (2^{255}-19)$.
Montgomery coordinates are fastest for $x$-only Diffie--Hellman (X25519), whereas twisted
Edwards coordinates provide complete, branch-free point addition for \textbf{Ed25519}, the
signature scheme built on the same underlying group (Chapter~22).
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Where curves run the internet.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{TLS 1.3} (HTTPS): key exchange uses ECDH, with X25519 as the default group
        in modern browsers and web servers.
  \item \textbf{Signal / WhatsApp}: Signal's Extended Triple Diffie--Hellman (X3DH)
        initial handshake and Double Ratchet per-message key-updating protocol both run on
        X25519, while identity signatures use Ed25519 (Chapter~22).
  \item \textbf{Secure Shell (SSH)}: \texttt{curve25519-sha256} is the default key
        exchange in OpenSSH, the standard open-source SSH implementation.
  \item \textbf{WireGuard} (virtual private networks, or VPNs): X25519 is the \emph{only}
        key-exchange primitive --- the protocol avoids algorithm negotiation in favor of
        one misuse-resistant curve.
  \item \textbf{Cryptocurrencies}: Bitcoin and Ethereum sign transactions using the
        Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA) on the Weierstrass curve
        \texttt{secp256k1}.
\end{itemize}
Compact keys, fast arithmetic, and side-channel-safe formulas make elliptic curves fit
phones, embedded microcontrollers, and high-throughput servers alike.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Why ECC dominates: the summary}
\label{ch21:dominance}

Elliptic-curve cryptography has four advantages over RSA and finite-field Diffie--Hellman.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Small keys for equal security.} $256$-bit ECC matches $3072$-bit RSA.
        Less bandwidth per handshake, less storage per key, smaller certificates.
  \item \textbf{Fast operations.} Scalar multiplication on a $256$-bit curve is cheaper
        than a $3072$-bit modular exponentiation, so handshakes are faster and servers
        carry more connections per core.
  \item \textbf{Hard problem, no shortcut.} The ECDLP resists the sub-exponential
        index-calculus attacks that force RSA and finite-field DH to inflate their keys;
        the best attack on a good curve is generic $\sqrt n$.
  \item \textbf{Misuse-resistant modern designs.} Curves like Curve25519 engineer away
        timing leaks, invalid-point attacks, and small-subgroup attacks at the level of
        the primitive, not the application.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{marginnotebox}
One caveat: ECC, like RSA and finite-field DH, falls to a large-scale quantum computer.
Shor's quantum algorithm solves the ECDLP (as well as integer factoring and finite-field
discrete log) in polynomial time. Smaller keys do not help --- a $256$-bit curve requires
\emph{fewer} error-corrected quantum bits (\emph{logical qubits}) to break than a
$3072$-bit RSA modulus. Chapter~35 covers \emph{post-quantum} schemes designed to resist
quantum attacks; current practice runs X25519 alongside a post-quantum key-exchange
algorithm in a \emph{hybrid handshake} (combining both shared secrets in HKDF), keeping
the session safe as long as either scheme holds.
\end{marginnotebox}

\section*{What to remember}

An elliptic curve over a finite field $\F_p$ is a finite abelian group whose elements are
points $(x,y)$ satisfying $y^2 \equiv x^3 + ax + b \pmod p$, plus an identity point at
infinity $\mathcal{O}$. The group law is chord-and-tangent addition, computed in practice
with projective coordinates so an entire scalar multiplication $kP$ needs only one modular
inversion at the end. Reversing $Q = kP$ is the ECDLP. Because no index-calculus attack is
known on a well-chosen curve, the fastest classical attack is generic $\sqrt{n}$ search,
letting $256$-bit curves match the $128$-bit security of $3072$-bit RSA. ECDH carries
Diffie--Hellman onto the curve unchanged, and X25519 packages it behind a constant-time,
misuse-resistant interface that secures most modern key exchanges.

Agreeing on a shared secret is not enough on its own: without authentication, an active
adversary in the middle can impersonate either party. Chapter~22 introduces digital
signatures, showing how RSA and elliptic curves bind messages and public keys to
verifiable identities.

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch21:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Point counting by hand]
\label{ch21:ex-count}
Let $E: y^2 \equiv x^3 + 2x + 3 \pmod 7$ over $\F_7$. First verify nonsingularity via
$4a^3 + 27b^2 \bmod 7$. Then, for each $x \in \{0,\dots,6\}$, compute $f(x)=x^3+2x+3
\bmod 7$, decide whether it is a quadratic residue (the nonzero squares mod $7$ are
$\{1,2,4\}$), and list all points. How many points does $E(\F_7)$ have, and does your
count satisfy Hasse's bound $\abs{\#E(\F_7) - 8} \le 2\sqrt 7 \approx 5.29$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Adding and doubling]
\label{ch21:ex-add}
On the curve $E: y^2 \equiv x^3 + 3x + 8 \pmod{13}$ over $\F_{13}$, take $P=(1,8)$. Using
the algebraic formulas, compute $4P$ (add $P$ to $3P=(9,6)$), then $5P,\dots$ until you
reach $\mathcal{O}$. Confirm that $9P = \mathcal{O}$, proving $P$ has order $9$. Show all
modular-inverse computations. (You should find $4P=(12,11)$ and $5P=(12,2)$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A full group]
\label{ch21:ex-subgroup}
Continuing the previous exercise, write out the complete cyclic group
$\langle P\rangle = \{\mathcal{O}, P, 2P, \dots, 8P\}$ with coordinates (here $P$
generates all nine points). Verify that $4P + 5P = \mathcal{O}$ and that $-P = 8P$ by
checking that $8P = (x_P, -y_P \bmod 13)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Toy ECDH]
\label{ch21:ex-ecdh}
Using the curve and base point of Exercise~\ref{ch21:ex-add}, let Alice's secret be
$a=3$ and Bob's be $b=4$. Compute the public points $A=3P$ and $B=4P$, then the shared
secret from each side ($3B$ and $4A$), and confirm they agree. What goes wrong with this
``key exchange'' for real use, given that $P$ has order only $9$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Why no eavesdropper shortcut]
\label{ch21:ex-eve}
Explain, in terms of the ECDLP and the Computational Diffie--Hellman problem, which
computational problem Eve must solve to recover the ECDH shared secret from the
transcript $(P, aP, bP)$. Why does knowing $aP$ and $bP$ not let her ``multiply'' to get
$(ab)P$ directly?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Key-size reasoning]
\label{ch21:ex-keysize}
The fastest generic discrete-log attack runs in about $\sqrt n$ group operations, where
$n$ is the subgroup order. (a) What size $n$ (in bits) gives $128$-bit security?
(b) Explain why $\Zns{p}$ needs a roughly $3072$-bit prime for the same $128$-bit
security, naming the attack responsible. (c) A junior engineer proposes a $128$-bit curve
for ``$128$-bit security.'' What is the actual security level of a curve with a $128$-bit
group order, and why?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Pohlig--Hellman intuition]
\label{ch21:ex-pohlig}
Suppose a point $P$ has order $n = 2^4 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot q$, where $q$ is a
$30$-bit prime. Roughly how much work is the ECDLP in $\langle P\rangle$, and which factor
dominates? State the design rule this motivates for choosing the group order, and identify
which attack class it defends against.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Invalid-curve attack]
\label{ch21:ex-invalid}
The addition formulas of Section~\ref{ch21:grouplaw-alg} never use the coefficient $b$.
Explain how an attacker exploits this when a server fails to check that a received point
lies on the agreed curve, and why working ``on the wrong curve'' can leak the server's
secret scalar modulo small numbers. State the two-line fix.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Discriminant matters]
\label{ch21:ex-singular}
Show that the curve $y^2 = x^3$ (so $a=b=0$) fails the nonsingularity test, and find the
single point where it is singular. Explain geometrically why the chord-and-tangent
construction breaks there (hint: what does the curve look like near the origin?).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Prove an inverse formula]
\label{ch21:ex-inverse}
Prove from the group law that for any non-identity point $P=(x,y)$ on
$y^2 = x^3+ax+b$, the inverse is exactly $-P = (x,-y)$. (Show that $P + (x,-y)$ falls
into the vertical-line case and therefore equals $\mathcal{O}$.) Then prove $-\mathcal{O}
= \mathcal{O}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Why X25519 needs no point check]
\label{ch21:ex-x25519}
X25519 operates on only the $x$-coordinate of points and accepts any $32$-byte string as
input. Argue informally why this design choice removes the invalid-curve attack of
Exercise~\ref{ch21:ex-invalid} as a class --- that is, why there is no ``off-curve''
input an attacker can supply that the implementation must reject. (Hint: think about the
quadratic twist and how clamping confines the result to the safe subgroup.)
\end{exercise}
