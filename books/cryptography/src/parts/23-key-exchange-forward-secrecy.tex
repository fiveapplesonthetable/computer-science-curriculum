\chapter{Key Exchange and Forward Secrecy}

In Chapter~18, we saw how Diffie and Hellman let two strangers agree on a shared
secret over a public channel, and in Chapter~22 we saw how digital signatures
prove who authored a message. On its own, however, an unauthenticated
Diffie--Hellman handshake has a fatal weakness: an active adversary, Eve, can
sit between Alice and Bob and run a separate handshake with each of them,
impersonating Bob to Alice and Alice to Bob.

What does it take to turn bare Diffie--Hellman into a key exchange safe to run
over an untrusted network? Four ingredients are missing. First,
\emph{authentication}: each party must verify who holds the other end of the
channel, so that Alice shares a secret with Bob rather than with Eve. Second,
\emph{forward secrecy}: if Bob's long-term private key is stolen next year, the
encrypted traffic Eve recorded this morning must stay unreadable. Third, a
\emph{key derivation function} to turn the single group element produced by the
handshake into the several independent symmetric keys a session requires.
Fourth, defenses against attackers who re-send recorded messages or tamper with
algorithm negotiation. Together, these pieces form the handshake of Transport
Layer Security (TLS)~1.3 and the Signal messaging protocol (Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-ake-ingredients}).

\section{The threat we are defending against}

Alice and Bob want to establish a shared secret key over a network controlled
by Eve. Eve has three capabilities:

\begin{itemize}
  \item She sees every byte that crosses the network (eavesdropping).
  \item She can drop, reorder, re-send, and modify messages, and inject her own
        (an \emph{active}, or \emph{man-in-the-middle} (MITM), adversary).
  \item She knows the protocol specification completely. The only things hidden
        from her are the secret keys and the random bits the parties generate.
\end{itemize}

Against this adversary we want two guarantees.

\begin{definition}[Informal goals of authenticated key exchange]
\label{ch23:ake-goals}
A \emph{key-exchange protocol} run between two parties produces a \emph{session
key} $k$ --- a temporary symmetric key used to protect one connection or
conversation. The protocol is a secure \emph{authenticated key exchange} (AKE)
if:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Secrecy.} An adversary who watches and tampers with the
        protocol cannot distinguish the session key $k$ from a uniformly random
        bitstring of the same length. To anyone outside the two participants,
        $k$ looks like fresh coin flips.
  \item \textbf{Authentication.} When Alice finishes a run believing she shares
        $k$ with Bob, either she shares $k$ with the genuine Bob, or Bob has no
        matching session at all (he never accepted). She is never fooled into
        sharing a key with Eve while thinking her partner is Bob.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Secrecy here is the distinguishing game from Chapters~8 and~10, applied to the
derived key: no efficient distinguisher can tell $k$ apart from uniform random
bits. In Diffie--Hellman protocols, this guarantee rests on the Decisional
Diffie--Hellman (DDH) assumption (Chapter~18), which states that given $g^a$ and
$g^b$, the shared element $g^{ab}$ is indistinguishable from a random group
element $g^c$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Secrecy means an outsider learns nothing useful about the key you derive.
Authentication means you know who else holds it. Plain Diffie--Hellman provides
the first without the second, and Eve exploits that gap.
\end{intuitionbox}

\section{Recalling the bare handshake, and watching it fail}

Fix a cyclic group $\G$ of prime order $q$ with generator $g$ --- for instance,
a prime-order subgroup of $\Zns{p}$ for a large prime $p$, or a prime-order
elliptic-curve group. Writing $x \getsr S$ for drawing $x$ uniformly at random
from a finite set $S$, the unauthenticated Diffie--Hellman exchange takes one
round (Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-bare-dh}):

Both sides arrive at $g^{ab}$. A passive eavesdropper sees $g^a$ and $g^b$; to
compute $g^{ab}$ from them alone requires solving the \emph{Computational
Diffie--Hellman} (CDH) problem (Chapter~18), which is assumed infeasible in $\G$.

Now make Eve active. Instead of attacking CDH, she runs two handshakes at once.

\begin{attackbox}
\label{ch23:mitm}
\textbf{Man-in-the-middle on unauthenticated Diffie--Hellman.}
Eve intercepts Alice's $A=g^a$ and Bob's $B=g^b$ so neither share reaches its
intended recipient. She samples her own exponent $e\getsr\{1,\dots,q-1\}$ and:
\begin{itemize}
  \item sends $E=g^e$ to Bob, pretending to be Alice;
  \item sends $E=g^e$ to Alice, pretending to be Bob (she may reuse $e$ or pick
        a separate $e'$ toward Alice).
\end{itemize}
Alice computes $k_A=E^a=g^{ae}$ and believes she shares it with Bob. Bob
computes $k_B=E^b=g^{be}$ and believes he shares it with Alice. Because Eve
knows $e$, she computes both keys: $k_A=A^e=g^{ae}$ and $k_B=B^e=g^{be}$
(Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-mitm}). She decrypts every message Alice encrypts under
$k_A$, reads or edits it, re-encrypts it under $k_B$, and forwards it to Bob.
Neither endpoint detects the relay.
\end{attackbox}

The lesson is: \textbf{a key is only as trustworthy as your knowledge of
who holds the other copy.} Diffie--Hellman gives you a shared secret with
\emph{somebody}. Turning ``somebody'' into ``Bob'' requires authentication, and
authentication cannot come from the group algebra alone. It must come from
something Alice already knows about Bob: a long-term public key bound to his
identity, whose private half Eve does not possess.

\section{Authenticating the handshake}

Two long-term-key mechanisms can pin an identity: digital signatures and
long-term Diffie--Hellman keys. They give rise to two families of authenticated
key exchange. TLS~1.3 uses the signature-based style; Signal and WireGuard use
the implicit Diffie--Hellman style.

\subsection{Signed ephemeral Diffie--Hellman}

An \emph{ephemeral} key is generated fresh for one session and erased right
after the session key is derived, whereas a \emph{long-term} (or \emph{static})
key persists across many sessions. Give Bob a long-term signature key pair
$(sk_B, pk_B)$, where $sk_B$ is his private signing key and $pk_B$ is his public
verification key. Suppose Alice already holds an authentic copy of $pk_B$ ---
via a \emph{certificate} (a digitally signed statement from a trusted authority
binding Bob's name to $pk_B$, treated in Chapter~24), a \emph{pinned key}
(hardcoded or pre-configured in Alice's software), or \emph{trust on first use}
(saving $pk_B$ on the first connection and verifying that future connections
present the same key). In signed ephemeral Diffie--Hellman, both Diffie--Hellman
shares are ephemeral, and Bob signs the exchange with $sk_B$
(Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-signed-dh}).

\label{ch23:fig-signed-dh}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Alice samples $a\getsr\{1,\dots,q-1\}$ and sends $A=g^a$.
  \item Bob samples $b\getsr\{1,\dots,q-1\}$ and signs the \emph{transcript} ---
        the ordered sequence of handshake messages exchanged so far, here the
        concatenation $A\concat B$ --- producing
        $\sigma_B=\Sign_{sk_B}(A\concat B)$. He sends $(B,\sigma_B)$.
  \item Alice runs the verification check $\Vrfy_{pk_B}(A\concat B,\sigma_B)$.
        If it accepts, she knows the holder of $sk_B$ saw \emph{her} $A$ and
        endorsed \emph{this} $B$. She computes $k=B^a=g^{ab}$ and erases $a$.
  \item Bob computes $k=A^b=g^{ab}$ and erases $b$. (When Bob must also verify
        Alice's identity --- \emph{mutual authentication} --- Alice holds her
        own signing key $sk_A$ and sends $\sigma_A=\Sign_{sk_A}(A\concat B)$ in
        step~3.)
\end{enumerate}

Why sign the transcript $A\concat B$ rather than $B$ alone? Because the
signature must \emph{bind Bob's ephemeral share to Alice's ephemeral share}. If
Bob signed $B$ by itself, Eve could record an old $(B,\sigma_B)$ pair from a
previous session and replay it into a new one, and the signature would still
verify. Covering $A\concat B$ ties the signature to this run: Alice put a fresh,
random $A$ on the wire, so a valid signature over $A\concat B$ proves Bob acted
\emph{after} seeing that $A$.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why MITM fails here.}
Return to the attack of Section~\ref{ch23:mitm}. To impersonate Bob to Alice,
Eve must send some $B'$ and a signature $\sigma'$ such that
$\Vrfy_{pk_B}(A\concat B',\sigma')$ accepts. Producing a valid signature under
$pk_B$ on a new message $A\concat B'$ is an existential forgery (Chapter~22),
which a secure signature scheme makes infeasible without $sk_B$. Eve can forward
the genuine messages untouched, in which case she has relayed an honest
Alice--Bob exchange and learned nothing: $g^{ab}$ remains hidden from her by
CDH. The signature turns ``a key with somebody'' into ``a key with the holder
of $sk_B$.''
\end{whybox}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Sign the transcript, not your own share alone.}
A recurring protocol bug is signing too little. If a party signs only its own
public value, or omits the peer's nonce, an attacker can splice signed pieces
from different sessions together. Signing a hash of the entire handshake
transcript seen so far --- as TLS~1.3's handshake-signing message
\texttt{CertificateVerify} does --- closes these splicing attacks.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{The foundation: ephemeral keys}

The exponents $a$ and $b$ are chosen fresh for one session and \emph{erased
afterward}. The long-term signing keys ($sk_B$, and $sk_A$ if used) authenticate
the transcript, yet they never enter the formula for $g^{ab}$. Separating
long-term keys for \emph{authentication} from ephemeral keys for \emph{secrecy}
is what provides forward secrecy.

\begin{historybox}
The station-to-station (STS) protocol of Diffie, van Oorschot, and Wiener (1992)
introduced signed ephemeral Diffie--Hellman with a second check: each party
signs the Diffie--Hellman shares and then computes a MAC over that signature
under the newly derived session key, proving both sides arrived at the same key
(\emph{key confirmation}). Hugo Krawczyk later showed that subtle
identity-misbinding bugs arise unless signatures and MACs cover the peers'
identities alongside the transcript; his \textsc{SIGMA} (``SIGn-and-MAc'')
protocol family (2003) fixed this and became the template for Internet Key
Exchange (IKE, the handshake protocol of Internet Protocol Security, IPsec) and
TLS~1.3.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Implicit authentication: long-term DH keys}

Signatures are not the only option. Suppose Bob's long-term key is itself a
Diffie--Hellman key: a secret exponent $b_{\mathrm{lt}}\in\{1,\dots,q-1\}$
(``lt'' for long-term) with public key $B_{\mathrm{lt}}=g^{b_{\mathrm{lt}}}$
known to Alice, while Alice holds $a_{\mathrm{lt}}$ with public key
$A_{\mathrm{lt}}=g^{a_{\mathrm{lt}}}$ known to Bob. Then Alice and Bob can
authenticate without signatures, because only the holders of $a_{\mathrm{lt}}$
and $b_{\mathrm{lt}}$ can compute the agreed secret.

An example is \emph{triple Diffie--Hellman}. Each party holds its long-term
identity key and generates a fresh ephemeral key (``eph'' for ephemeral):
\[
  \text{Alice: } (a_{\mathrm{lt}},\, a_{\mathrm{eph}}) \text{ with } (A_{\mathrm{lt}},\, A_{\mathrm{eph}}), \qquad
  \text{Bob: } (b_{\mathrm{lt}},\, b_{\mathrm{eph}}) \text{ with } (B_{\mathrm{lt}},\, B_{\mathrm{eph}}).
\]
After exchanging their ephemeral public shares $A_{\mathrm{eph}}=g^{a_{\mathrm{eph}}}$
and $B_{\mathrm{eph}}=g^{b_{\mathrm{eph}}}$, both sides compute three
Diffie--Hellman values (Figure~\ref{ch23:tripledh}):
\begin{align*}
  B_{\mathrm{eph}}^{\,a_{\mathrm{lt}}} &= g^{a_{\mathrm{lt}} b_{\mathrm{eph}}} = A_{\mathrm{lt}}^{\,b_{\mathrm{eph}}} && \text{(authenticates Alice)}, \\
  B_{\mathrm{lt}}^{\,a_{\mathrm{eph}}} &= g^{a_{\mathrm{eph}} b_{\mathrm{lt}}} = A_{\mathrm{eph}}^{\,b_{\mathrm{lt}}} && \text{(authenticates Bob)}, \\
  B_{\mathrm{eph}}^{\,a_{\mathrm{eph}}} &= g^{a_{\mathrm{eph}} b_{\mathrm{eph}}} = A_{\mathrm{eph}}^{\,b_{\mathrm{eph}}} && \text{(injects per-session freshness)},
\end{align*}
and hash their concatenation with a cryptographic hash function $\Hash$:
\[
  k = \Hash\!\bigl(
    g^{a_{\mathrm{lt}} b_{\mathrm{eph}}} \concat
    g^{a_{\mathrm{eph}} b_{\mathrm{lt}}} \concat
    g^{a_{\mathrm{eph}} b_{\mathrm{eph}}}
  \bigr).
\]

\caption{The three Diffie--Hellman combinations in triple DH. Each long-term-against-ephemeral pair ties one party's long-term identity into the key; the ephemeral-against-ephemeral pair supplies fresh randomness for forward secrecy.}
\label{ch23:tripledh}
\end{figure}

Each factor carries one security role. The first combines Alice's long-term key
with Bob's ephemeral share: only someone holding $a_{\mathrm{lt}}$ (Alice) or
$b_{\mathrm{eph}}$ (Bob) can compute it, authenticating Alice to Bob. The second
combines Bob's long-term key with Alice's ephemeral share, authenticating Bob to
Alice. The third combines the two ephemeral shares, making $k$ depend on
throwaway per-session randomness. Authentication is \emph{implicit} because no
separate signature is verified: any peer able to use the resulting $k$ must hold
the expected long-term private key.

\begin{intuitionbox}
A signature is a notarized statement: ``I, Bob, endorse this handshake,''
verifiable by anyone holding $pk_B$. Implicit authentication builds identity
directly into the key formula: nobody sends a signature, because neither side
can compute $k$ without its long-term Diffie--Hellman secret. Signal favors the
implicit style in part for privacy: omitting signatures leaves no
non-repudiable third-party proof of participation on the wire.
\end{intuitionbox}

\section{Forward secrecy}

Suppose Eve cannot break your handshake today, so she records every encrypted
session byte for byte and archives the ciphertexts. Months later she obtains
your server's \emph{long-term private key} --- through a stolen machine, a
compelled disclosure, or a memory-exposure bug (such as the 2014 Heartbleed bug
in OpenSSL, which let attackers read server RAM). What happens next hinges on
one question:

\begin{center}
\emph{Does learning the long-term private key today let Eve decrypt the traffic
she recorded yesterday?}
\end{center}

\begin{definition}[Perfect forward secrecy]
\label{ch23:pfs}
A key-exchange protocol has \emph{(perfect) forward secrecy} if compromising all
long-term private keys does not expose the session keys of \emph{past} sessions.
Equivalently, an adversary who records a session transcript and \emph{later}
learns the participants' long-term private keys still cannot distinguish that
session's key from uniform randomness.
\end{definition}

The word ``perfect'' is historical; modern specifications usually say ``forward
secrecy.'' What matters is the \emph{time ordering}: session keys derived and
used before the compromise stay safe.

\subsection{Why ephemeral DH gives it, and static encryption does not}

Compare two ways for Alice and Bob to establish a session key $k$
(Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-pfs-timeline}).

\textbf{Way 1 --- static key transport (no forward secrecy).} Alice samples a
random session key $k$, encrypts it under Bob's long-term public key $pk_B$, and
sends $c = \Enc_{pk_B}(k)$. TLS~1.2 and earlier offered this as static RSA key
transport. Eve records $c$ along with the encrypted session. Years later she
steals $sk_B$, computes $\Dec_{sk_B}(c) = k$, and decrypts the archived session.
Here $sk_B$ acts as a master key to the past: a single compromise exposes every
session ever recorded under $pk_B$.

\textbf{Way 2 --- ephemeral Diffie--Hellman (forward secrecy).} Alice and Bob
run signed ephemeral Diffie--Hellman. The session key comes from $g^{ab}$, and
both sides erase the ephemeral exponents $a$ and $b$ as soon as the handshake
ends. Bob's long-term key $sk_B$ only signed the transcript; it never entered
$g^{ab}$. When Eve later steals $sk_B$, she can forge signatures in
\emph{future} handshakes to impersonate Bob from that moment onward. For the
recorded past session, however, she holds only $g^a$, $g^b$, and $\sigma_B$.
Recovering $g^{ab}$ from $g^a$ and $g^b$ still requires solving CDH, and $sk_B$
gives no help because $sk_B$ is algebraically unrelated to $a$ and $b$.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why past sessions survive.}
Forward secrecy holds because the session secret is computed from ephemeral
exponents destroyed right after the handshake, while the long-term key only
authenticates the exchange. Compromising the authenticator cannot reconstruct
ephemeral secrets that were already erased.
\end{whybox}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{``Ephemeral'' is only as good as your memory wipe.}
Forward secrecy belongs to the full system, not the paper protocol alone. If a
server leaves the ephemeral exponent $b$ in RAM, writes it to a debug log, or
reuses one ``ephemeral'' share across thousands of connections to save CPU time,
stealing that value exposes all those sessions. Several real TLS servers cached
a single Diffie--Hellman exponent for the lifetime of the server process (a
\emph{semi-static} key) to avoid recomputing $g^b$, forfeiting forward secrecy
for every connection sharing that exponent. Ephemeral means generated fresh and
\emph{zeroized} --- overwritten with zeros in memory before deallocation --- as
soon as the session key is derived.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
Modern TLS deployments require \texttt{DHE} (Diffie--Hellman Ephemeral) or
\texttt{ECDHE} (Elliptic-Curve Diffie--Hellman Ephemeral) \emph{cipher suites}
--- named bundles specifying which algorithms a connection uses for key
exchange, authentication, encryption, and hashing. The trailing \texttt{E}
stands for \emph{ephemeral}. TLS~1.3 went further and made ephemeral
Diffie--Hellman the \emph{only} key-exchange mechanism, removing static RSA key
transport from the standard so that stealing a server's certificate key can
never decrypt archived traffic.
\end{applicationbox}

\subsection{A worked tiny example}

An example with small numbers illustrates the time ordering (Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-toy-pfs}).
Work in $\Zns{23}$, which has order $22$, with generator $g=5$. All congruences
below are modulo $23$.

\textbf{The session.} Alice chooses ephemeral $a=6$ and computes $A = 5^6 \bmod 23$:
\begin{align*}
  5^2 &= 25 \\
      &\equiv 2 \pmod{23}, \\
  5^3 &\equiv 2 \cdot 5 \\
      &= 10 \pmod{23}, \\
  A &= 5^6 \\
    &= (5^3)^2 \\
    &\equiv 10^2 \\
    &= 100 \\
    &\equiv 8 \pmod{23}.
\end{align*}
Bob chooses ephemeral $b=15$ and computes $B = 5^{15} \bmod 23$ by repeated squaring
($5^1 = 5$, $5^2 \equiv 2$, $5^4 \equiv 4$, $5^8 \equiv 16$):
\begin{align*}
  B &= 5^{15} \\
    &= 5^8 \cdot 5^4 \cdot 5^2 \cdot 5^1 \\
    &\equiv 16 \cdot 4 \cdot 2 \cdot 5 \pmod{23} \\
    &\equiv 18 \cdot 2 \cdot 5 \pmod{23} \\
    &\equiv 13 \cdot 5 \pmod{23} \\
    &= 65 \\
    &\equiv 19 \pmod{23}.
\end{align*}

\textbf{Shared secret and erasure.} Alice computes $B^a = 19^6 \bmod 23$ and Bob
computes $A^b = 8^{15} \bmod 23$; both equal $g^{ab} = 5^{90} \bmod 23$. Because
$g=5$ has order $22$ in $\Zns{23}$, $5^{22} \equiv 1 \pmod{23}$, and reducing
the exponent modulo $22$ ($90 = 4\cdot 22 + 2$) gives
\begin{align*}
  5^{90} &= (5^{22})^4 \cdot 5^2 \\
         &\equiv 1^4 \cdot 5^2 \\
         &\equiv 2 \pmod{23}.
\end{align*}
(Directly, $19 \equiv -4 \pmod{23}$, so $19^6 \equiv (-4)^6 = 4096 = 178\cdot 23 + 2 \equiv 2 \pmod{23}$.)
Both sides obtain the session secret $2$ and immediately erase $a=6$ and $b=15$.

\textbf{After the theft.} Suppose tomorrow Eve steals Bob's long-term signing
key $sk_B$. From the recorded wire she holds $A=8$, $B=19$, and $\sigma_B$. The
stolen $sk_B$ lets her forge new signatures, yet tells her nothing about the
deleted $a=6$ or $b=15$. Recovering $k=2$ requires solving $5^x \equiv 8 \pmod{23}$
or $5^y \equiv 19 \pmod{23}$ --- a discrete logarithm. With the toy modulus
$p=23$ brute force is trivial, whereas in a $2048$-bit prime-field subgroup or a
$256$-bit elliptic-curve group that search is infeasible. The stolen signing key
provides no shortcut to the archived session's key.

\section{From one group element to many keys: KDFs}

The handshake produces a single shared secret --- a group element such as our
$2$, or in practice a $256$-bit coordinate. Yet a session needs several
independent symmetric keys: one to encrypt client-to-server traffic, one for
server-to-client traffic, separate MAC keys or initialization vectors (IVs,
Chapter~13), and a key to confirm the handshake. You cannot slice the raw
Diffie--Hellman output into chunks and use them directly, for two reasons.

First, the raw shared secret is not a uniform random bitstring. A
Diffie--Hellman value $g^{ab}$ in $\Zns{p}$ comes from a structured algebraic
set: its integer encoding leaves gaps in the bitstring space, its bits are
correlated, and if incoming shares are not validated, $g^{ab}$ could even be
confined to a small subgroup (Chapter~5). Symmetric ciphers and MACs expect keys
indistinguishable from uniform bits, so we must distill that uneven entropy into
a uniform key.

Second, keys used for different roles require \emph{domain separation}: they
must be computationally independent so that a weakness or leak in one role
cannot compromise another. Reusing one key across two roles invites
cross-protocol bugs where messages from one role validate or decrypt in the
other (Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-domain-separation}).

Both jobs belong to a \emph{key derivation function} (KDF). The standard
construction, \emph{HMAC-based Key Derivation Function} (HKDF, RFC~5869), splits
derivation into two stages: \emph{extract-then-expand}.

\begin{definition}[HKDF, extract-then-expand]
\label{ch23:hkdf}
Built from $\mathrm{HMAC}$ (Chapter~15) over a hash function $\Hash$ of output
length $L$ bytes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Extract.} From raw, non-uniform input keying material
        $\mathrm{IKM}$ (here the encoded group element $g^{ab}$) and an optional
        non-secret byte string $\mathrm{salt}$ (defaulting to $L$ zero bytes if
        omitted), compute one $L$-byte pseudorandom key:
        \[
          \mathrm{PRK} = \mathrm{HMAC}(\mathrm{salt},\, \mathrm{IKM}).
        \]
        Acting as a randomness extractor (Chapter~17), $\mathrm{HMAC}$
        concentrates the min-entropy of $\mathrm{IKM}$ into a near-uniform key
        $\mathrm{PRK}$.
  \item \textbf{Expand.} From $\mathrm{PRK}$, a context label $\mathrm{info}$,
        and a requested output length $\ell$ bytes (with $N = \ceil{\ell/L} \le
        255$), generate $N$ blocks one $\mathrm{HMAC}$ call at a time:
        \[
          T_i = \mathrm{HMAC}\bigl(\mathrm{PRK},\, T_{i-1}\concat \mathrm{info}\concat i\bigr)
          \quad \text{for } i = 1, 2, \dots, N, \qquad T_0 = \varepsilon,
        \]
        where $\varepsilon$ is the empty byte string and the block counter $i$
        is encoded as a single byte; then take the first $\ell$ bytes of
        $T_1\concat T_2\concat\cdots\concat T_N$. Domain separation comes from
        $\mathrm{info}$: passing \texttt{"c hs traffic"} (client handshake
        traffic) for one key and \texttt{"s hs traffic"} (server handshake
        traffic) for another produces computationally independent keys.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{intuitionbox}
\emph{Extract} condenses the structured Diffie--Hellman shared element into one
uniform key $\mathrm{PRK}$. \emph{Expand} combines $\mathrm{PRK}$ with a
purpose label (\texttt{"c2s"}, \texttt{"s2c"}, \texttt{"fin"}) to produce an
independent key per label (Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-hkdf}). Learning one output key
reveals nothing about the others.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Why two stages instead of hashing the Diffie--Hellman output once? Chapters~7
and~17 separated an \emph{extractor} (distilling a biased source with sufficient
min-entropy into near-uniform bits, justified by the leftover hash lemma) from a
\emph{pseudorandom generator} (stretching a short uniform seed into a long
pseudorandom stream). Extract handles the non-uniform \emph{source}; Expand
handles the \emph{output length} and \emph{labels}. The security of Expand rests
on $\mathrm{HMAC}$ behaving as a pseudorandom function (PRF, Chapter~15) when
keyed by $\mathrm{PRK}$.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{implementbox}
\textbf{Toy HKDF over SHA-256 --- learning only.}
This code illustrates the extract-then-expand structure. It is a teaching model
without memory zeroization or constant-time guarantees. \textbf{Do not use this
in production --- call your platform's vetted HKDF implementation.}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
import hmac, hashlib

def _hmac(key, msg):
    return hmac.new(key, msg, hashlib.sha256).digest()

def hkdf_extract(salt, ikm):
    # salt acts as the HMAC key; if absent, use a zero block
    if not salt:
        salt = b"\x00" * hashlib.sha256().digest_size
    return _hmac(salt, ikm)            # PRK: one uniform pseudorandom key

def hkdf_expand(prk, info, length):
    out, t, counter = b"", b"", 1
    while len(out) < length:
        # T_i = HMAC(PRK, T_{i-1} || info || i)
        t = _hmac(prk, t + info + bytes([counter]))
        out += t
        counter += 1
    return out[:length]

def hkdf(salt, ikm, info, length):
    return hkdf_expand(hkdf_extract(salt, ikm), info, length)

# One shared DH secret -> several independent keys via different labels
shared = b"raw-diffie-hellman-output-not-uniform"
prk_salt = b"handshake transcript hash goes here"
enc_key = hkdf(prk_salt, shared, b"c2s enc", 32)   # client->server
dec_key = hkdf(prk_salt, shared, b"s2c enc", 32)   # server->client
mac_key = hkdf(prk_salt, shared, b"c2s mac", 32)
print(enc_key.hex())
print(dec_key.hex())     # unrelated to enc_key, by domain separation
\end{lstlisting}
The three keys come from one shared secret yet remain computationally
independent because each call supplies a distinct \texttt{info} label. In
TLS~1.3, the salt and info inputs incorporate running hashes of the handshake
transcript, binding every derived key to the messages exchanged so far.
\end{implementbox}

\section{Replay and downgrade}

Two active attacks bypass key secrecy and target the \emph{protocol logic}:
replaying old messages and downgrading algorithm negotiation.

\subsection{Replay}

A \emph{replay} attack records a valid protocol message and re-sends it later.
If Eve records Alice's encrypted ``transfer \$100 to Eve'' packet and transmits
ten copies, each copy is a valid ciphertext; encryption and a static MAC by
themselves do not tell the receiver it is a duplicate.

The defense in key exchange is \emph{freshness}: mix an unpredictable,
single-use value contributed by each party into every session so that a
recording from one connection cannot validate in another
(Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-replay-0rtt}, left). Ephemeral Diffie--Hellman builds
freshness into the key itself: when Eve replays Alice's captured messages to
Bob, Bob chooses a new ephemeral $b' \neq b$ and derives $g^{ab'} \neq g^{ab}$,
so ciphertexts from the recorded session fail authentication. Within an
established session, protocols attach a strictly increasing per-record
\emph{sequence counter} (used as the AEAD nonce, Chapter~16) so replaying a
packet inside the same connection is rejected as well.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The 0-RTT replay hole in TLS~1.3.}
TLS~1.3 offers an optional \emph{0-RTT} (zero round-trip time) resumption mode
in which a returning client sends application data in its opening message burst
(its first \emph{flight}), encrypted under a key $k_{\mathrm{psk}}$ derived from
a secret saved during an earlier connection. Saving a round trip comes with a
trade-off: because the server has not yet contributed a fresh ephemeral share or
nonce, Eve can capture that first flight and replay it to the server
(Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-replay-0rtt}, right). Standards therefore restrict 0-RTT
to \emph{idempotent} requests --- operations whose effect is unchanged if run
more than once, such as an HTTP \texttt{GET} fetching a static image, as opposed
to a non-idempotent HTTP \texttt{POST} that transfers money --- and servers keep
anti-replay caches of recently seen 0-RTT identifiers to reject duplicates within
a time window.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Downgrade}

A \emph{downgrade} attack tricks two endpoints that both support strong
cryptography into negotiating a weak algorithm instead. Real protocols support
multiple versions and cipher suites for backward compatibility, and negotiation
begins with the client listing its supported options in unencrypted
\emph{cleartext} before any shared key exists. Sitting on the wire, Eve edits
that list.

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Stripping strong cipher suites.}
Suppose Alice's opening handshake message (\texttt{ClientHello}) offers modern
\texttt{ECDHE} suites alongside a legacy ``export-grade'' $512$-bit
Diffie--Hellman suite --- a deliberately small key size created to comply with
1990s US export restrictions. Eve intercepts \texttt{ClientHello}, deletes the
strong suites, and forwards only the $512$-bit option. Bob sees only the weak
suite and selects it. Eve then computes the $512$-bit discrete logarithm (within
reach of precomputation on academic clusters) and reads the session. Neither
endpoint wanted $512$-bit cryptography, yet the negotiation itself was
unauthenticated. This mechanism powered the \textsc{FREAK} and \textsc{Logjam}
attacks on TLS in 2015, after which browsers and servers removed export-grade
suites altogether.
\end{attackbox}

The defense is \emph{transcript authentication} (Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-downgrade}):
at the close of the handshake, once the session keys have been derived, each
party computes a MAC over a hash of \emph{every handshake message it sent and
received}, and the peer verifies that MAC before exchanging application data.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why a final transcript MAC stops downgrade.}
When Eve edits the cipher-suite list, Alice's recorded transcript $\mathrm{tr}_A$
and Bob's recorded transcript $\mathrm{tr}_B$ disagree. Once a strong key $k$ is
derived, each side sends $\MAC_k(\Hash(\text{its transcript}))$ in a
\texttt{Finished} message. Because $\mathrm{tr}_A \neq \mathrm{tr}_B$, the MACs
do not match and the handshake aborts. Eve cannot forge a matching MAC unless
she has already broken the session key $k$ in real time during the handshake
itself (which is why weak export suites had to be retired alongside adding
transcript MACs). Negotiation can travel in cleartext at the start, provided the
entire transcript is ratified under the authenticated key before the session
opens.
\end{whybox}

\section{Putting it together: the shape of TLS 1.3 and Signal}

TLS~1.3 and Signal assemble these same components for two different settings:
TLS connects a client to an online server in a live round trip, whereas Signal
opens an encrypted conversation even when the recipient's device is offline.

\subsection{TLS 1.3 at a glance}

handshake between a client and a server holding long-term signing key pair
$(sk_S, pk_S)$.

Each defense from earlier sections has a specific job in Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-tls13}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The shares $A=g^a$ and $B=g^b$ are \emph{ephemeral} ECDH values, giving
        forward secrecy (Definition~\ref{ch23:pfs}).
  \item \texttt{CertificateVerify} signs the running transcript hash $\Hash(\mathrm{tr})$
        with $sk_S$, authenticating the server via signed ephemeral
        Diffie--Hellman (the certificate binds $pk_S$ to the server's domain
        name, Chapter~24).
  \item Handshake and application keys are derived from $g^{ab}$ via HKDF with
        the transcript hash mixed in, producing separate domain-separated keys
        for each direction and phase.
  \item The two \texttt{Finished} MACs authenticate the full transcript against
        downgrade and confirm both sides derived matching keys.
  \item Fresh random nonces in both \texttt{Hello} messages prevent replay.
\end{itemize}
TLS~1.3 achieved this compact structure by deleting older mechanisms that lacked
these guarantees, including static RSA key transport, weak export/CBC suites,
and mid-connection \emph{renegotiation} (a TLS~1.2 feature that re-ran a
handshake inside an active connection and enabled cross-handshake splicing bugs).

\subsection{Signal at a glance}

Signal addresses an asynchronous problem: Bob's phone may be \emph{offline} when
Alice sends her first message, so no live round trip is possible. Signal's
\emph{Extended Triple Diffie--Hellman} (X3DH) handshake lets Alice establish a
shared key using a bundle of public keys that Bob uploaded to a relay server in
advance (Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-x3dh}).

Write $\mathrm{DH}(x, Y) = Y^x$ for the Diffie--Hellman shared value computed
from one party's private key $x$ and another party's public share $Y = g^y$ (on
Signal's elliptic curve Curve25519, scalar multiplication $xY$). While online,
Bob publishes three kinds of public keys to the server:
\begin{itemize}
  \item his long-term \emph{identity key} $B_{\mathrm{id}} = g^{b_{\mathrm{id}}}$;
  \item a medium-term \emph{signed prekey} $B_{\mathrm{spk}} = g^{b_{\mathrm{spk}}}$,
        rotated periodically and accompanied by a signature under Bob's identity
        key;
  \item a batch of single-use \emph{one-time prekeys} $B_{\mathrm{otp}} = g^{b_{\mathrm{otp}}}$,
        each handed out to at most one initiator.
\end{itemize}
To start a chat, Alice fetches Bob's bundle, verifies the signature on
$B_{\mathrm{spk}}$, generates her own fresh ephemeral key pair
$(a_{\mathrm{eph}}, A_{\mathrm{eph}} = g^{a_{\mathrm{eph}}})$ alongside her
long-term identity key $(a_{\mathrm{id}}, A_{\mathrm{id}} = g^{a_{\mathrm{id}}})$,
and combines four Diffie--Hellman pairs:
\[
  k = \operatorname{KDF}\!\bigl(
    \mathrm{DH}(a_{\mathrm{id}},\, B_{\mathrm{spk}}) \concat
    \mathrm{DH}(a_{\mathrm{eph}},\, B_{\mathrm{id}}) \concat
    \mathrm{DH}(a_{\mathrm{eph}},\, B_{\mathrm{spk}}) \concat
    \mathrm{DH}(a_{\mathrm{eph}},\, B_{\mathrm{otp}})
  \bigr),
\]
where $\operatorname{KDF}$ is HKDF. Alice erases $a_{\mathrm{eph}}$ and sends
$(A_{\mathrm{id}}, A_{\mathrm{eph}})$ with her encrypted initial message. When
Bob comes back online, he computes the matching four values
$\mathrm{DH}(b_{\mathrm{spk}}, A_{\mathrm{id}})$,
$\mathrm{DH}(b_{\mathrm{id}}, A_{\mathrm{eph}})$,
$\mathrm{DH}(b_{\mathrm{spk}}, A_{\mathrm{eph}})$, and
$\mathrm{DH}(b_{\mathrm{otp}}, A_{\mathrm{eph}})$, derives $k$, and deletes the
spent private prekey $b_{\mathrm{otp}}$.

Each term in X3DH mirrors triple Diffie--Hellman: $\mathrm{DH}_1$ and
$\mathrm{DH}_2$ provide mutual implicit authentication; $\mathrm{DH}_3$ mixes
Alice's ephemeral with Bob's signed prekey for forward secrecy; and the
single-use prekey in $\mathrm{DH}_4$, deleted as soon as Bob processes the first
message, adds replay resistance and immediate forward secrecy even before Bob's
medium-term prekey rotates. From $k$, HKDF derives initial \emph{chain keys} ---
symmetric keys advanced through a KDF with each message --- and Signal's
\emph{double ratchet} (Chapter~27) then mixes in new ephemeral Diffie--Hellman
exchanges as the conversation continues, refreshing keys at per-message
granularity.

\begin{applicationbox}
TLS authenticates with a \emph{signature} over the transcript because an online
web server proves its identity to arbitrary browsers through a public
certificate chain. Signal authenticates \emph{implicitly} with long-term
Diffie--Hellman keys (plus one prekey signature) to support offline session
setup without leaving signed transcripts of conversations. WireGuard, a modern
virtual private network (VPN) protocol, occupies a third point in the same
design space: built on the \emph{Noise} protocol framework (a standardized
catalog of implicitly authenticated Diffie--Hellman handshakes), it uses static
and ephemeral Diffie--Hellman keys pre-shared between peers and omits
certificates altogether.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Summary of authenticated key exchange.}
A deployable key exchange pairs ephemeral Diffie--Hellman (for \emph{forward
secrecy}) with an \emph{authenticator} bound to the transcript (a digital
signature in TLS~1.3, or long-term Diffie--Hellman terms in Signal) to block
\emph{man-in-the-middle} impersonation. It incorporates fresh \emph{nonces} or
ephemeral shares against \emph{replay}, seals negotiation with a final
\emph{transcript MAC} against \emph{downgrade}, and expands the shared secret
into independent keys via \emph{HKDF}. Drop ephemeral erasure and forward
secrecy vanishes; drop transcript binding and authentication or downgrade
resistance breaks.
\end{focusbox}

Throughout this chapter, we assumed Alice already held an authentic copy of
Bob's public key $pk_B$. Chapter~24 examines how she obtains one at internet
scale: digital certificates and Public-Key Infrastructure (PKI).

\begin{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Explain why a server that caches one ``ephemeral'' DH value $g^b$ and

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Consider the signed handshake but with Bob signing only $B$, not $A\concat B$.
Give a concrete replay attack in which Eve causes Alice to accept a session using

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Prove a small lemma about HKDF's domain separation under the assumption that
HMAC keyed by $\mathrm{PRK}$ is a pseudorandom function. Argue that for distinct
labels $\mathrm{info}_1\neq\mathrm{info}_2$, an adversary given the expanded
output for $\mathrm{info}_1$ cannot distinguish the output for $\mathrm{info}_2$
from random. (Hint: reduce to the PRF security of HMAC; the labels become
distinct inputs.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
A protocol designer proposes to skip the Extract stage of HKDF and feed the raw
Diffie--Hellman element $g^{ab}\in\Zns{p}$ directly into Expand as the
$\mathrm{PRK}$. Give two distinct reasons this is dangerous, one about the
\emph{non-uniformity} of $g^{ab}$ and one about \emph{small-subgroup} structure
(what if $g^{ab}$ lands in a small subgroup?). Relate each to a property HKDF's
Extract is supposed to provide.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Reconstruct the \textsc{Logjam}-style downgrade at the protocol level. Alice
offers suites $\{\text{ECDHE-strong},\ \text{DHE-export-512}\}$; Eve wants the
export suite chosen. Write the message Eve forwards to Bob, the message Bob
returns, and then show where the final transcript-MAC check (if present)
would have caught her --- and why it does not if the handshake omits transcript
authentication.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
In TLS~1.3 0-RTT, explain why data in the client's first flight is replayable
while data sent \emph{after} the server's \texttt{ServerHello} is not. Identify the
specific ingredient (from this chapter) that the later data has and the 0-RTT
data lacks. Then classify which of these HTTP requests are safe to send as 0-RTT:
\texttt{GET /balance}, \texttt{POST /transfer}, \texttt{GET /logo.png},
\texttt{POST /search}.
\end{exercise}
