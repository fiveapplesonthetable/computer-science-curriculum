\chapter{Randomness and CSPRNGs}

In Chapter~16 we saw how a single repeated nonce destroys AES-GCM, which points to a question we have put off for sixteen chapters: where do fresh random bits come from? Every secret in this book starts as a coin flip: an AES key is $128$ coin flips, while a nonce, initialization vector, password salt, or \emph{ephemeral key} (a single-use key generated for one session and then discarded) demands bits no adversary can predict. Producing those bits on a deterministic machine is a genuine engineering problem whose failure means broken plaintexts and exposed keys.

A practical computer solves that problem in two stages: harvest a small amount of genuine physical noise from hardware, then feed that seed into a \emph{cryptographically secure pseudorandom number generator} (CSPRNG) that stretches it into all the keys and nonces applications need. When either stage fails -- a predictable seed, a backdoored generator, or a reused output -- even unbroken ciphers hand their secrets to an attacker.

\section{Why Cryptography Lives or Dies on Randomness}
\label{ch17:why-randomness}

Recall the one-time pad: it achieves perfect secrecy, but \emph{only} when the key is uniformly random and never reused. Without the randomness, the same algorithm gives no security at all. The same holds across cryptography. By Kerckhoffs's principle (\S\ref{ch01:kerckhoffs}), the algorithm is public; the only thing standing between Eve and the plaintext is a quantity she cannot guess. If she \emph{can} guess it -- because it came from a predictable source -- the public algorithm becomes a public decryption procedure that she runs as easily as Bob does.

\begin{intuitionbox}
A cipher is a lockbox whose combination is your key. Kerckhoffs's principle says the lockbox design is printed on the side; security rests entirely on the combination being unguessable. Now suppose the factory sets every combination by reading the wall clock at manufacturing time. The boxes are still ``random'' in the loose sense that you cannot easily predict any one of them in advance -- yet an attacker who knows the trick tries the few thousand plausible timestamps and opens them all. The combination space did not shrink because the math changed; it shrank because the \emph{source} of the combination had far less unpredictability than its length suggested. In cryptography what matters is how far an adversary can narrow the value down, not how long it is.
\end{intuitionbox}

The quantity that matters is \emph{how best-guess probability scales}, not how many bits long the value is. Recall min-entropy from Chapter~7: if a random variable $X$ takes each outcome $x$ with probability $p_x = \Pr[X = x]$, its min-entropy is
\[
  H_\infty(X) \;=\; -\log_2 \max_x p_x .
\]
Here $\max_x p_x$ picks out the \emph{most likely} outcome -- the attacker's best single guess. If the most likely outcome has probability $2^{-128}$, then $H_\infty(X) = 128$: no single guess succeeds with probability above $2^{-128}$, and finding $X$ takes roughly $2^{127}$ tries on average, so the value is worth its full length. If instead some outcome has probability $2^{-20}$ -- say a flawed generator only ever produces one of a million distinct keys -- then $H_\infty(X) = 20$ no matter how many bits the key occupies in memory. A ``$256$-bit key'' with $20$ bits of min-entropy offers only $20$ bits of real security.

\begin{focusbox}
Min-entropy, not Shannon entropy, is the right measure for cryptography. Shannon entropy $H(X) = -\sum_x p_x \log_2 p_x$ averages over all outcomes and answers ``how many bits to encode this on average.'' An attacker does not care about the average; she guesses the \emph{single most likely} value first. Picture a broken generator that outputs the $256$-bit all-zero string $0^{256}$ half the time and one of the remaining $2^{256} - 1$ strings uniformly at random the other half (Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-spiked-keyspace}). Its Shannon entropy is about $\tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac12 \cdot 257 = 129$ bits, which sounds ample for a $128$-bit key. Yet Eve guesses $0^{256}$ first and wins half the time. The min-entropy, $-\log_2(1/2) = 1$ bit, tells the truth. Always size a key by its worst case, which is min-entropy.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{walkbox}
Suppose a website generates ``random'' six-digit login codes by seeding a generator with the current Unix time in seconds (seconds elapsed since January~1, 1970) and reading one code out. Two users who request a code in the same second get correlated codes. Even ignoring that, how big is the real keyspace an attacker faces over a one-day window? (Hint: it is not $10^6$. Count seconds.) Keep the count in mind; it is the difference between $10^6$ guesses and $86{,}400$.
\end{walkbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Min-entropy, Shannon entropy, and collision entropy all belong to the R\'enyi entropy family $H_\alpha$ (Chapter~7). Min-entropy is $H_\infty$ (the $\alpha\to\infty$ limit), Shannon entropy is $H_1$, and \emph{collision entropy} $H_2(X) = -\log_2 \sum_x p_x^2$ measures the probability $\sum_x p_x^2$ that two independent draws match, governing birthday bounds. Because $H_\infty(X) \le H_2(X) \le H_1(X)$ for every distribution, sizing a key by min-entropy never overstates its security.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{True Randomness versus Pseudorandomness}
\label{ch17:true-vs-pseudo}

There are two fundamentally different ways to get bits that look random, and a real system uses them in series. Confusing the two is one of the most common conceptual errors in applied cryptography.

\textbf{True (physical) randomness} comes from a physical process believed to be genuinely unpredictable: thermal noise across a resistor (voltage fluctuations from heat-driven electron motion), jitter between independent clock oscillators (tiny timing drift between two crystal clocks), radioactive decay timings, exact arrival times of network packets and keystrokes, or shot noise in a reverse-biased diode (electrons randomly crossing a semiconductor barrier). Its unpredictability is a property of the physical world rather than any algorithm; no formula can reproduce it, because it was never computed. Physical sources, however, are slow, biased, and hard to verify. A noisy diode might yield only a few kilobits per second of raw bits, skewed toward $0$ or $1$, that must be cleaned up before use.

\textbf{Pseudorandomness} comes from a deterministic algorithm: feed in a short \emph{seed} and it stretches that seed into a long stream of bits that \emph{look} random to anyone who does not know the seed. Identical seeds always produce identical streams -- determinism brings speed and reproducibility, yet makes everything predictable if the seed leaks. A pseudorandom generator manufactures no new entropy. If the seed has $128$ bits of min-entropy, a gigabyte of output still has at most $128$ bits of min-entropy; the generator spreads that unpredictability across its output rather than creating more.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Think of true randomness as mining raw ore and pseudorandomness as stamping coins. The mine (your diode, your interrupt timings) produces a trickle of irregular, impure metal -- real value, slow to extract. The mint (your CSPRNG) takes a lump of that metal as a seed and stamps out millions of identical-looking coins fast and cheaply. The mint creates no new gold; it spreads the gold from the seed across all the coins. Spend the seed's gold twice -- reuse or leak the seed -- and every coin stamped from it is compromised. A real system uses both: gather enough true entropy \emph{once} to seed the mint, then let the mint do the bulk production.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{formalbox}
Recall from Chapter~10 that a \emph{pseudorandom generator} (PRG) is a deterministic, efficiently computable function
\[
  G:\{0,1\}^n \;\to\; \{0,1\}^{\ell(n)}, \qquad \ell(n) > n,
\]
mapping an $n$-bit seed in $\{0,1\}^n$ to a longer $\ell(n)$-bit string. Its output ranges over at most $2^n$ of the $2^{\ell(n)}$ possible $\ell(n)$-bit strings, a tiny fraction when $\ell(n) \gg n$, yet must look indistinguishable from the uniform distribution on $\{0,1\}^{\ell(n)}$ to every efficient observer.
\end{formalbox}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{``It passes the statistical tests, so it is secure.''} Statistical test batteries (Diehard, TestU01, and the National Institute of Standards and Technology suite NIST SP 800-22) check whether output is distinguishable from coin flips by fixed surface statistics such as bit bias, run lengths, and short-range correlations. Passing them is necessary, never sufficient, for cryptography. A \emph{linear congruential generator} (LCG), which updates an integer state $x_i$ via $x_{i+1} = (a x_i + c) \bmod m$ for multiplier $a$, increment $c$, and modulus $m$, can pass many statistical checks, yet a handful of consecutive outputs reveal $a$, $c$, and every future and past value by modular algebra. The \emph{Mersenne Twister} -- a fast non-cryptographic generator with a $624$-word state, default in Python's \texttt{random} module and many other languages -- passes standard statistical suites and remains completely predictable: observing $624$ consecutive $32$-bit outputs lets an attacker reconstruct its internal state and clone the stream. Statistical randomness tests the output in isolation; cryptographic randomness tests the output against an adversary who knows the generator's code.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{example}[Breaking an LCG from three outputs]
\label{ch17:lcg-break}
Take an LCG $x_{i+1} = (a x_i + c) \bmod m$ with known modulus $m = 2^{31}$ and unknown multiplier $a$ and increment $c$. Suppose we observe three consecutive outputs $x_0 = 5$, $x_1 = 33$, and $x_2 = 117$. Two unknowns require two transition congruences:
\[
  33 \equiv 5a + c \pmod{2^{31}}, \qquad 117 \equiv 33a + c \pmod{2^{31}}.
\]
Subtract the first congruence from the second to eliminate $c$, then solve for $a$:
\begin{align*}
  117 - 33 &\equiv (33 - 5)\,a \pmod{2^{31}} && \text{(subtract to cancel $c$)} \\
  84 &\equiv 28\,a \pmod{2^{31}} && \text{($84 = 4 \cdot 21$, $28 = 4 \cdot 7$)} \\
  21 &\equiv 7\,a \pmod{2^{29}} && \text{(divide modulus and both sides by $\gcd(4, 2^{31}) = 4$).}
\end{align*}
Because $7$ is odd, $\gcd(7, 2^{29}) = 1$, so $7$ has a unique inverse modulo $2^{29}$ (namely $7^{-1} \equiv 383{,}479{,}223 \pmod{2^{29}}$). Multiplying $7a \equiv 7 \cdot 3 \pmod{2^{29}}$ by $7^{-1}$ yields
\[
  a \equiv 3 \pmod{2^{29}}.
\]
Modulo $2^{31}$, this leaves four candidate values $a = 3 + j \cdot 2^{29}$ for $j \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$, each paired with $c = (33 - 5a) \bmod 2^{31}$. All four candidates generate the exact same output sequence: every $x_i$ satisfies $x_i \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, so $x_i - 5$ is a multiple of $4$ and the extra term $j \cdot 2^{29}(x_i - 5)$ vanishes modulo $2^{31}$. Choosing $j = 0$ gives $a = 3$, $c = 33 - 5 \cdot 3 = 18$, and the next output
\[
  x_3 \;=\; (3 \cdot 117 + 18) \bmod 2^{31} \;=\; 369.
\]
Three observed outputs and a few modular steps expose the entire future and past stream. The single affine update $x \mapsto ax + c$ that makes an LCG fast also lets two equations recover its parameters.
\end{example}

\section{What Makes a Generator \emph{Cryptographically} Secure}
\label{ch17:csprng-def}

A simulation generator (or \emph{pseudorandom number generator}, PRNG) only needs output unbiased and uncorrelated enough for numerical estimates to converge. A CSPRNG -- called a \emph{deterministic random bit generator} (DRBG) in NIST standards -- must defeat an adversary who knows the algorithm, sees part of the output, and tries to compute the rest. Chapter~10 formalized baseline PRG security in two ways; we recall both here before adding the state-compromise requirements that a long-running generator needs.

\begin{definition}[Next-bit unpredictability]
\label{ch17:def-nextbit}
Let $G : \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}^{\ell(n)}$ map a uniform $n$-bit seed $s \getsr \{0,1\}^n$ to output bits $(b_1, b_2, \dots, b_{\ell(n)}) = G(s)$. The generator $G$ is \emph{next-bit unpredictable} if no probabilistic polynomial-time ($\PPT$) algorithm $A$, given the first $i$ output bits, guesses bit $b_{i+1}$ with probability noticeably above $1/2$: for every $\PPT$ predictor $A$ and every $i \in \{0, 1, \dots, \ell(n)-1\}$,
\[
  \Pr_{s \getsr \{0,1\}^n}\!\bigl[A(b_1, b_2, \dots, b_i) = b_{i+1}\bigr] \;\le\; \tfrac12 + \negl(n),
\]
where $\negl(n)$ is a negligible function of the seed length $n$ (Chapter~8).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Indistinguishability / CSPRNG]
\label{ch17:def-indist}
A PRG $G : \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}^{\ell(n)}$ with output length $\ell = \ell(n) > n$ is a \emph{CSPRNG} if its output on a uniform seed $s \getsr \{0,1\}^n$ is computationally indistinguishable from a uniform string $r \getsr \{0,1\}^{\ell}$: for every $\PPT$ distinguisher $D$,
\[
  \abs[\Big]{\,\Pr_{s\getsr\{0,1\}^n}\!\big[D(G(s))=1\big] \;-\; \Pr_{r\getsr\{0,1\}^{\ell}}\!\big[D(r)=1\big]\,} \;\le\; \negl(n).
\]
Handed either $G(s)$ or $\ell$ independent coin flips, no efficient algorithm $D$ can tell which it received better than guessing.
\end{definition}

As Chapter~10 proved for stream-cipher PRGs, these two definitions are equivalent.

\begin{theorem}[Yao, 1982]
\label{ch17:thm-yao}
A PRG is next-bit unpredictable if and only if it is indistinguishable from uniform.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why next-bit unpredictability gives indistinguishability.} Recall the hybrid argument of Chapter~10 (\S\ref{ch10:next-bit}): suppose a distinguisher $D$ tells $G(s)$ from uniform with advantage $\eps$ (flipping $D$'s output bit if needed so $D$ outputs $1$, ``real,'' more often on $G(s)$ than on uniform strings). Step across the $\ell + 1$ hybrid distributions that take their first $i$ bits from $G(s)$ and their remaining $\ell - i$ bits uniformly at random, from $i = 0$ (all uniform) to $i = \ell$ (all $G(s)$). Because $D$'s acceptance probability rises by $\eps$ across $\ell$ steps, at some position $i$ switching bit $i$ from random to $b_i$ increases $D$'s probability of outputting $1$ by at least $\eps/\ell$. A predictor given $(b_1, \dots, b_{i-1})$ exploits that jump by guessing a random bit $g$, filling bits $i+1, \dots, \ell$ with coin flips, and running $D$: outputting $g$ when $D$ says $1$ and $1 - g$ when $D$ says $0$ predicts $b_i$ with probability at least $\tfrac12 + \eps/\ell$. Any global distinguishing edge converts into a local next-bit prediction, and vice versa.
\end{whybox}

Unlike a one-shot stream cipher, an operating-system CSPRNG holds internal state in memory for hours or months, and an attacker may eventually read that state through a memory bug or snapshot. Two state-compromise properties, traced in Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-ratchet}, protect outputs around a leak:

\begin{description}
  \item[Backtracking resistance (forward secrecy).] An adversary who learns the generator's internal state at step $t$ \emph{cannot} recover bits output \emph{before} $t$. Updating the state through a one-way function prevents inverting from $s_t$ back to $s_{t-1}$, keeping earlier keys safe.
  \item[Prediction resistance / recovery (backward secrecy).] After a state leak, \emph{reseeding} with fresh physical entropy makes future output unpredictable again, locking out the attacker who captured the old state. Without reseeding, a state leak is permanent because a deterministic generator with known state has no remaining secrets.
\end{description}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Backtracking resistance is a one-way ratchet on the state: each step grinds the old state through a non-invertible update, so capturing today's state reveals nothing about yesterday's keys. Prediction resistance is a re-locking mechanism: stir in fresh physical entropy and an attacker holding a snapshot of the old state can no longer compute subsequent states. A production CSPRNG provides both.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{example}[A CSPRNG you already have: AES in counter mode]
\label{ch17:ctr-prg}
Any secure block cipher turns into a CSPRNG. Take a uniform $128$-bit key $k \getsr \{0,1\}^{128}$ as the seed and encrypt successive $128$-bit counter blocks $0, 1, 2, \dots$:
\[
  G(k) \;=\; \Enc_k(0)\,\concat\,\Enc_k(1)\,\concat\,\Enc_k(2)\,\concat\cdots
\]
If AES is a secure pseudorandom permutation (Chapter~12), its outputs on distinct counter blocks are computationally indistinguishable from independent uniform blocks (so long as far fewer than $2^{64}$ blocks are drawn under one key, the birthday bound of Chapter~13), making $G$ a CSPRNG. This is the core of \texttt{CTR\_DRBG} in NIST SP 800-90A, one of the most widely deployed CSPRNG designs: after each request it generates a fresh key $k'$ from cipher output and overwrites the old key $k$, providing backtracking resistance.
\end{example}

\section{Where Entropy Actually Comes From}
\label{ch17:entropy-sources}

A CSPRNG is a deterministic mint; it needs a seed of real, physical, unpredictable bits to stamp. Where does a computer -- a machine engineered to be perfectly deterministic and repeatable -- find unpredictability? It collects it from the few places where physical processes affect the machine's behavior.

\begin{description}
  \item[Hardware noise sources.] Modern CPUs include a dedicated \emph{random number generator} (RNG) circuit. Intel's \texttt{RDRAND}/\texttt{RDSEED} instructions and AMD's equivalents sample thermal noise in a \emph{metastable latch} (a circuit balanced between $0$ and $1$ until thermal fluctuations tip it to one side). Hardware instructions are the fastest physical entropy source on a server, though because the circuit sits inside opaque silicon that software cannot inspect, operating systems mix \texttt{RDSEED} with other sources rather than trusting it alone (\S\ref{ch17:dualec}).
  \item[Interrupt and device timing.] At each \emph{hardware interrupt} -- a signal from a device requesting CPU attention as disk reads complete, network packets arrive, keys are pressed, or a mouse moves -- the operating system reads the CPU's high-frequency \emph{cycle counter} (which ticks billions of times per second). The low bits of those timestamps reflect chaotic physical variation (air turbulence in drives, oscillator drift, human muscle jitter) and remain unpredictable even to other processes on the machine.
  \item[Uninitialized and chaotic state.] Older systems sometimes read \emph{uninitialized memory} (RAM cells read before being written) or thread-scheduling races. Both are weak and discouraged: right after boot they can repeat from run to run (and relying on uninitialized reads triggered the Debian failure of \S\ref{ch17:debian}).
\end{description}

Raw bits from any physical source are \emph{biased} and \emph{correlated} -- a diode may emit $0$ sixty percent of the time, and consecutive interrupt timestamps share their upper bits. Using them directly as a key would leak structure. Instead, the system passes them through an \emph{entropy extractor} (in practice a cryptographic hash or HMAC) that compresses many biased input bits into a shorter, nearly uniform seed.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why hashing concentrates entropy.} Suppose you collect $1000$ raw bits from a biased, correlated source whose joint distribution still has at least $256$ bits of min-entropy -- meaning no single $1000$-bit pattern occurs with probability above $2^{-256}$. Hash those $1000$ bits with SHA-256. A hash that behaves like a random oracle (Chapter~14) maps distinct inputs independently across $\{0,1\}^{256}$, stirring lumpy input unpredictability into a nearly uniform $256$-bit digest; without any random-oracle assumption, the \emph{leftover hash lemma} below gives a provable bound using universal hashing at the cost of a small output-length reduction. Either way, the hard step is \emph{estimating} how much min-entropy the raw samples carry: if you credit the source with $256$ bits when it holds only $40$, the SHA-256 digest looks uniform to every statistical test yet takes only $2^{40}$ guesses to brute-force from the seed side.
\end{whybox}

\begin{connectionbox}
The \emph{leftover hash lemma} makes extraction unconditional. Let $X$ be a source with min-entropy $H_\infty(X) \ge k$, and choose $H$ uniformly at random from a \emph{universal hash family} with $m$-bit outputs -- a set of keyed functions such that any two distinct inputs $x \ne y$ collide, $\Pr_H[H(x) = H(y)] \le 2^{-m}$. Whenever
\[
  m \;\le\; k - 2\log_2\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\eps}\Bigr),
\]
the pair $(H, H(X))$ has statistical distance at most $\eps$ from uniform (even to an attacker who knows which hash function $H$ was chosen). The $2\log_2(1/\eps)$ deduction is the ``entropy tax'' paid for an unconditional guarantee, derived in the companion probability book from collision entropy $H_2$.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{example}[Estimating min-entropy from a biased coin]
\label{ch17:biased-coin}
Suppose your entropy source flips an independent biased coin that lands heads ($1$) with probability $p = 0.6$ and tails ($0$) with probability $0.4$. How many flips are needed for $128$ bits of min-entropy? The per-flip min-entropy is set by the more likely outcome:
\[
  H_\infty(\text{one flip}) \;=\; -\log_2(0.6) \;\approx\; 0.737 \text{ bits}.
\]
Across $n$ independent flips, min-entropy adds to $0.737\,n$ bits, so reaching $128$ bits requires
\[
  n \;\ge\; \frac{128}{0.737} \;\approx\; 174 \text{ flips}.
\]
You need $174$ flips rather than $128$ even though each flip produces one bit -- bias costs entropy, and as Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-biased-entropy} shows, min-entropy drops far more steeply with bias than Shannon entropy ($H(0.6) \approx 0.971$ bits). Assuming ``$128$ flips give $128$ bits'' leaves only $128 \times 0.737 \approx 94$ bits of strength, shrinking a $2^{128}$ search to $2^{94}$. Real physical sources also exhibit correlations between adjacent samples, requiring an additional safety discount.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Sizing an extractor with the leftover-hash tax]
\label{ch17:extractor-tax}
Now pass those biased flips through a universal-hash extractor and track the bit budget (Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-extractor-budget}). From Example~\ref{ch17:biased-coin}, $174$ flips of the $p=0.6$ coin supply $k \approx 128$ bits of min-entropy. For a target statistical distance $\eps = 2^{-64}$ from uniform, the leftover hash lemma caps the provably uniform output length at
\[
  m \;\le\; k - 2\log_2\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\eps}\Bigr) \;=\; 128 - 2 \cdot 64 \;=\; 0 \text{ bits}.
\]
At $\eps = 2^{-64}$ the $128$-bit safety margin consumes the entire $128$-bit budget. Extracting a $128$-bit key at this margin requires $k \ge m + 2\log_2(1/\eps) = 128 + 128 = 256$ bits of input min-entropy, which takes
\[
  n \;\ge\; \frac{256}{0.737} \;\approx\; 348 \text{ flips}.
\]
Provable extraction of a $128$-bit key from this coin therefore needs $348$ flips rather than $174$. Operating systems often model SHA-256 or BLAKE2s as a random oracle to avoid paying the full $2\log_2(1/\eps)$ universal-hash tax, yet still harvest conservative margins (for instance $256$ bits of estimated entropy before seeding) to absorb estimation error.
\end{example}

\section{Seeding, Reseeding, and the OS CSPRNG}
\label{ch17:seeding}

Put the pieces together and you get the architecture every modern operating system uses (Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-arch}). Physical entropy collection is solved in one place by the \emph{kernel} (the privileged core of the operating system), which feeds a fast CSPRNG that applications share:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Collect.} The kernel continuously harvests raw bits from interrupt timings, hardware RNG instructions, and device events into an \emph{entropy pool}, tracking a conservative estimate of how much min-entropy the pool holds.
  \item \textbf{Seed.} Once estimated min-entropy crosses an initialization threshold (on Linux, $256$ bits), the kernel hashes the pool to seed its CSPRNG -- on modern Linux, a ChaCha20-based generator.
  \item \textbf{Stretch.} Applications call \texttt{getrandom()}, read \texttt{/dev/urandom}, or call \texttt{BCryptGenRandom}, and the CSPRNG produces as many bytes as requested at memory speed.
  \item \textbf{Reseed.} The kernel periodically folds fresh harvested noise into the CSPRNG state, restoring security (prediction resistance) if an earlier state ever leaked.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Which interface to call.} On Linux and modern Unix, call \texttt{getrandom()} (or read \texttt{/dev/urandom} once initialized); in Python use \texttt{secrets} or \texttt{os.urandom}; in Go use \texttt{crypto/rand}; in Java use \texttt{SecureRandom}; in browsers use \texttt{crypto.getRandomValues}; on Windows use \texttt{BCryptGenRandom}. All of these route to the OS CSPRNG. Never build your own generator or seed one yourself -- the kernel already handles entropy collection, reseeding, and fork safety. One boot-time detail matters: before the pool reaches its initial threshold, \texttt{/dev/urandom} historically returned low-entropy bytes without warning, whereas \texttt{getrandom()} with default flags \emph{blocks} until the pool is seeded. Prefer \texttt{getrandom()}.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{\texttt{/dev/random} versus \texttt{/dev/urandom}: the myth.} Old advice claimed \texttt{/dev/random} was ``more secure'' because it blocked whenever an ``entropy counter'' ran low. On modern Linux that model has been retired. Once a CSPRNG is seeded with $256$ bits of min-entropy, it generates cryptographically strong output indefinitely -- reading bytes does not ``drain'' computational security the way a one-time pad consumes key bits. The legacy blocking behavior of \texttt{/dev/random} stalled servers during TLS handshakes and key generation without adding security. You need a strong seed \emph{once} at boot; after that, draw freely from \texttt{getrandom()} or \texttt{/dev/urandom}.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Fork Safety: When One Becomes Two}
\label{ch17:fork}

A CSPRNG is deterministic: identical states emit identical bytes. On Unix-like operating systems, the \texttt{fork()} system call spawns a child process by cloning the parent process's memory byte for byte. If a program maintains a \emph{userspace} CSPRNG -- generator state stored in the program's own memory rather than inside the kernel, seeded once at startup -- and then calls \texttt{fork()}, parent and child inherit \emph{identical} generator state (left half of Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-fork-safety}). Both processes then step through the exact same ``random'' stream -- generating identical session keys and identical nonces -- without knowing the other exists.

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Fork-induced nonce reuse.} Picture a web server that seeds an in-process CSPRNG at startup, then forks worker processes to handle connections. Every worker inherits the identical seed. Worker A and worker B each generate what they think is a fresh random nonce for AES-GCM -- and produce the \emph{same} nonce for different messages under the same key. As Chapter~16 showed, a repeated GCM nonce leaks the XOR of the two plaintexts and exposes the GHASH authentication key, allowing tag forgeries. One unguarded fork breaks a sound AEAD cipher, and the same bug has repeatedly caused duplicate signature nonces (\S\ref{ch17:nonce-reuse}).
\end{attackbox}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why the OS interface is fork-safe and a userspace cache is not.} \texttt{getrandom()} and \texttt{/dev/urandom} query the kernel, which holds \emph{one} CSPRNG state outside process memory and serializes reads, so parent and child receive distinct bytes after a fork (right half of Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-fork-safety}). A userspace library that caches generator state in process memory forfeits that guarantee. Three defenses exist:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Call the OS interface directly on each request (simplest and recommended).
  \item Record the \emph{process ID} (PID) at seed time, compare against \texttt{getpid()} on each draw, and reseed on mismatch -- fragile on its own because finite PIDs eventually wrap around and get reused by later descendants.
  \item On Linux, mark the state buffer with \texttt{MADV\_WIPEONFORK} so the kernel zeroes the child's copy during \texttt{fork()}, forcing a fresh reseed before output. OpenSSL combines this with fork-generation counters in its userspace DRBG.
\end{enumerate}
\end{whybox}

\begin{implementbox}
A toy showing the two failures and how the OS interface dodges them. \textbf{Learning only -- never ship a hand-rolled generator; call your platform CSPRNG.}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
import os, time, hashlib

# --- TOY userspace CSPRNG (insecure illustration) -------------------
class ToyDRBG:
    def __init__(self, seed: bytes):
        self.state = hashlib.sha256(seed).digest()
    def random_bytes(self, n: int) -> bytes:
        out = b""
        while len(out) < n:
            # one-way step: hashing gives backtracking resistance
            self.state = hashlib.sha256(b"S" + self.state).digest()
            out += hashlib.sha256(b"O" + self.state).digest()
        return out[:n]

# PITFALL 1: seeding from a predictable source (the clock).
bad = ToyDRBG(seed=str(int(time.time())).encode())   # ~seconds of entropy
print("clock-seeded (guessable):", bad.random_bytes(8).hex())

# PITFALL 2: fork inherits identical state -> identical output.
g = ToyDRBG(seed=os.urandom(32))      # good seed, but cached in memory
pid = os.fork()
if pid == 0:                          # child
    print("child :", g.random_bytes(8).hex())
    os._exit(0)
else:                                 # parent
    os.wait()
    print("parent:", g.random_bytes(8).hex())  # SAME as child -- bug

# THE FIX: ask the OS every time. Different in parent and child,
# correctly seeded at boot, no state to clone.
print("os.urandom:", os.urandom(8).hex())
\end{lstlisting}
Run it: the two \texttt{ToyDRBG} lines after the fork print \emph{identical} hex (shared cloned state), while \texttt{os.urandom} differs. The clock-seeded line is reproducible by anyone who guesses the second you ran it. Both bugs vanish the moment you defer to \texttt{os.urandom} / \texttt{getrandom()}.
\end{implementbox}

\subsection{Health Tests: Catching a Source That Dies}
\label{ch17:health}

A physical entropy source is hardware, and hardware fails: a noise diode degrades, a \emph{ring oscillator} (a loop of logic inverters whose timing jitter is sampled for noise) locks onto a periodic cycle, or \texttt{RDSEED} returns all-ones when a voltage rail droops. A failed source often keeps emitting bits -- predictable ones. Downstream, a hash and CSPRNG turn even all-ones input into balanced-looking key material with no complaint. Real entropy pipelines therefore run \emph{online health tests} directly on the raw samples, before extraction, to catch a dying source (Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-health-placement}):

\begin{description}
  \item[Repetition Count Test.] Raise an alarm if the same raw sample value repeats $C$ times in a row, catching stuck hardware. NIST SP 800-90B sets $C = 1 + \ceil{20 / H}$ from the source's claimed per-sample min-entropy $H$ (for a false-alarm probability near $2^{-20}$); a source claiming $H = 1$ bit per sample trips on $21$ identical samples.
  \item[Adaptive Proportion Test.] Across a sliding window (such as $512$ or $1024$ raw samples), count how often the window's first sample value recurs. If that count exceeds a threshold set from the binomial distribution (the count distribution of independent trials), the source has drifted toward heavy bias even if it is not completely stuck.
\end{description}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why test the raw source and not the CSPRNG output.} A CSPRNG is built to emit uniform-looking bits from any seed, including a predictable seed from a dead diode. Statistical suites run on CSPRNG output therefore test the hash or cipher rather than the physical noise source. Hardware degradation is visible only \emph{upstream}, on the raw samples before extraction removes their surface bias (Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-health-placement}).
\end{whybox}

\section{Famous RNG Failures}
\label{ch17:failures}

The ideas of min-entropy and reseeding are easier to appreciate after seeing real keys recovered from broken generators. We look at three cases, each with a distinct lesson: a bug that shrank the keyspace, a backdoor baked into a standard, and a misuse that leaks the secret key directly.

\subsection{Debian OpenSSL (2008): The 15-Bit Keyspace}
\label{ch17:debian}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{What happened.} In 2006 a Debian maintainer, silencing warnings from the Valgrind and Purify memory debuggers (tools that flag reads of memory locations that have not yet been written), commented out two lines in OpenSSL's seeding code that mixed uninitialized buffer bytes into its entropy pool -- including the buffer through which OpenSSL also passed its \texttt{/dev/urandom} bytes. That edit stripped nearly all physical entropy from OpenSSL on Debian and its derivatives (such as Ubuntu) for almost two years, leaving the process ID as the only varying seed input.

\textbf{The damage.} Default Linux PIDs ranged from $1$ to $32{,}768$ ($2^{15}$ values). Every key OpenSSL generated on an affected machine -- SSH, TLS, and virtual private network (OpenVPN) keys, as well as RSA and DSA/ECDSA public--private key pairs (Part~III) -- came from roughly $2^{15}$ possible seeds per architecture and key size. Researchers precomputed the entire table of candidate keys: given a server's public key, an attacker looked it up in the $32{,}768$-entry list and read off the matching private key without breaking any number theory. Worse, any DSA or ECDSA signature created on an affected system leaked the signer's long-term private key because its per-signature nonce came from the same tiny pool (\S\ref{ch17:nonce-reuse}). Millions of keys worldwide had to be revoked and replaced.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Nothing was wrong with the underlying ciphers: RSA and AES remained unbroken. The keys collapsed because the seed carried about $15$ bits of min-entropy instead of $128$ or more. To an attacker holding the precomputed table, a $2048$-bit RSA key generated from a $15$-bit seed is a $15$-bit key. Your cryptography is no stronger than the min-entropy of its seed.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The meta-lesson: do not silence a warning by gutting security.} Reading uninitialized memory is ordinarily a bug, so the patch looked like routine cleanup. Two engineering rules prevent repeats: subtle security-critical code needs comments explaining \emph{why} unusual lines exist, and changes to cryptographic libraries require review by someone who knows how the entropy pipeline works, not only whether the C code compiles cleanly.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Dual\_EC\_DRBG (c.\ 2006--2013): A Standardized Backdoor}
\label{ch17:dualec}

\begin{historybox}
\texttt{Dual\_EC\_DRBG} was one of four DRBGs standardized in NIST SP 800-90A (2006), alongside \texttt{Hash\_DRBG}, \texttt{HMAC\_DRBG}, and \texttt{CTR\_DRBG}. Recall from Chapter~5 that points on an elliptic curve over a finite field $\F_p$ form a cyclic group of prime order $q$, where \emph{scalar multiplication} $s \cdot P = P + P + \dots + P$ ($s$ summands) is fast to compute forward while recovering $s$ from $s \cdot P$ (the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm, Chapter~21) is infeasible. \texttt{Dual\_EC\_DRBG} stepped its state using two fixed public curve points $P$ and $Q$. From the start it stood out: it ran orders of magnitude slower than AES- or hash-based generators and had a measurable statistical bias. At Crypto~2007, Dan Shumow and Niels Ferguson showed that whoever generated the standard constants $P$ and $Q$ could know a secret integer $d$ satisfying $Q = d \cdot P$ that recovers the internal state from a single observed output. In 2013, disclosed documents indicated that the U.S.\ National Security Agency (NSA) had championed the design and paid RSA Security \$10 million to make it the default generator in its BSAFE library. NIST removed \texttt{Dual\_EC\_DRBG} from SP 800-90A in 2014.
\end{historybox}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{How the backdoor works.} At step $t$, the generator holds a secret integer state $s_t$, updates to the next state $s_{t+1} = x(s_t \cdot P)$ (the $x$-coordinate of the curve point $s_t \cdot P$), and outputs $r_t$, formed by dropping the top $16$ bits of $x(s_t \cdot Q)$. To an ordinary observer who knows only the points $P$ and $Q$, recovering $s_t$ from $s_t \cdot Q$ requires solving an elliptic-curve discrete logarithm. Suppose, however, the designer chose $P$ and $Q$ by picking a secret scalar $d \in \{1, \dots, q-1\}$ and setting $Q = d \cdot P$. Given one output block $r_t$, the holder of $d$ tries all $2^{16}$ possibilities for the dropped top bits to get candidate $x$-coordinates, plugs each into the curve equation $y^2 \equiv x^3 + ax + b \pmod p$ to recover the few valid points $s_t \cdot Q$, and multiplies by $d^{-1} \bmod q$ (the modular inverse of $d$ modulo the group order $q$):
\[
  d^{-1}\,(s_t \cdot Q) \;=\; d^{-1}\,(s_t d \cdot P) \;=\; s_t \cdot P.
\]
Taking the $x$-coordinate of $s_t \cdot P$ hands the attacker $s_{t+1}$ directly, exposing every subsequent key and nonce the generator produces.
\end{attackbox}

Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-dualec} traces the asymmetry: normal operation advances along the top row from $s_t$ through $s_t \cdot P$ to $s_{t+1}$, while anyone holding the trapdoor $d^{-1} \bmod q$ lifts one published output $r_t$ back up to $s_t \cdot P$ and computes $s_{t+1}$ alongside the victim.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why this case is the most troubling.} Debian was a bug -- a mistake anyone could have made and anyone could find by reading the diff. \texttt{Dual\_EC} was, by all available evidence, a deliberate, \emph{mathematically invisible} backdoor in a published international standard, exploitable only by whoever generated the constants $P$ and $Q$. To everyone else it looked like an ordinary (if slow) CSPRNG; you could audit the algorithm line by line and find nothing, because the secret was not in the code but in the \emph{provenance of two numbers}. This is the origin of the modern demand for \emph{nothing-up-my-sleeve} constants: cryptographic parameters must be derived from public, arbitrary values (digits of $\pi$, hashes of fixed strings) so that no designer could have chosen them to hide a trapdoor. When you cannot explain \emph{where a constant came from}, treat it as hostile.
\end{whybox}

\begin{focusbox}
The practical takeaway is not to avoid elliptic curves -- ECC is excellent and Chapter~21 is devoted to it. Instead, \emph{prefer simple, fast, auditable generators with transparently derived constants}, and be suspicious of any primitive that is mysteriously slow, mysteriously biased, or mysteriously favored. CTR\_DRBG (AES) and the ChaCha20-based and HMAC\_DRBG constructions show none of these warning signs; use them.
\end{focusbox}

\subsection{Repeated Nonces in Digital Signatures: The Secret Key Falls Out}
\label{ch17:nonce-reuse}

The previous two failures weakened a generator. This third case shows what a weak generator \emph{does} to a public-key protocol. Digital signature schemes such as the Digital Signature Algorithm (DSA) and its elliptic-curve counterpart ECDSA (developed in Chapter~22) require a fresh secret random value, the \emph{nonce} $k$, for every signature. Reuse $k$ once, and anyone who sees the two public signatures recovers the signer's long-term private key using only the modular arithmetic of Chapters~3 and~4.

To sign a message hash $h$ modulo a prime $n$ under a long-term private key $x \in \{1, \dots, n-1\}$, the signer chooses a per-signature secret nonce $k \in \{1, \dots, n-1\}$, computes a public value $r \in \{1, \dots, n-1\}$ deterministically from $k$ alone (for instance $r = (g^k \bmod p) \bmod n$ in DSA), and evaluates
\[
  s \equiv k^{-1}\,(h + x\,r) \pmod n.
\]
The signature is the pair $(r, s)$, and $r$, $s$, and $h$ are all public. Both the private key $x$ and the nonce $k$ must remain secret, and $k$ must never repeat.

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Two signatures, one nonce, and the private key falls out.} Suppose Alice signs two distinct messages with hashes $h_1 \not\equiv h_2 \pmod n$ while reusing the \emph{same} secret nonce $k$ -- because of a broken RNG, a forked worker process, or a hardcoded constant. Because $r$ is a deterministic function of $k$, both signatures carry the exact same $r$, advertising the mistake in public. Write out the two signature congruences:
\[
  s_1 \equiv k^{-1}(h_1 + x r) \pmod n, \qquad s_2 \equiv k^{-1}(h_2 + x r) \pmod n.
\]
Subtracting the second from the first cancels $x r$ and gives $s_1 - s_2 \equiv k^{-1}(h_1 - h_2) \pmod n$. Because $h_1 \not\equiv h_2 \pmod n$ and $n$ is prime, $s_1 - s_2 \not\equiv 0 \pmod n$ has a modular inverse $(s_1 - s_2)^{-1} \bmod n$, solving directly for the nonce:
\[
  \boxed{\,k \equiv (h_1 - h_2)\,(s_1 - s_2)^{-1} \pmod n\,}.
\]
With $k$ known, multiply $s_1 \equiv k^{-1}(h_1 + xr) \pmod n$ by $k$ and invert $r \bmod n$ to solve for the long-term private key:
\[
  \boxed{\,x \equiv (s_1\,k - h_1)\,r^{-1} \pmod n\,}.
\]
Two public signatures sharing $r$ and two modular inversions expose Alice's master signing key (Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-nonce}). No discrete logarithms or brute force are needed -- only two linear equations in two unknowns.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{example}[Working it with tiny numbers]
\label{ch17:nonce-tiny}
Work modulo the prime $n = 23$ (computing modular inverses as in Chapters~3 and~4). Let the signer's secret key be $x = 7$ and the reused secret nonce be $k = 4$, with public $r = 9$, signing message hashes $h_1 = 5$ and $h_2 = 11$. Since $4 \cdot 6 = 24 \equiv 1 \pmod{23}$, we have $k^{-1} \equiv 6 \pmod{23}$, producing the two signatures:
\begin{align*}
  s_1 &\equiv 6\,(5 + 7 \cdot 9) = 6 \cdot 68 = 408 \equiv 17 \pmod{23} && \text{($408 = 17 \cdot 23 + 17$)}, \\
  s_2 &\equiv 6\,(11 + 7 \cdot 9) = 6 \cdot 74 = 444 \equiv 7 \pmod{23} && \text{($444 = 19 \cdot 23 + 7$)}.
\end{align*}
An eavesdropper observes $(r, s_1, h_1) = (9, 17, 5)$ and $(r, s_2, h_2) = (9, 7, 11)$, spots the matching $r = 9$, and solves for $k$:
\begin{align*}
  k &\equiv (h_1 - h_2)\,(s_1 - s_2)^{-1} \equiv (5 - 11)\,(17 - 7)^{-1} \pmod{23} \\
    &\equiv (-6)\cdot 10^{-1} \equiv 17 \cdot 7 \pmod{23} && \text{($-6 \equiv 17$, and $10 \cdot 7 = 70 \equiv 1$)} \\
    &= 119 \equiv 4 \pmod{23} && \text{($119 = 5 \cdot 23 + 4$)}.
\end{align*}
Substituting $k = 4$ back into the first signature congruence recovers the private key $x$:
\begin{align*}
  x &\equiv (s_1 k - h_1)\,r^{-1} \equiv (17 \cdot 4 - 5)\cdot 9^{-1} \pmod{23} \\
    &\equiv 63 \cdot 9^{-1} \equiv 17 \cdot 18 \pmod{23} && \text{($63 \equiv 17$, and $9 \cdot 18 = 162 \equiv 1$)} \\
    &= 306 \equiv 7 \pmod{23} && \text{($306 = 13 \cdot 23 + 7$)}.
\end{align*}
The signer's private key $x = 7$ comes out in six lines of arithmetic.
\end{example}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Real-world casualties.} In 2010, Sony's PlayStation~3 firmware-signing system was found to use ECDSA with a \emph{static} nonce -- the exact same $k$ on every update. The \texttt{fail0verflow} team applied the two equations above to two official Sony signatures and extracted Sony's master signing key, letting anyone sign custom code the console accepted as genuine. In 2013, a bug in Android's \texttt{SecureRandom} implementation caused Bitcoin wallet apps to generate duplicate ECDSA nonces; because Bitcoin records every transaction on a public ledger (blockchain), attackers scanned the ledger for matching $r$ values and swept funds from affected addresses.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{The fix: deterministic nonces.} If a repeated random $k$ is fatal, remove the live RNG from signing altogether. Internet standard RFC~6979 specifies \emph{deterministic} DSA and ECDSA: compute $k$ from an HMAC of the message hash keyed by the private key $x$ (via a counter loop around $\operatorname{HMAC}_x(h)$). Because HMAC is a pseudorandom function (Chapter~15), $k$ is unpredictable to anyone without $x$; because $h$ changes when the message changes, distinct messages never share a nonce; and because signing reads zero random bytes at runtime, a broken or forked RNG cannot cause a repeat. The Edwards-curve signature scheme Ed25519 (Chapter~22) builds deterministic nonce derivation directly into its specification.
\end{whybox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Example~\ref{ch17:nonce-tiny} relied only on $n$ being prime so nonzero differences are invertible modulo $n$ via the extended Euclidean algorithm. Even \emph{partial} nonce bias is fatal: if a flawed RNG leaves only the top few bits of each $k$ predictable across a few hundred signatures, each signature imposes an approximate linear constraint on $x$ modulo $n$, and geometry-of-numbers algorithms (\emph{lattice reduction}, Chapter~35) combine those constraints to recover $x$ in full.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Getting Randomness Right on a Real System}
\label{ch17:getting-it-right}

On a modern operating system, the safe path is also the simplest: rely on the kernel's generator rather than building your own.

\begin{description}
  \item[Use the OS CSPRNG.] Call your operating system's cryptographic interface directly: Linux's \texttt{getrandom()}, Windows's \texttt{BCryptGenRandom}, Python's \texttt{secrets} or \texttt{os.urandom}, the Go package \texttt{crypto/rand}, or Java's \texttt{SecureRandom}. The kernel handles collection, extraction, reseeding, and fork safety.
  \item[Never seed from time, PID, or counters.] Those caused the clock-seeding and Debian keyspace collapses. Every cryptographic seed must come from physical entropy via the OS.
  \item[Never use simulation PRNGs for secrets.] Non-cryptographic generators such as C's \texttt{rand()}, Python's \texttt{random} module, Mersenne Twister, and \texttt{java.util.Random} are fast and statistically balanced yet predictable from observed output. Keys, nonces, IVs, salts, and session tokens require a CSPRNG.
  \item[Guard the boot window.] Before the kernel entropy pool is initialized, early reads can lack entropy. Use blocking \texttt{getrandom()} (without \texttt{GRND\_NONBLOCK}); in virtual machines (VMs) and embedded devices -- headless boxes without a keyboard, mouse, or spinning disk -- feed boot entropy from the host hypervisor (via \texttt{virtio-rng}) or hardware RNG instructions.
  \item[Stay fork-safe.] If an application caches DRBG state in userspace, wipe or reseed it across \texttt{fork()}. Calling the OS interface on each draw avoids the hazard altogether.
  \item[Make nonces collision-safe.] Use deterministic nonces (RFC~6979, Ed25519) for signatures; for AEAD ciphers, use a monotonic counter or an extended random nonce such as XChaCha20-Poly1305's $192$-bit nonce (an extended-nonce variant of ChaCha20-Poly1305 from Chapter~16 whose $2^{192}$ space makes random collisions negligible), never a short nonce drawn from an unvetted RNG.
  \item[Prefer auditable primitives.] Avoid opaque designs like \texttt{Dual\_EC\_DRBG} in favor of generators with nothing-up-my-sleeve constants, and mix hardware instructions like \texttt{RDRAND} into the OS pool rather than using them as your sole source.
\end{description}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Where protocols spend random bits.} TLS~1.3 (Chapter~25) draws each connection's public handshake nonces, single-use key-exchange secrets, and resumption tickets from the OS CSPRNG; predicting the key-exchange secret exposes the session keys. WiFi's WPA2 and WPA3 handshakes (Chapter~26) exchange fresh random nonces when a device joins an access point -- the 2017 Key Reinstallation Attacks (KRACK) replayed handshake messages to force nonce reuse and reset stream-cipher counters, triggering the AEAD failure of Chapter~16. Signal (Chapter~27) generates a fresh Diffie--Hellman key pair on every turn of conversation so that a later phone compromise cannot decrypt old messages; a predictable RNG would forfeit that protection. Every protocol proof assumes an honest coin-flipping tape, and the OS CSPRNG is what provides it.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{focusbox}
The chapter can be summarized in one sentence: \emph{collect real physical entropy once, seed a good CSPRNG with it, then stretch -- and let the operating system do all three for you.} Every disaster in \S\ref{ch17:failures} came from violating some part of that sentence -- a seed without entropy (Debian), a CSPRNG you cannot trust (Dual\_EC), or output reused where it had to be fresh (nonce reuse). If the random bits are weak, the mathematics in the rest of the book protects nothing.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
This chapter brings Part~II to a close. Every tool we have built so far -- stream ciphers, block ciphers, hash functions, MACs, authenticated encryption, and CSPRNGs -- belongs to \emph{symmetric} cryptography, where Alice and Bob already share a secret key. In Part~III, starting with Chapter~18, we ask how two strangers can establish a shared secret over a public channel monitored by an eavesdropper.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch17:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Min-entropy versus length]
A token generator produces $128$-bit values, yet internally it picks a word uniformly at random from a $50{,}000$-word English dictionary and takes the first $128$ bits of its SHA-256 hash. What is the min-entropy of a token? How many guesses does an optimal attacker need on average, and how does that compare to the $2^{128}$ space suggested by its bit-length? State the general lesson in one sentence.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Biased-source seeding]
A hardware source emits independent bits, each equal to $1$ with probability $p = 0.75$. (a) Compute the min-entropy per bit. (b) How many bits must you collect to seed a $256$-bit key with full min-entropy? (c) Compare with the Shannon entropy per bit $-\bigl(p\log_2 p + (1-p)\log_2(1-p)\bigr)$, explain why the two numbers differ, and say which one you must use.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Predicting an LCG]
A linear congruential generator outputs $x_{i+1} = (a x_i + c) \bmod m$ with unknown $a, c$ and known $m = 2^{31}$. You observe consecutive outputs $x_0 = 2$, $x_1 = 22$, $x_2 = 122$. Set up the two congruences $x_1 \equiv a x_0 + c$ and $x_2 \equiv a x_1 + c \pmod m$, solve for $a$ and $c$ (here $x_1 - x_0 = 20$ is divisible by $4$, so four candidate values $a + j \cdot 2^{29} \bmod 2^{31}$ fit; verify that all four predict the same $x_3$), and predict $x_3$. Why does this show an LCG is unusable for cryptography even when its output passes statistical suites?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Break a reused signature nonce]

Recover $k$ and then the private key $x$ using the elimination formulas of \S\ref{ch17:nonce-reuse}. Show each modular inverse modulo $29$ using the extended Euclidean algorithm (Chapters~3 and~4).

\begin{exercise}[The Debian keyspace]

Estimate the total number of distinct RSA-$2048$ keys an attacker must pre-generate to cover $4$ architectures and $2$ key sizes. \begin{exercise}[Why deterministic nonces are safe]
RFC~6979 derives $k$ deterministically from $\operatorname{HMAC}_x(h)$, where $h$ is the message hash and $x$ is the private key. (a) Argue why an attacker who does not know $x$ cannot predict $k$. (b) Argue why two distinct messages do not share a nonce. (c) Explain why needing zero entropy at signing time prevents the failures in \S\ref{ch17:nonce-reuse}. (d) If an attacker somehow learned one deterministic nonce $k$, could they still recover $x$? Relate your answer to the recovery formula.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Nothing-up-my-sleeve]

\begin{exercise}[The leftover-hash tax]
