\chapter{Perfect Secrecy and the One-Time Pad}

In Chapter~8, we built the language of computational hardness and bounded adversaries. Here we examine the \emph{unconditional} baseline -- security that rests on no unproven hardness assumptions and no limit on the attacker's computer. One encryption scheme in this book cannot be broken even by an adversary with unlimited computing power and unlimited time: the one-time pad, proved secure by Claude Shannon in 1949.

If an unbreakable cipher exists, why does the rest of this book exist? Because unconditional secrecy carries a steep cost: the secret key must be genuinely random, at least as long as everything you ever send, and never used twice.

\section{The setting: Alice, Bob, Eve, and a shared secret}
\label{ch09:setting}

Alice wants to send a plaintext message $m$ to Bob over a channel that Eve can read in full. Alice and Bob share a secret key $k$ that Eve does not know. Using an encryption algorithm $\Enc$, Alice computes a ciphertext $c = \Enc_k(m)$ and sends $c$; using a decryption algorithm $\Dec$, Bob computes $m = \Dec_k(c)$ and recovers the message. Eve sees $c$ and nothing else. This is \emph{symmetric} or \emph{private-key} encryption: both legitimate parties hold the same secret key, used both to encrypt and to decrypt.

\begin{definition}[Private-key encryption scheme]
\label{ch09:scheme-def}
A \emph{private-key encryption scheme} over message space $\mathcal{M}$, key space $\mathcal{K}$, and ciphertext space $\mathcal{C}$ (the sets of all allowed messages, keys, and ciphertexts) is a triple of algorithms $(\Gen, \Enc, \Dec)$ for key generation, encryption, and decryption:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\Gen$ outputs a key $k \in \mathcal{K}$, chosen according to a fixed probability distribution (usually uniform).
  \item $\Enc$ takes a key $k$ and a message $m \in \mathcal{M}$ and outputs a ciphertext $c = \Enc_k(m) \in \mathcal{C}$. It may be randomized (meaning $\Enc$ may flip internal random coins so encrypting the same $m$ twice can produce different ciphertexts; for the one-time pad, $\Enc$ is deterministic).
  \item $\Dec$ takes a key $k$ and a ciphertext $c$ and outputs a message $m = \Dec_k(c)$.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Correctness} requires that for every key $k$ that $\Gen$ can output and every message $m \in \mathcal{M}$,
\[
  \Dec_k(\Enc_k(m)) = m.
\]
\end{definition}

Correctness only guarantees that Bob recovers what Alice sent; it says nothing about what Eve learns.

Two separate sources of randomness enter this picture, and keeping them distinct prevents confusion. Throughout this chapter, uppercase letters ($K, M, C$) denote random variables and lowercase letters ($k, m, c$) denote specific values they can take.

The first source is the key. Running $\Gen$ chooses $k$ at random; write $K$ for the random variable representing the key.

The second source is the message. From Eve's point of view, the message is also uncertain -- if she already knew $m$, there would be nothing to hide. We model her uncertainty with a random variable $M$ over $\mathcal{M}$, with distribution $\Pr[M = m]$. This distribution is Eve's \emph{prior}: whatever probabilities she assigned to candidate messages before seeing any ciphertext. Maybe she knows Alice is voting in an election with $\mathcal{M} = \{\texttt{yes}, \texttt{no}\}$ and $\Pr[M=\texttt{yes}] = 0.7$. The scheme does not choose this distribution; the outside world does. A definition of secrecy must hold for \emph{every} possible prior.

\begin{focusbox}
Two random variables, kept rigorously separate:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $K$ -- the key, sampled by $\Gen$, unknown to Eve. The scheme controls its distribution.
  \item $M$ -- the message, modeling Eve's prior uncertainty. The world controls its distribution.
\end{itemize}
$K$ and $M$ are \textbf{independent}: Alice's choice of what to say has nothing to do with the random key her crypto library happened to generate. The ciphertext $C = \Enc_K(M)$ is then a random variable determined by both.
\end{focusbox}

\section{What should ``secret'' mean? Three tries}
\label{ch09:three-tries}

\textbf{Try 1: ``Eve cannot recover the key.''} This is too weak and aims at the wrong target. We do not care about the key for its own sake; we care about the message. A scheme could leak the whole message while keeping the key safe -- imagine $\Enc_k(m) = m$, ignoring the key entirely. The key stays completely hidden, yet the message travels in plaintext.

\textbf{Try 2: ``Eve cannot recover the message.''} Better, but still too weak. Suppose Eve learns the first half of a message but not the second. She has not ``recovered the message,'' yet she has learned plenty. Or suppose the ciphertext reveals that $m$ is one of two possibilities and rules out a million others. Recovering nothing in full while learning almost everything is a real failure mode.

\textbf{Try 3: ``The ciphertext tells Eve nothing she did not already know.''} Seeing $c$ should not change Eve's beliefs about the message at all. Whatever probability she assigned to each candidate message before, she assigns the exact same probability after. The ciphertext carries zero information about the message.

Shannon captured this requirement in a single equation.

\begin{definition}[Perfect secrecy]
\label{ch09:perfect-def}
An encryption scheme $(\Gen,\Enc,\Dec)$ over message space $\mathcal{M}$ is \emph{perfectly secret} if for every prior distribution over $M$, every message $m \in \mathcal{M}$, and every ciphertext $c \in \mathcal{C}$ with $\Pr[C = c] > 0$,
\[
  \Pr[M = m \mid C = c] \;=\; \Pr[M = m].
\]
\end{definition}

The condition $\Pr[C = c] > 0$ restricts attention to ciphertexts that can occur. The left-hand side $\Pr[M = m \mid C = c]$ is Eve's \emph{posterior} probability for $m$ -- her updated belief after seeing $c$. Perfect secrecy says that conditioning on the ciphertext changes nothing: posterior equals prior (Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-perfect-vs-leaky}). In the language of Chapter~7, $M$ and $C$ are independent random variables.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Perfect secrecy is the statement ``$C$ and $M$ are independent.'' Eve can inspect the ciphertext at length, run any computation on it, and cross-reference it with side knowledge -- her posterior belief about the message stays identical to her prior. The ciphertext depends on the message through the key, but because the key is unknown and random, that dependence carries no usable information.
\end{intuitionbox}

This is a strong demand. It must hold for \emph{every} prior distribution, so an adversary with strong side information about the message is still no better off after seeing $c$. Nothing in the definition mentions computing power: Eve may take unlimited time. Perfect secrecy is an \emph{information-theoretic} guarantee -- the information is not present in $c$ to be extracted, regardless of how much computation anyone spends.

\subsection{An equivalent and easier-to-check form}
\label{ch09:equiv}

Definition~\ref{ch09:perfect-def} quantifies over all priors, which is awkward to check directly. The following equivalent condition checks only the encryption mechanism itself.

\begin{lemma}[Ciphertext distribution is message-independent]
\label{ch09:equiv-lemma}
A scheme is perfectly secret if and only if for every pair of messages $m_0, m_1 \in \mathcal{M}$ and every ciphertext $c \in \mathcal{C}$,
\[
  \Pr[\Enc_K(m_0) = c] \;=\; \Pr[\Enc_K(m_1) = c],
\]
where $K \gets \Gen$ denotes sampling a key by running $\Gen$, and the probability is taken over that choice (and any internal randomness in $\Enc$).
\end{lemma}

In words: the probability distribution of the ciphertext is the same no matter which message you encrypt. Encrypting \texttt{ATTACK} and encrypting \texttt{RETREAT} draw ciphertexts from \emph{identical} distributions. Because the ciphertext distribution carries no fingerprint of the message, observing a particular $c$ cannot favor one message over another.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why the two forms agree.} Suppose Lemma~\ref{ch09:equiv-lemma} holds, so $\Pr[\Enc_K(m) = c] = \Pr[C = c \mid M = m]$ has a single value $p(c)$ independent of $m$ (using independence of $K$ and $M$). Sum over all candidate messages $m' \in \mathcal{M}$ to find the overall probability of seeing $c$:
\begin{align*}
  \Pr[C = c] &= \sum_{m' \in \mathcal{M}} \Pr[C = c \mid M = m']\,\Pr[M = m'] && \text{(partition by message)} \\
             &= \sum_{m' \in \mathcal{M}} p(c)\,\Pr[M = m'] = p(c) && \Bigl(\text{since } \sum_{m'} \Pr[M = m'] = 1\Bigr).
\end{align*}
Now substitute $\Pr[C = c \mid M = m] = p(c)$ and $\Pr[C = c] = p(c)$ into Bayes' rule:
\[
  \Pr[M = m \mid C = c] = \frac{\Pr[C = c \mid M = m]\,\Pr[M = m]}{\Pr[C = c]}
  = \frac{p(c)\,\Pr[M = m]}{p(c)} = \Pr[M = m],
\]
which is Definition~\ref{ch09:perfect-def}. Running the same algebra in reverse gives the converse: if $\Pr[M = m \mid C = c] = \Pr[M = m]$ under a uniform prior over $\mathcal{M}$, Bayes' rule rearranges to $\Pr[\Enc_K(m) = c] = \Pr[C = c]$ for every $m$.
\end{whybox}

We now have a concrete goal: build a scheme in which every message produces the same probability distribution over ciphertexts. The one-time pad achieves this using bitwise exclusive-or (XOR).

\section{XOR: the operation that makes pads work}
\label{ch09:xor}

Bitwise exclusive-or, written $\xor$, acts on single bits according to
\[
  0 \xor 0 = 0,\quad 0 \xor 1 = 1,\quad 1 \xor 0 = 1,\quad 1 \xor 1 = 0.
\]
XOR is addition modulo $2$: it outputs $1$ when the two input bits differ and $0$ when they match. On bit-strings of equal length we apply $\xor$ position by position. Three algebraic properties make it suited to encryption.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{It is its own inverse.} Because $b \xor b = 0$ for any bit $b$, we have $(x \xor k) \xor k = x \xor (k \xor k) = x \xor 0 = x$. Applying $\xor k$ a second time undoes the first, so decryption uses the same operation as encryption.
  \item \textbf{It is associative and commutative.} You can reorder and regroup XORs freely: $a \xor b \xor c$ is unambiguous.
  \item \textbf{XOR with a uniform random bit produces a uniform random bit that hides the input.} Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-xor-masking} contrasts this property with bitwise AND ($\land$), which collapses outcomes and leaks the input.
\end{itemize}

\begin{lemma}[The masking lemma]
\label{ch09:masking}
Let $x$ be any fixed bit (or $n$-bit string) and let $K$ be a uniformly random bit (or $n$-bit string), independent of $x$. Then $Y = x \xor K$ is uniformly distributed, so its distribution does not depend on $x$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Take a single bit first. If $x = 0$, then $Y = K$, so $\Pr[Y=0] = \Pr[K=0] = \tfrac12$ and $\Pr[Y=1] = \tfrac12$. If $x = 1$, then $Y = 1 \xor K$, which equals $1$ when $K=0$ and $0$ when $K=1$; again each outcome has probability $\tfrac12$. Either way, $Y$ is uniform on $\{0,1\}$. For $n$-bit strings, the $n$ bits of $K$ are independent fair coins, so the $n$ output bits $Y_i = x_i \xor K_i$ are also independent fair coins; each of the $2^n$ strings in $\{0,1\}^n$ therefore occurs with probability $(\tfrac12)^n = 2^{-n}$, independent of $x$.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuitionbox}
A fair coin XOR-ed onto your bit \emph{is} a fair coin. The mask $K$ is equally likely to leave your bit alone (when $K=0$) or to flip it (when $K=1$), so the output is a fair coin flip regardless of what you started with. Whatever bit you wanted to hide, the output has the same uniform distribution whether your secret was $0$ or $1$ -- Lemma~\ref{ch09:equiv-lemma} on one bit. Stack $n$ independent fair coins side by side and an $n$-bit message disappears behind $n$ bits of uniform noise.
\end{intuitionbox}

\section{The one-time pad}
\label{ch09:otp}

\begin{definition}[One-time pad]
\label{ch09:otp-def}
Fix a message length $n$. The message, key, and ciphertext spaces are all $\mathcal{M} = \mathcal{K} = \mathcal{C} = \{0,1\}^n$ (the set of all $n$-bit strings). The scheme is:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\Gen$: output a key $k \getsr \{0,1\}^n$, i.e.\ $n$ uniformly random independent bits.
  \item $\Enc_k(m) = m \xor k$.
  \item $\Dec_k(c) = c \xor k$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

Correctness follows immediately because XOR is self-inverse; writing $0^n$ for the string of $n$ zeros,
\[
  \Dec_k(\Enc_k(m)) = (m \xor k) \xor k = m \xor (k \xor k) = m \xor 0^n = m.
\]

Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-otp-flow} shows the pipeline: Alice XORs the message with $k$ before transmission, and Bob XORs the ciphertext with the same $k$ on arrival.

\subsection{A tiny worked example}
\label{ch09:tiny}

Suppose Alice sends the $5$-bit message $m = \texttt{10110}$ and $\Gen$ hands her $k = \texttt{01101}$. XOR them column by column, writing $1$ where the bits differ:
\[
\begin{array}{rccccc}
 m = & 1 & 0 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\
 \xor\; k = & 0 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 \\ \hline
 c = & 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 1
\end{array}
\]
So $c = \texttt{11011}$ goes on the wire. Bob, holding the same $k = \texttt{01101}$, XORs again:
\[
\begin{array}{rccccc}
 c = & 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\
 \xor\; k = & 0 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 \\ \hline
 m = & 1 & 0 & 1 & 1 & 0
\end{array}
\]
and recovers \texttt{10110}. In the third column, $1 \xor 1 = 0$ during encryption and $0 \xor 1 = 1$ during decryption: the key bit toggles the message bit on the way out and toggles it back on the way in.

The same example shows why the pad is perfectly secret. Suppose Eve intercepts $c = \texttt{11011}$ and wonders whether the message was $\texttt{10110}$. The key that maps $\texttt{10110}$ to $c$ is $k = m \xor c = \texttt{10110} \xor \texttt{11011} = \texttt{01101}$, which has probability $1/32$. If she tests $\texttt{00000}$ instead, the required key is $k' = \texttt{00000} \xor \texttt{11011} = \texttt{11011}$, which also has probability $1/32$. For \emph{every} one of the $2^5 = 32$ candidate messages, there is a single key that maps it to the observed $c$, and all $32$ keys are equally likely. Eve has no basis to prefer any message over any other.

\begin{walkbox}
You intercept the 3-bit ciphertext $c = \texttt{101}$. The message space is all of $\{0,1\}^3$. For the candidate message $m = \texttt{111}$, which key would Alice have needed? And for $m = \texttt{000}$? Convince yourself each of the $8$ candidate messages has its own unique explanatory key. \\[2pt]
\emph{Answer.} Solve $m \xor k = c$ by XOR-ing $m$ on both sides: $k = m \xor c$. For $\texttt{111}$: $\texttt{111}\xor\texttt{101} = \texttt{010}$. For $\texttt{000}$: $\texttt{000}\xor\texttt{101}=\texttt{101}$. As $m$ ranges over all $8$ strings, $k = m \xor c$ also ranges over all $8$ strings, hitting each once -- the map $m \mapsto m\xor c$ is a bijection (a one-to-one correspondence). Eight messages correspond to eight distinct, equally likely keys, so no message is favored.
\end{walkbox}

\subsection{The proof of perfect secrecy}
\label{ch09:otp-proof}

\begin{theorem}[Shannon, 1949]
\label{ch09:otp-secure}
The one-time pad is perfectly secret.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We check Lemma~\ref{ch09:equiv-lemma} by computing $\Pr[\Enc_K(m) = c]$ for an arbitrary message $m \in \{0,1\}^n$ and ciphertext $c \in \{0,1\}^n$:
\begin{align*}
  \Pr[\Enc_K(m) = c] &= \Pr[m \xor K = c] && \text{(definition of $\Enc$)} \\
                     &= \Pr[K = m \xor c] && \text{(XOR both sides with $m$)} \\
                     &= 2^{-n}            && \text{($K$ is uniform on $\{0,1\}^n$)}.
\end{align*}
Because $2^{-n}$ does not depend on $m$, every message produces the exact same uniform distribution over ciphertexts. By Lemma~\ref{ch09:equiv-lemma}, the scheme is perfectly secret.
\end{proof}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Checking an uneven prior with numbers.} Suppose Alice encrypts her $1$-bit election vote ($0$ for \texttt{no}, $1$ for \texttt{yes}) under a $1$-bit pad, where Eve's prior is $\Pr[M = 1] = 0.7$ and $\Pr[M = 0] = 0.3$. Eve intercepts $c = 1$. Does seeing $c = 1$ raise the odds of $M = 1$? Compute both ways $c = 1$ can arise:
\begin{align*}
  \Pr[C = 1 \text{ and } M = 1] &= \Pr[M = 1]\,\Pr[K = 0] = 0.7 \cdot 0.5 = 0.35, \\
  \Pr[C = 1 \text{ and } M = 0] &= \Pr[M = 0]\,\Pr[K = 1] = 0.3 \cdot 0.5 = 0.15.
\end{align*}
Summing gives $\Pr[C = 1] = 0.35 + 0.15 = 0.50$, so Bayes' rule gives
\[
  \Pr[M = 1 \mid C = 1] = \frac{0.35}{0.50} = 0.70 = \Pr[M = 1].
\]
Both key bits have probability $0.5$, so that factor of $0.5$ scales every prior probability equally and cancels out of the ratio.
\end{whybox}

\begin{trapbox}
A frequent misreading: ``the one-time pad is secure because the key is secret.'' Secrecy of the key is necessary for every cipher, including breakable ones. What makes the pad \emph{perfect} is sharper: the key is \emph{as long as the message} and \emph{uniformly random}, so the set of possible keys matches the set of possible messages one-to-one for every ciphertext $c$. Shorten the key, reuse the key, or skew its distribution, and that bijection breaks -- some messages become more likely than others given $c$, and the proof fails.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{historybox}
The construction is older than its proof. In 1882 Sacramento banker Frank Miller described a cipher using single-use random key lists for telegraph traffic, though the idea was forgotten. In 1917 Gilbert Vernam, an engineer at AT\&T, patented an electromechanical cipher that XOR-ed teletype characters (5-bit telegraph codes) against a punched paper key tape. Vernam originally used repeating loops of tape; Captain Joseph Mauborgne of the U.S. Army Signal Corps recognized that repeating tapes fell to frequency analysis, whereas a genuinely random, never-reused tape could not be broken.

That engineering insight became a theorem in 1949 when Claude Shannon published \emph{Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems}, founding mathematical cryptography a year after founding information theory. Shannon defined perfect secrecy, proved the pad achieves it, and proved that \emph{any} perfectly secret scheme needs a key at least as long as the message (Section~\ref{ch09:key-length}). Soviet intelligence used pads heavily for diplomatic and espionage cables, and the Washington--Moscow ``hot line'' established after the Cuban Missile Crisis ran on one-time tape.
\end{historybox}

\begin{implementbox}
A toy one-time pad in Python, encrypting byte by byte.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
import os

def otp_encrypt(message: bytes, key: bytes) -> bytes:
    if len(key) != len(message):
        raise ValueError("key must be as long as the message")
    return bytes(m ^ k for m, k in zip(message, key))  # ^ is bitwise XOR

# decryption is the SAME function: XOR is its own inverse
otp_decrypt = otp_encrypt

msg = b"ATTACK AT DAWN"
key = os.urandom(len(msg))          # fresh message-length key
ct  = otp_encrypt(msg, key)
print(ct.hex())                      # indistinguishable from random bytes
assert otp_decrypt(ct, key) == msg   # round-trips to the original message

# Security depends on three rules this function cannot check on its own:
#   1. key from a true random source (os.urandom is pseudorandom)
#   2. key length == message length (checked above)
#   3. NEVER reuse a key across two messages
\end{lstlisting}
\textbf{Learning only.} Production systems rarely use one-time pads because the three rules above cannot be maintained at scale. Moreover, \texttt{os.urandom} is a cryptographically secure pseudorandom number generator (CSPRNG) that stretches an operating-system entropy pool rather than delivering pure physical randomness, so this code provides \emph{computational} security, not perfect secrecy. For real software, use a vetted authenticated-encryption library (which protects both secrecy and integrity against tampering; Chapter~16).
\end{implementbox}

\section{Why the key must be long, random, and fresh}
\label{ch09:three-conditions}

Theorem~\ref{ch09:otp-secure} relied on three properties of the key: it is uniformly random, it is at least as long as the message, and (because we encrypted a single message) it is used only once. Drop any one of the three and perfect secrecy fails.

\subsection{Random: a biased pad leaks}
\label{ch09:must-random}

The masking lemma required $K$ to be \emph{uniform}. Suppose instead an implementation produces biased key bits, each equal to $1$ with probability $p = 0.9$ and $0$ with probability $0.1$. Then for a single-bit message,
\[
  \Pr[\Enc_K(0) = 1] = \Pr[K = 1] = 0.9, \qquad \Pr[\Enc_K(1) = 1] = \Pr[K = 0] = 0.1.
\]
The ciphertext bit's distribution now \emph{depends on the message}: observing $c = 1$ strongly favors $m = 0$ (Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-bias-leak}). Lemma~\ref{ch09:equiv-lemma} fails whenever $p \ne \tfrac12$. The sequence of bits XOR-ed with the message -- called the \emph{keystream} -- must be uniform, which requires a physical entropy source rather than a predictable counter or a low-entropy seed (Chapter~17).

\subsection{Long: a short key cannot cover the messages}
\label{ch09:must-long}

What if the key is shorter than the message -- say a $128$-bit key for a $1$-megabyte file? Stretching the key with an algorithm is what a \emph{stream cipher} does (Chapter~10), trading perfect secrecy for computational security. Repeating the short key end-to-end across the message is the mistake of Chapter~2's Vigen\`ere cipher and falls quickly to attack. More fundamentally, Shannon's theorem shows that \emph{no} encoding or algorithm can achieve perfect secrecy when the key space is smaller than the message space.

\section{Shannon's theorem: perfect secrecy forces a huge key}
\label{ch09:key-length}

The one-time pad's steepest requirement -- a key at least as long as everything you send -- is mandatory for \emph{every} perfectly secret scheme, regardless of how $\Gen$, $\Enc$, and $\Dec$ are built.

\begin{theorem}[Shannon's key-length bound]
\label{ch09:shannon-bound}
If $(\Gen,\Enc,\Dec)$ is perfectly secret over message space $\mathcal{M}$ with key space $\mathcal{K}$, then $\abs{\mathcal{K}} \ge \abs{\mathcal{M}}$, where $\abs{\cdot}$ denotes the number of elements in a set. In particular, when $\mathcal{M} = \{0,1\}^n$, keys require at least $\log_2 \abs{\mathcal{K}} \ge n$ bits.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Assume for contradiction that $\abs{\mathcal{K}} < \abs{\mathcal{M}}$.

Pick any ciphertext $c$ with $\Pr[C = c] > 0$, and collect every message to which $c$ can decrypt as $k$ varies over the entire key space:
\[
  \mathcal{M}(c) = \set{\,\Dec_k(c) : k \in \mathcal{K}\,}.
\]
Each key $k \in \mathcal{K}$ produces a single output $\Dec_k(c)$, so $\mathcal{M}(c)$ contains at most $\abs{\mathcal{K}}$ messages. Because $\abs{\mathcal{K}} < \abs{\mathcal{M}}$, at least one message $m^{*} \in \mathcal{M}$ lies outside $\mathcal{M}(c)$: \emph{no key} decrypts $c$ to $m^{*}$ (Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-shannon-bound}).

By correctness ($\Dec_k(\Enc_k(m^{*})) = m^{*}$), no key can encrypt $m^{*}$ to $c$ either, so $\Pr[C = c \mid M = m^{*}] = 0$. Therefore, when Eve observes $c$,
\[
  \Pr[M = m^{*} \mid C = c] = 0.
\]
Choose the uniform prior over $\mathcal{M}$, which gives $\Pr[M = m^{*}] = 1/\abs{\mathcal{M}} > 0$. Then $\Pr[M = m^{*} \mid C = c] = 0 \ne \Pr[M = m^{*}]$, contradicting Definition~\ref{ch09:perfect-def}. Hence $\abs{\mathcal{K}} \ge \abs{\mathcal{M}}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Perfect secrecy demands that \emph{every} message remain a plausible explanation of \emph{every} ciphertext; ruling even one message out leaks information. Keeping every message in play for a given $c$ requires at least one key per message that decrypts $c$ back to that message. With fewer keys than messages, at least one message has no key pointing to it from $c$, so Eve can cross it off as soon as she sees $c$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{focusbox}
To send up to $2^n$ different messages with perfect secrecy you need at least $2^n$ keys, hence an $n$-bit key: one fresh key bit for every message bit you transmit. Protecting a gigabyte requires sharing a gigabyte of secret key beforehand over a channel you already trusted. A perfectly secret cipher \emph{transports} secrecy established earlier rather than creating new secrecy from a short secret.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Chapter~7 defined the Shannon entropy $H(M)$, the average uncertainty of $M$ in bits. Recall from Chapter~7 the conditional entropy $H(M \mid C)$, the average uncertainty about $M$ remaining after Eve observes $C$. Perfect secrecy is equivalent to $H(M \mid C) = H(M)$: observing $C$ removes none of the message's uncertainty. Because $M = \Dec_K(C)$ is determined by $K$ and $C$ together, the remaining uncertainty in $M$ cannot exceed the uncertainty in $K$, giving $H(K) \ge H(M)$. A short key has too little entropy to conceal a high-entropy message, forcing the excess structure to leak into the ciphertext.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{The two-time pad: reuse is fatal}
\label{ch09:two-time}

The ``one'' in ``one-time pad'' matters. Reusing a pad on two messages breaks secrecy outright rather than weakening it gradually, and it is the most common way real one-time-pad systems have failed.

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Setup.} Alice encrypts two $n$-bit messages $m_1$ and $m_2$ with the \emph{same} key $k$. Eve intercepts both ciphertexts:
\[
  c_1 = m_1 \xor k, \qquad c_2 = m_2 \xor k.
\]
\textbf{The break.} Eve XORs the two ciphertexts together, and the shared key cancels:
\begin{align*}
  c_1 \xor c_2 &= (m_1 \xor k) \xor (m_2 \xor k) \\
               &= (m_1 \xor m_2) \xor (k \xor k) && \text{(commutativity and associativity)} \\
               &= m_1 \xor m_2 && \text{(since $k \xor k = 0^n$)}.
\end{align*}
The secret key has vanished (Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-twotime}). Eve holds $m_1 \xor m_2$, the bitwise XOR of the two plaintexts, reducing the cipher to separating two overlaid messages using the redundancy of natural language or file formats.

\textbf{Why $m_1 \xor m_2$ leaks the plaintexts.} English text and structured data are far from uniform bit-strings. Under ASCII (the standard $8$-bit character encoding), uppercase \texttt{A} is \texttt{01000001}, lowercase \texttt{a} is \texttt{01100001}, and the space character is \texttt{00100000} (hexadecimal \texttt{0x20}), which has a $1$ only in the bit distinguishing uppercase from lowercase; thus $\text{letter} \xor \text{space}$ produces the same letter with flipped case. Eve exploits this through \emph{crib dragging} (Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-crib-dragging}): she takes a \emph{crib} -- a guessed common word or phrase such as \texttt{"the~"} -- slides it across positions of $c_1 \xor c_2 = m_1 \xor m_2$, and XORs it against each $4$-byte window. Wherever $m_2$ contains \texttt{"the~"}, the XOR reveals the matching $4$-byte slice of $m_1$ as readable English. Extending partial words in both messages unzips the rest of $m_1$ and $m_2$.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{example}[A reuse break in miniature]
\label{ch09:reuse-example}
Take $6$-bit messages
\[
  m_1 = \texttt{101100},\quad m_2 = \texttt{110010},\quad k = \texttt{011011}.
\]
Encrypting both under $k$ produces
\[
  c_1 = m_1 \xor k = \texttt{110111},\qquad c_2 = m_2 \xor k = \texttt{101001}.
\]
Eve never learns $k$, yet she computes
\[
  c_1 \xor c_2 = \texttt{110111} \xor \texttt{101001} = \texttt{011110},
\]
which matches $m_1 \xor m_2 = \texttt{101100} \xor \texttt{110010} = \texttt{011110}$. If Eve learns or guesses $m_1 = \texttt{101100}$, she recovers $m_2$ immediately without recovering $k$:
\[
  m_2 = (c_1 \xor c_2) \xor m_1 = \texttt{011110} \xor \texttt{101100} = \texttt{110010}.
\]
\end{example}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Historical case study: VENONA and pad reuse.} During World War II, Soviet intelligence encrypted diplomatic and espionage cables with one-time pads. Under wartime supply pressure, the pad-printing operation duplicated pages of key material, causing separate messages to share a pad. U.S. and British cryptanalysts in the VENONA project (1943--1980) detected the collisions and exploited $c_1 \xor c_2 = m_1 \xor m_2$ via crib dragging and frequency analysis, exposing atomic-espionage networks. Operational pad reuse defeated a mathematically unbreakable cipher.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{A modern case: WEP and stream-cipher reuse.} The same failure broke Wired Equivalent Privacy (WEP), Wi-Fi's original security protocol. WEP used the RC4 stream cipher, which stretched a shared secret key plus a $24$-bit \emph{initialization vector} (IV) into a per-packet keystream. The IV traveled in the clear so the receiver could generate the matching keystream. Because $24$ bits gives only $2^{24} \approx 1.68 \times 10^7$ possible IVs, the birthday bound from Chapter~7 causes random IV collisions within roughly $2^{12} = 4096$ packets, and counters wrap around within hours on busy networks. Two packets sharing an IV under the same key reuse the exact same keystream, exposing $m_1 \xor m_2$; predictable network packet headers supply immediate cribs. Modern protocols replace short IVs with a longer \emph{nonce} -- a per-message value guaranteed never to repeat under a given key.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{trapbox}
Workarounds such as ``hash the key differently each time'' or ``append a counter'' define new stream ciphers that require computational security proofs of their own. A true one-time pad has no patch for reuse: every message of length $n$ bits consumes $n$ fresh key bits that are then destroyed. Once you want to encrypt multiple messages under a fixed secret, you need a stream or block cipher with a nonce.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Why we settle for computational security}
\label{ch09:computational}

Shannon's bound (Theorem~\ref{ch09:shannon-bound}) fixes the price of perfect secrecy: a key at least as long as all traffic it will ever protect, uniformly random, delivered beforehand over a trusted channel, and never reused (Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-key-scaling}). Two parties who can exchange a suitcase of random tape before a mission can pay that price. A web browser opening a Transport Layer Security (TLS) connection -- the protocol behind \texttt{https} -- to a server it has never contacted before, with no shared secret and megabytes of data to transfer, cannot.

Modern cryptography therefore trades \emph{perfect} secrecy for \emph{computational} secrecy: a ciphertext may determine the message in information-theoretic principle, provided recovering that information takes more computation than any realistic attacker -- say $2^{128}$ operations -- can perform.

\begin{focusbox}
Computational security relaxes perfect secrecy in two controlled ways (Chapter~8):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Bounded adversary.} Instead of defending against a computationally unlimited Eve, we defend against every probabilistic polynomial-time ($\PPT$) adversary.
  \item \textbf{Negligible advantage.} Instead of demanding zero leakage, we allow the adversary's advantage to be \emph{negligible} ($\negl(n)$): smaller than $1/p(n)$ for every polynomial $p$ once the security parameter $n$ is large enough.
\end{itemize}
Those two concessions buy short keys ($32$ bytes protecting terabytes), safe multi-message encryption under unique nonces, and -- with public-key cryptography, where encryption and decryption use separate keys (Part~III) -- secure communication between strangers with no pre-shared secret.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Is the trade safe?} It is conditional. Perfect secrecy is an unconditional theorem, whereas computational secrecy rests on \emph{unproven} hardness assumptions: that factoring large integers is slow, that the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES, Chapter~12) behaves like a random permutation (a random reversible shuffling of fixed-length bit blocks), or that discrete logarithms are intractable. If an underlying assumption falls, schemes built on it fall too. Cryptographers accept that risk because decades of sustained cryptanalysis have failed to break well-studied candidates, and doubling an $n$-bit symmetric key to $2n$ bits squares brute-force search from $2^n$ to $2^{2n} = (2^n)^2$ operations.
\end{whybox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Where the pad survives, and where computational security takes over.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{One-time pad:} diplomatic and intelligence channels where couriers can pre-distribute key material and messages must remain secret indefinitely. Shortwave ``numbers stations'' broadcasting spoken digit groups have long been used to send one-time-pad traffic to field agents, and quantum key distribution (QKD) uses quantum measurements to establish fresh shared random bits for pad encryption.
  \item \textbf{Computational security:} TLS (\texttt{https}), Wi-Fi Protected Access (WPA2 and WPA3, which replaced broken WEP), Signal's end-to-end messaging, Secure Shell (SSH), and full-disk encryption. All rely on short keys, bounded adversaries, and tested hardness assumptions so they can scale to billions of devices.
\end{itemize}
\end{applicationbox}

\section{What to remember}
\label{ch09:summary}

Perfect secrecy means the ciphertext is statistically independent of the message: $\Pr[M = m \mid C = c] = \Pr[M = m]$ for every prior, against an adversary with unlimited computing power (Definition~\ref{ch09:perfect-def}). The one-time pad achieves it by XOR-ing the message with an independent, uniformly random key of equal length, turning every plaintext into the same uniform distribution over ciphertexts (Lemma~\ref{ch09:masking} and Theorem~\ref{ch09:otp-secure}).

Shannon's bound (Theorem~\ref{ch09:shannon-bound}) proves that \emph{every} perfectly secret scheme requires $\abs{\mathcal{K}} \ge \abs{\mathcal{M}}$. The key must also be uniform and single-use: reusing a pad on two messages reveals $c_1 \xor c_2 = m_1 \xor m_2$ and exposes the plaintexts to crib dragging (Section~\ref{ch09:two-time}), as happened in VENONA and WEP.

Because pre-sharing message-length keys cannot work across open networks, modern systems trade perfect secrecy for computational secrecy against $\PPT$ adversaries with negligible advantage. Chapter~10 makes the first step in that trade: replacing the long random tape of the one-time pad with an algorithm that stretches a short random seed into a long pseudorandom keystream -- a stream cipher.

\section*{Exercises}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[XOR warm-up]
\label{ch09:ex-xor}
Let $m = \texttt{11010010}$ and $k = \texttt{01100101}$. Compute $c = m \xor k$. Then verify $c \xor k = m$. Finally, suppose Eve learns that the ciphertext is $c$ and the message is $m$; show she can compute the key, and explain in one sentence why this does not violate perfect secrecy.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Every message is reachable]
\label{ch09:ex-reachable}
Fix a one-time-pad ciphertext $c \in \{0,1\}^n$. Prove that the map $m \mapsto m \xor c$ is a bijection on $\{0,1\}^n$, and explain how this bijection is exactly the statement that every candidate message has exactly one explanatory key. Relate this to the counting step in the proof of Shannon's bound (Theorem~\ref{ch09:shannon-bound}).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Posterior equals prior, by hand]
\label{ch09:ex-bayes}
Let $\mathcal{M} = \{0,1\}^2$ and suppose Eve's prior is $\Pr[M=\texttt{00}]=\tfrac12$, $\Pr[M=\texttt{01}]=\tfrac14$, $\Pr[M=\texttt{10}]=\tfrac18$, $\Pr[M=\texttt{11}]=\tfrac18$. Using a one-time pad with a uniform $2$-bit key, compute $\Pr[M=\texttt{00}\mid C=\texttt{10}]$ from first principles with Bayes' rule, and confirm it equals the prior $\tfrac12$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Break a two-time pad]
\label{ch09:ex-twotime}
You intercept two ciphertexts encrypted under the \emph{same} 8-bit pad:
\[
  c_1 = \texttt{10011100}, \qquad c_2 = \texttt{11001010}.
\]
Compute $c_1 \xor c_2$. You are then told (a crib) that $m_1 = \texttt{01000001}$. Recover $m_2$ without ever learning the key, and state the general formula you used.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The bias leak]
\label{ch09:ex-bias}
An implementation generates each key bit independently as $1$ with probability $p \ne \tfrac12$. For a one-bit message, compute $\Pr[M=0 \mid C=1]$ under a uniform prior $\Pr[M=0]=\Pr[M=1]=\tfrac12$, as a function of $p$. Show it equals $\tfrac12$ only when $p=\tfrac12$, and interpret what a deviation tells the adversary.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A scheme that is not perfectly secret]
\label{ch09:ex-notperfect}
Define a ``shift cipher'' over $\mathcal{M}=\Zn{26}$: $\Gen$ picks $k \getsr \Zn{26}$, $\Enc_k(m) = (m+k) \bmod 26$. Prove that for a \emph{single} letter this scheme \emph{is} perfectly secret. Then argue that for two-letter messages drawn from English (so $M$ is non-uniform), encrypting both letters with the same $k$ is no longer perfectly secret, and exhibit a ciphertext-message pair whose posterior differs from its prior.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Shannon's bound is tight]
\label{ch09:ex-tight}
The one-time pad meets Shannon's bound with equality: $\abs{\mathcal{K}} = \abs{\mathcal{M}} = 2^n$. Construct a \emph{different} perfectly secret scheme over $\mathcal{M} = \Zn{q}$ (any modulus $q \ge 2$, not necessarily a power of two) with $\abs{\mathcal{K}} = \abs{\mathcal{M}} = q$, prove it perfectly secret, and confirm it meets the bound with equality. (Hint: replace XOR by addition modulo $q$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Why pre-sharing is the catch]
\label{ch09:ex-presharing}
Estimate the amount of one-time-pad key material two people would need to pre-share to protect one year of typical messaging traffic (pick and state a reasonable bytes-per-day figure). Compare this to the $32$ bytes of a typical symmetric key in a computationally secure scheme. Use the contrast to explain, in a short paragraph, why TLS cannot use a one-time pad.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Perfect secrecy via entropy]
\label{ch09:ex-entropy}
Using the conditional entropy formulation from Section~\ref{ch09:key-length} and Chapter~7, perfect secrecy is equivalent to $H(M \mid C) = H(M)$. Assuming this, prove $H(K) \ge H(M)$, and deduce Shannon's key-length bound $\abs{\mathcal{K}} \ge \abs{\mathcal{M}}$. (Hint: because $M = \Dec_K(C)$ is determined by $K$ and $C$, $H(M \mid C) \le H(K \mid C)$; conditioning cannot increase average uncertainty, so $H(K \mid C) \le H(K)$; then choose a uniform prior on $M$ and apply Chapter~7's bound $H(K) \le \log_2 \abs{\mathcal{K}}$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Malleability of the pad]
\label{ch09:ex-malleable}
The one-time pad is perfectly \emph{secret} but offers no \emph{integrity}: it is \emph{malleable}, meaning an attacker can alter a ciphertext in transit to cause a predictable change in the decrypted plaintext. Suppose Eve knows that $m$ is an account number and intercepts $c = m \xor k$. Show that by XOR-ing a chosen mask $\delta$ into $c$, Eve can flip chosen bits of the decrypted message without knowing $k$. Give a concrete $4$-bit example, and explain why perfect secrecy does not protect against active tampering -- motivating message authentication in Chapter~15.
\end{exercise}
