\chapter{Certificates and Public-Key Infrastructure}

In Chapter~23, we saw how ephemeral Diffie--Hellman and digital signatures combine to
create forward-secret, authenticated sessions. Yet the entire construction rested on
one assumption we did not examine: when Alice grabs Bob's public verification
key, it belongs to Bob. Diffie--Hellman lets two strangers agree on a shared
secret over a wire that Eve is reading. RSA and elliptic-curve signatures let anyone
verify that a message came from the holder of a particular private key. None of
that tells you \emph{whose} key you are holding. A public key is a large number;
nothing inside the number itself tells you who generated it.

Linking a public key to the real-world identity of its owner is the \emph{binding problem}. If Eve can convince you that her key is Bob's, every guarantee fails: she decrypts what you meant for Bob, signs in his name, and sits in the middle of your ``secure'' channel relaying a doctored copy of everything. No cryptography is broken; what fails is your belief about whose key you hold.

This chapter asks how you can verify who owns a public key you have never seen before, how that guarantee holds up when keys are stolen or authorities misbehave, and what alternatives exist when there is no central authority.

\chapterline

\section{The binding problem}
\label{ch24:binding}

Suppose Alice wants to send a credit-card number to her bank's website, \texttt{bank.example}. The bank holds an RSA key pair $(pk_B, sk_B)$, consisting of a public key $pk_B$ and a secret private key $sk_B$. The plan: Alice fetches $pk_B$, encrypts under it (or uses it to verify the bank's signature on a Diffie--Hellman exchange), and only the bank, holding $sk_B$, can complete the other side. This works only if Alice gets the real $pk_B$.

Eve runs the coffee-shop WiFi. When Alice's laptop asks the network for \texttt{bank.example}, Eve answers. She hands Alice her own public key $pk_E$ (paired with Eve's private key $sk_E$) and claims it is the bank's. Alice, seeing only a number, has no way to tell. She encrypts to $pk_E$, Eve decrypts with $sk_E$, reads the card number, re-encrypts to the real $pk_B$, and forwards it on. Both Alice and the bank see a working session. Eve sees everything.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Public-key cryptography solves \emph{secrecy} and \emph{authenticity of messages} given that you already know the right key. It does not, by itself, solve \emph{identity}. ``This message was signed by the holder of key $K$'' is a mathematical fact. ``Key $K$ belongs to Bob's Bank'' is a fact about the world, and the world is not provable by exponentiation. Some out-of-band authority (acting outside the untrusted network channel itself) has to vouch for the mapping
\[
\text{name} \;\longmapsto\; \text{public key}.
\]
A certificate is that vouching, written down and digitally signed.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Three ways to bind a name to a key}

There are three main architectures for solving the binding problem, and every deployed system uses one of them or a blend.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Trust on first use (TOFU).} The first time you talk to a party, you accept whatever key they present and store it locally. On every later connection you check that the key has not changed. This is how Secure Shell (SSH) treats \emph{host keys} (a server's long-term public key): on first login it prompts you to confirm a hash fingerprint of the key, then stays silent unless the key changes, when it raises a loud warning. TOFU needs no central authority, though the first connection is unprotected and \emph{key rotation} (replacing an old key with a new one) looks indistinguishable from an attack.
\item \textbf{A trusted introducer (the certificate authority, or CA, model).} A small set of authorities you have decided to trust sign statements of the form ``key $K$ belongs to \texttt{bank.example}.'' Your software ships with those authorities' public keys preinstalled. Anyone can then prove ownership of their key by presenting such a signed statement. This is the public web's model, and the focus of most of this chapter.
\item \textbf{A web of trust.} There are no central authorities. Instead, \emph{everyone} can sign everyone else's public keys, and you decide whom to believe by tracing chains of personal endorsements. Pretty Good Privacy (PGP), an email-encryption system introduced in 1991, popularized this design (Section~\ref{ch24:wot}).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{historybox}
Loren Kohnfelder invented the digital certificate in his 1978 MIT bachelor's thesis under Leonard Adleman, proposing that a trusted authority sign a record binding a user's name to a public key. A decade later, both the phrase ``public-key infrastructure'' and the X.509 certificate format grew out of the 1988 X.500 directory project, an effort by the Consultative Committee for International Telegraphy and Telephony (CCITT, now part of the International Telecommunication Union, ITU) to build a single global directory of people and organizations. X.500 itself never saw wide deployment, yet its certificate format, X.509, became the standard for TLS, Secure/Multipurpose Internet Mail Extensions (S/MIME, for encrypted and signed email), code signing (verifying software packages), and smart cards. The data structure your browser checks hundreds of times a day was designed for a 1980s phone-company directory that was never built. The version used today, X.509v3 (1999), added the \emph{extensions} mechanism that kept the format adaptable.
\end{historybox}

\section{A certificate is a signed statement}
\label{ch24:cert-as-signature}

Without the formatting, a certificate is one sentence with a signature under it:

\begin{center}
\itshape ``The public key $pk$ belongs to the entity named $N$, and this is true until time $T$. --- signed, the CA.''
\end{center}

Formally, let the certificate authority have its own signature key pair $(pk_{\mathrm{CA}}, sk_{\mathrm{CA}})$. A certificate for subject name $N$ with public key $pk_N$ and expiration time $T$ combines a ``to-be-signed'' body $m$ with the CA's signature $\sigma$:
\[
\mathrm{cert} \;=\; \bigl(\, \underbrace{N \concat pk_N \concat T \concat \dots}_{\text{body } m} \,,\; \sigma \,\bigr),
\qquad
\sigma = \Sign(sk_{\mathrm{CA}}, m).
\]
To check the certificate, you run $\Vrfy(pk_{\mathrm{CA}}, m, \sigma)$. If verification succeeds and you trust the CA, you accept $pk_N$ as $N$'s public key (Figure~\ref{fig:ch24-binding}). You no longer need to know Bob's key in advance. Knowing a single key $pk_{\mathrm{CA}}$ lets you authenticate the keys of millions of websites you have never visited. A starting key accepted without a certificate of its own is called a \emph{trust anchor}: all other keys derive their authenticity from it.

\begin{whybox}
Why is forging a certificate hard? Forging $\sigma$ means forging a signature under $sk_{\mathrm{CA}}$, which by the unforgeability of the signature scheme (Chapter~22) is infeasible without $sk_{\mathrm{CA}}$. Eve can write any body $m$ she likes, such as ``$pk_E$ belongs to \texttt{bank.example},'' yet she cannot produce a $\sigma$ that verifies under $pk_{\mathrm{CA}}$. The security of the web's identity layer reduces to two conditions: (1)~the signature scheme is unforgeable, and (2)~CAs guard their private keys and sign only true statements. Condition~(1) is mathematics we can prove. Condition~(2) is an operational, human, and political problem, and it is where real-world failures have occurred.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{A toy certificate with tiny numbers}

We sign one by hand using textbook RSA signatures (Chapter~19) with small numbers. Real keys are thousands of bits long; ours are a dozen bits.

Let the CA pick primes $p = 61$ and $q = 53$, giving modulus $n = pq = 3233$ and Euler's totient $\varphi(n) = (p-1)(q-1) = 60 \cdot 52 = 3120$. Choose public exponent $e = 17$, which is coprime to $3120$. The private exponent $d$ satisfies $ed \equiv 1 \pmod{3120}$; running the extended Euclidean algorithm gives $d = 2753$ (check: $17 \cdot 2753 = 46801 = 15 \cdot 3120 + 1$). Thus
\[
pk_{\mathrm{CA}} = (n, e) = (3233, 17), \qquad sk_{\mathrm{CA}} = d = 2753.
\]

Now suppose the body of Bob's certificate hashes to the integer $m = 100$. (A real certificate hashes its encoded byte representation with SHA-256; here we suppose that hash digest is $100$.) The CA signs by raising $m$ to $d$ modulo $n$:
\[
\sigma = m^{d} \bmod n = 100^{2753} \bmod 3233.
\]
Computing this by square-and-multiply gives $\sigma = 1391$. Bob's certificate consists of the body (hashing to $m = 100$) together with the signature $\sigma = 1391$.

A browser that already trusts $pk_{\mathrm{CA}} = (3233,17)$ verifies by raising $\sigma$ to $e$ modulo $n$:
\[
\sigma^{e} \bmod n = 1391^{17} \bmod 3233 = 100 = m.
\]
It recomputes the hash of the body, gets $100$, sees that it matches $\sigma^e \bmod n$, and accepts. Eve, who knows $(n,e)$ without $d$, cannot find a $\sigma'$ satisfying $(\sigma')^{17} \equiv 100 \pmod{3233}$ without inverting RSA.

\begin{trapbox}
Textbook RSA signatures like the toy above are dangerously malleable: given signatures on $m_1$ and $m_2$, an attacker gets a signature on $m_1 m_2 \bmod n$ by multiplying them, and can forge on chosen values by working backwards from $\sigma$. Never sign a raw message this way. Production certificates use a padded, randomized RSA scheme such as the RSA Probabilistic Signature Scheme (RSASSA-PSS) or elliptic-curve signatures such as ECDSA or Ed25519 (Chapter~22). The toy example illustrates only the flow: the CA signs the body with $sk_{\mathrm{CA}}$, and the verifier checks with $pk_{\mathrm{CA}}$.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Inside an X.509 certificate}
\label{ch24:x509}

The sentence ``$pk$ belongs to $N$ until $T$'' must be written in a format every implementation agrees on, byte for byte, or signatures will not verify. That format is X.509, specified in \textbf{Abstract Syntax Notation One} (ASN.1, a schema language for describing structured data fields) and serialized into bytes using \textbf{Distinguished Encoding Rules} (DER, a strict encoding that produces one canonical byte sequence per value so signer and verifier always hash identical bytes). Tools decode those bytes into the fields shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ch24-x509-anatomy}.

Several of these fields are security-critical, and bugs in checking them have caused major vulnerabilities.

\textbf{Subject, Distinguished Names, and the Common Name trap.} Both \texttt{Issuer} and \texttt{Subject} are written as \emph{Distinguished Names} (DNs): structured lists of attribute--value pairs inherited from X.500, such as \texttt{C=US, O=Bob's Bank, CN=bank.example}. Historically an entity's name lived in the DN's \emph{Common Name} (CN), a free-text attribute meant for human-readable labels like ``Bob's Bank, Inc.'' Early browsers also matched the website's domain name against the CN, an error that enabled parser-confusion attacks (Section~\ref{ch24:misissuance}). Modern certificates put server hostnames in the \textbf{Subject Alternative Name} (SAN) extension, a structured list of Domain Name System (DNS --- the Internet directory mapping domain names to numerical network addresses) names such as \texttt{bank.example} and \texttt{www.bank.example}. Browsers since 2017 ignore the CN and match \emph{only} the SAN.

\textbf{Basic Constraints: \texttt{cA = TRUE/FALSE}.} This single true/false flag states whether the certificate's key is allowed to sign \emph{other} certificates. A \emph{leaf certificate} (also called an \emph{end-entity} certificate, sitting at the bottom tip of the signing tree for a specific website) has \texttt{cA = FALSE}: it can authenticate a TLS server, never mint child certificates. A CA certificate has \texttt{cA = TRUE}. Skipping this check caused a severe chain-forgery vulnerability (Section~\ref{ch24:misissuance}).

\textbf{Key Usage and Extended Key Usage.} These extensions restrict what operations the key may perform: signing certificates (\texttt{keyCertSign}), authenticating a TLS server, signing software packages, or encrypting email. A certificate issued for TLS server authentication must be rejected if presented to sign code.

\textbf{Authority Information Access (AIA) and revocation pointers.} These extensions carry URLs telling the verifier where to download the issuer's own certificate (via AIA) and where to check whether this certificate has been cancelled before its expiration date --- either by fetching a \emph{Certificate Revocation List} (CRL) or querying the \emph{Online Certificate Status Protocol} (OCSP), both examined in Section~\ref{ch24:revocation}.

\begin{implementbox}
OpenSSL can fetch and decode a live certificate from the command line. The command below is \emph{for inspection only}; never parse security-critical certificates with hand-written string code, use a vetted TLS library.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
# Connect to example.com on port 443 (standard HTTPS port) and print its cert:
openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -servername example.com </dev/null 2>/dev/null \
  | openssl x509 -noout -text

# Key fields in the output:
#   Issuer:  (which CA signed it)
#   Subject: (whose key it is)
#   Validity Not Before / Not After
#   X509v3 Subject Alternative Name:  DNS:example.com, DNS:www.example.com
#   X509v3 Basic Constraints: CA:FALSE
#   X509v3 Extended Key Usage: TLS Web Server Authentication
\end{lstlisting}
The \texttt{-text} flag renders the signed DER bytes in human-readable form.
\end{implementbox}

\section{Chains of trust}
\label{ch24:chains}

If a single CA had to sign every certificate on Earth with its root key, that key would sit on an internet-connected machine, busy all day and one breach away from disaster. Instead, CAs use \emph{delegation}, expressed through certificates of its own.

At the top sits a \textbf{root CA}. Its certificate is self-signed: the issuer and subject are identical, and it signs the certificate with its own private key. A self-signed signature proves nothing cryptographically, since anyone can sign their own key. Roots are trusted because their public keys are \emph{preinstalled in your browser or operating system}, admitted into a \emph{root store} by a vendor root program (Mozilla, Apple, Microsoft, or Google) after independent audits. All trust in the hierarchy originates in that local root store.

The root rarely signs website certificates directly. Instead it signs one or more \textbf{intermediate CA} certificates (with \texttt{cA = TRUE}), and those online intermediates sign the millions of \textbf{leaf} certificates for individual websites (Figure~\ref{ch24:fig-chain}). The root's private key stays powered off in a physical vault, brought out only a few times a year to sign or revoke intermediates.

When you connect to a website, the server sends its leaf certificate together with the intermediate certificate(s) forming the chain. Your client validates the chain from bottom to top:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Verify the leaf's signature using the intermediate's public key.
\item Verify the intermediate's signature using the root's public key.
\item Check that the root is in your local trust store.
\item For \emph{every} link, check: the current time falls inside \texttt{notBefore} and \texttt{notAfter}; each certificate's \texttt{Issuer} matches the \texttt{Subject} of the certificate above it; every signing certificate has \texttt{cA = TRUE} and the \texttt{keyCertSign} usage; any \emph{path-length constraint} (\texttt{pathLen}, capping how many further intermediate CAs may follow below --- \texttt{pathLen = 0} allows only leaf certificates beneath it) and \emph{name constraints} (restricting which domain subtrees a CA may certify) are obeyed; and no certificate is revoked.
\item Check that the hostname you requested matches an entry in the leaf's SAN extension.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{formalbox}
A chain $C_0, C_1, \dots, C_k$ of $k+1$ certificates (from leaf $C_0$ up to root $C_k$) is \emph{valid for name $N$ at time $t$} if and only if:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\Vrfy\bigl(pk_{i+1},\ \mathrm{TBS}(C_i),\ \sigma(C_i)\bigr) = 1$ for all $i = 0,\dots,k-1$, where $\mathrm{TBS}(C_i)$ is the to-be-signed body of $C_i$, $\sigma(C_i)$ is its signature, and $pk_{i+1}$ is the public key in $C_{i+1}$;
\item $C_k$ (or its public key $pk_k$) is a preinstalled trust anchor;
\item $t \in [\texttt{notBefore}_i, \texttt{notAfter}_i]$ for every $i \in \{0,\dots,k\}$;
\item every $C_i$ with $i \ge 1$ has \texttt{BasicConstraints.cA} $=$ \texttt{TRUE} and the \texttt{keyCertSign} key-usage flag;
\item path-length and name constraints in all CA certificates $C_1,\dots,C_k$ are satisfied;
\item $N$ matches a SAN entry of the leaf $C_0$; and
\item no $C_i$ is revoked at time $t$.
\end{itemize}
Dropping \emph{any one} of these checks creates an exploitable vulnerability; missing the \texttt{cA} check is the best-known example (Section~\ref{ch24:misissuance}).
\end{formalbox}

\subsection{Tracing a tiny chain by hand}

Reuse the toy RSA CA from Section~\ref{ch24:cert-as-signature} as the \emph{root}: $pk_{\mathrm{root}} = (3233, 17)$ and $sk_{\mathrm{root}} = 2753$. Suppose an intermediate CA generates its own key pair and the hash of its certificate body is $m_{\mathrm{int}} = 920$. The root signs that body:
\[
\sigma_{\mathrm{int}} = 920^{2753} \bmod 3233 = 995.
\]
(Check: $995^{17} \bmod 3233 = 920$.) Next, the intermediate uses \emph{its own} key pair to sign Bob's leaf certificate. Give the intermediate the small RSA key from Chapter~19: $pk_{\mathrm{int}} = (55, 3)$ and $sk_{\mathrm{int}} = 27$, where $\varphi(55) = 4 \cdot 10 = 40$ and $3 \cdot 27 = 81 \equiv 1 \pmod{40}$. That public key $(55, 3)$ is the key certified inside $m_{\mathrm{int}} = 920$. The intermediate signs Bob's leaf body $m_{\mathrm{leaf}} = 12$:
\[
\sigma_{\mathrm{leaf}} = 12^{27} \bmod 55 = 23.
\]

A client holding only $pk_{\mathrm{root}} = (3233, 17)$ verifies the chain in three steps:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\sigma_{\mathrm{int}}^{\,17} \bmod 3233 = 995^{17} \bmod 3233 = 920 = m_{\mathrm{int}}$: the intermediate's certificate verifies under the root's key, establishing $pk_{\mathrm{int}} = (55,3)$.
\item $\sigma_{\mathrm{leaf}}^{\,3} \bmod 55 = 23^{3} \bmod 55 = 12167 \bmod 55 = 12 = m_{\mathrm{leaf}}$: the leaf verifies \emph{under the intermediate's key}, which uses its own modulus and exponent.
\item The root key $(3233,17)$ is in the local trust store, completing the chain.
\end{itemize}
The client knew neither Bob's key nor the intermediate's key beforehand; a single preinstalled anchor authenticated both in sequence.

\begin{walkbox}
Why not sign every leaf certificate directly with the offline root, skipping intermediates? You could, and private internal systems sometimes do. On the public web, however, bringing the root's private key out of its vault millions of times multiplies the risk of theft. Worse, if a root key ever leaked, replacing it would require shipping a software update to every browser, phone, and operating system on Earth and waiting years for unpatched devices to retire. Intermediates contain the damage: if an intermediate's key is compromised, the offline root revokes that one intermediate and signs a replacement, with no client software update needed.
\end{walkbox}

\section{Revocation: taking it back}
\label{ch24:revocation}

A certificate says ``valid until $T$,'' yet events intervene before $T$. A server's private key is stolen. An administrator with key access leaves the company. A CA discovers it issued a certificate by mistake. The certificate remains a valid signed statement whose math still verifies, yet every client in the world needs to stop trusting it \emph{now}, before its natural expiry (Figure~\ref{fig:ch24-revocation-window}).

Cancelling a certificate before $T$ is called \emph{revocation}, and it is one of the hardest engineering problems in PKI. Signature verification is offline and local, whereas revocation is a mutable fact about the present moment stored at the CA. Learning whether a certificate was revoked five minutes ago requires fresh information from outside the certificate itself.

\subsection{Certificate Revocation Lists (CRLs)}

In the earliest design, each CA publishes a signed list of the serial numbers of all unexpired certificates it has revoked. A client downloads the CRL, verifies the CA's signature on it, and rejects any certificate whose serial number appears.

\begin{intuitionbox}
A CRL is a timestamped, signed blocklist, like a booklet of cancelled card numbers a bank mails to merchants. Once downloaded, it can be checked offline. Its weakness is freshness versus size: a busy CA's CRL can grow to many megabytes because it lists \emph{every} unexpired revoked certificate. Downloading megabytes before each TLS handshake is too slow, so clients cache CRLs for hours or days --- opening the exposure window in Figure~\ref{fig:ch24-revocation-window}, during which a stolen, revoked certificate is still accepted.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{OCSP: ask about one certificate}

The Online Certificate Status Protocol (OCSP) avoids downloading the entire list by querying a single entry. The client asks a CA-operated \emph{OCSP responder} about the certificate in front of it: ``serial \texttt{4F:A3:\dots} --- good, revoked, or unknown?'' The responder returns a short, signed, timestamped status message.

OCSP responses are compact and fresh, yet live OCSP queries create three new problems:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Latency.} Querying the CA adds an extra network round-trip before the page can finish connecting.
\item \textbf{Privacy.} Because the browser contacts the OCSP responder for each site you visit, the CA can observe your browsing history in real time.
\item \textbf{Soft-fail.} What should the browser do if the responder times out? If it \emph{hard-fails} (refuses to connect), anyone who floods the responder in a denial-of-service (DoS) attack can knock websites offline. Consequently, browsers historically used \emph{soft-fail}: if the OCSP query times out, accept the certificate anyway. Against an active network attacker holding a stolen, revoked certificate, soft-fail defeats the check completely --- the attacker drops the OCSP packets and the browser proceeds.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{OCSP stapling: bring your own proof}

\textbf{OCSP stapling} fixes the latency and privacy problems by shifting the query from client to server (Figure~\ref{fig:ch24-ocsp-stapling}). The web server periodically fetches a fresh, CA-signed OCSP response for its own certificate and ``staples'' that signed response directly into its TLS handshake. The client receives both the certificate and a recent CA-signed status proof in one round-trip, without contacting the CA or revealing what site it is visiting.

Stapling alone still leaves soft-fail open: an attacker with a revoked certificate can omit the staple and rely on the browser's ``proceed anyway'' fallback. The X.509 \textbf{Must-Staple} extension closes that loophole by instructing browsers to reject the certificate whenever a valid stapled OCSP response is absent. Deployment of Must-Staple has remained sparse because an outage at the CA's OCSP responder can leave a site unable to renew its staple and lock out visitors.

\begin{applicationbox}
Major browsers have largely replaced live per-connection OCSP checks with \emph{pushed} revocation snapshots. Chrome distributes \textbf{CRLSets}: Google crawls CAs' published CRLs, extracts high-priority revocations into a compact file, and ships updates to Chrome automatically so checks run locally with zero handshake delay. Firefox ships \textbf{CRLite}, which compresses the revocation status of \emph{every} known certificate in the \emph{Web PKI} (the public-key infrastructure of browser root stores and public CAs securing websites) into a few megabytes using a cascade of \emph{Bloom filters} --- compact bit-array set summaries that never miss a true member (no \emph{false negatives}), though a single filter can occasionally flag a non-member (a \emph{false positive}). Short-lived certificates (Section~\ref{ch24:acme}) attack the problem from the other end: if a certificate expires after 90 days --- or only a few days --- the maximum revocation window shrinks accordingly.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
CRLite's Bloom-filter cascade applies the probabilistic data structures from the companion algorithms book over a \emph{known, finite} universe: all unexpired certificates recorded in Certificate Transparency logs (Section~\ref{ch24:ct}). Layer~1 stores revoked certificates; Layer~2 stores the known non-revoked certificates that triggered false positives in Layer~1; further tiny layers alternate until zero false positives remain across the entire universe. Birthday-bound and false-positive-rate calculations size each layer so the complete cascade fits in a few megabytes.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{When the trust breaks: CA mis-issuance}
\label{ch24:misissuance}

When a CA signs a certificate for the wrong party or with invalid fields, the failure is called \emph{mis-issuance}. The Web PKI has a structural vulnerability to it: \textbf{any} CA in your root store can vouch for \textbf{any} domain name. Hundreds of root and intermediate CAs operate across dozens of countries, and your browser accepts a certificate for \texttt{bank.example} signed by any one of them, even if the bank has never dealt with that CA. The security of name binding is therefore only as strong as the \emph{weakest} trusted CA.

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{The null-prefix / CN confusion attack.} In X.509's DER encoding, each string carries an explicit byte count up front (a \emph{length-prefixed} string, such as ``34 bytes follow''). Many early TLS clients, however, were written in C, where strings have no stored length and instead stop at the first zero byte (written \texttt{\textbackslash 0} and called a \emph{NUL byte}). In 2009 Moxie Marlinspike exploited this mismatch by requesting a certificate for a subdomain he controlled under \texttt{attacker.example}, with an embedded NUL byte inside the Common Name:
\[
\text{\texttt{www.bank.example\textbackslash 0.attacker.example}}
\]
A CA reading all 34 bytes saw a subdomain of \texttt{attacker.example}, verified that Marlinspike controlled \texttt{attacker.example}, and signed the certificate (Figure~\ref{fig:ch24-null-prefix}). A C-based browser reading the Common Name stopped at \texttt{\textbackslash 0} and saw \texttt{www.bank.example}, accepting the certificate as valid for the bank. The remedies: stop matching hostnames against the free-text CN, match only structured SAN entries, and reject certificates containing NUL bytes.
\end{attackbox}

\textbf{The missing \texttt{Basic Constraints} check.} Only certificates with \texttt{cA = TRUE} may sign other certificates. The client verifies every signature along the chain \texttt{bank.example} $\leftarrow$ \texttt{attacker.example} $\leftarrow$ intermediate CA $\leftarrow$ root CA, and accepts. \end{attackbox}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{DigiNotar, 2011: a compromised CA.} Attackers breached the Dutch CA DigiNotar and used its signing servers to issue hundreds of fraudulent certificates, including a \emph{wildcard certificate} for \texttt{*.google.com} (where \texttt{*} matches any subdomain, such as \texttt{mail.google.com}). That certificate was used to intercept traffic from roughly 300{,}000 Iranian Gmail users, whose browsers showed a valid padlock throughout the attack. Every signature was valid because the CA itself had been subverted. Browser vendors removed DigiNotar from their root stores within days, and the company went bankrupt within weeks. DigiNotar demonstrated that a single compromised CA endangers every domain on the web, directly motivating Certificate Transparency (Section~\ref{ch24:ct}).
\end{attackbox}

\begin{whybox}
Why does one bad CA endanger everyone when hundreds of honest CAs exist? Trust in the CA model is a logical \emph{OR}, not an \emph{AND}: a certificate is accepted if \emph{any} trusted CA signed it. Security equals that of the weakest trusted CA, not the strongest or the average. Adding more CAs expands the set of private keys capable of forging a certificate for your bank. Defenses added since --- pinning, CAA records, name constraints, and Certificate Transparency, discussed next --- all restrict or audit that open-ended OR without discarding the CA model.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{Partial fixes before transparency}

\textbf{HTTP Public Key Pinning (HPKP)} let a website send an HTTP header instructing browsers to accept only a specified set of public keys for that domain on future visits, narrowing the any-CA OR down to an explicit allowlist. It proved hazardous in practice: if an operator rotated or lost the pinned keys by mistake, returning browsers refused to connect until the pin expired months later (``HPKP suicide''). Browsers deprecated HPKP.

\textbf{Certification Authority Authorization (CAA) records} let a domain publish in DNS which CAs are authorized to issue certificates for it --- for example, the record \texttt{bank.example.\allowbreak\ CAA\allowbreak\ 0\allowbreak\ issue\allowbreak\ "goodca.example"} permits only \texttt{goodca.example} to issue for \texttt{bank.example}. Root-program rules require CAs to query CAA before issuing and refuse if unlisted. A compromised CA can ignore CAA, so it does not stop an attacker inside a CA, yet it blocks accidental mis-issuance by compliant CAs.

\textbf{Name constraints} let a root restrict an intermediate CA to specific domain suffixes (such as ``this intermediate may issue only under \texttt{*.gov.uk}''). When enforced by clients, they contain the damage of a compromised intermediate CA to those domains.

\section{Certificate Transparency}
\label{ch24:ct}

A major reason the DigiNotar attack lasted weeks was that the forgery stayed \emph{invisible}. Iranian users were intercepted via a man-in-the-middle attack, while Google had no way to see that a \texttt{*.google.com} certificate had been minted. \textbf{Certificate Transparency} (CT) solves this visibility gap: rather than trying to prevent a CA from signing a bad certificate, CT makes every issued certificate \emph{public and undeniable}.

Every certificate a CA issues must be recorded in a public, append-only, cryptographically verifiable \textbf{log}, and browsers reject certificates that lack proof of logging. Domain owners such as Google or \texttt{bank.example}, along with independent monitors, watch those logs continuously and receive an alert the moment an unexpected CA logs a certificate for their domain.

\begin{intuitionbox}
CT does not make CAs trustworthy; it makes them \emph{accountable}, much like a vault camera records a theft rather than physically blocking the door. Turning ``append-only public ledger'' from an operator's promise into a verifiable proof relies on one data structure: the \emph{Merkle tree}.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Merkle trees: proving membership and append-only-ness}

A CT log is a growing list of certificates that must satisfy two properties: (1)~a verifier can check ``certificate $c$ is in this log'' without downloading millions of entries, and (2)~the log operator cannot \emph{rewrite history} by deleting or altering an already logged certificate. A binary hash tree called a \textbf{Merkle tree} provides both (Figure~\ref{ch24:fig-ct-tree}).

Each logged certificate $c_i$ is hashed with a cryptographic hash function $\Hash$ to form a leaf $h_i = \Hash(c_i)$. Adjacent nodes are concatenated and hashed in pairs up the tree until reaching a single \textbf{root hash} $R$. The log periodically signs $(R, T, N)$ --- the root hash $R$, timestamp $T$, and leaf count $N$ --- publishing a \textbf{Signed Tree Head} (STH) that commits to those exact $N$ certificates in order.

\caption{Certificate Transparency Merkle tree audit proof. To prove that certificate $c_2$ is recorded in the log, the log returns the sibling hashes along the path to the root (orange). The auditor hashes upward from $\Hash(c_2)$ and checks that the resulting root $R'$ matches the Signed Tree Head.}
\label{ch24:fig-ct-tree}
\end{figure}

To prove that certificate $c_2$ is in the log, the log supplies the \textbf{audit path}: the sibling hashes needed to recompute the root, here $h_1$ and $H_{34}$. The verifier computes
\[
\Hash\bigl(\Hash(h_1 \concat \Hash(c_2)) \concat H_{34}\bigr)
\]
and checks that it equals the signed root $R$. In a log of $N$ certificates, the audit path contains at most $\lceil \log_2 N \rceil$ hashes --- 30 hashes (960 bytes with SHA-256) even for $N = 10^9$ certificates.

\begin{whybox}
Why can't the log cheat? The signed root hash $R$ commits to the entire tree under a collision-resistant hash $\Hash$ (Chapter~14). Fabricating an audit path for an unlogged certificate $c_2$ that still hashes to $R$ would require finding a collision or second preimage (two distinct inputs with the same hash) in $\Hash$. Likewise, deleting or modifying an earlier entry after publishing a Signed Tree Head would change the root. Between any earlier tree of $m$ certificates and a later tree of $n$ certificates ($m < n$), CT also defines a logarithmic-size \textbf{consistency proof} showing that the size-$n$ tree contains the exact size-$m$ tree as a prefix with $n - m$ new entries appended. External auditors check consistency proofs continuously; a log that ever fails one is removed from browsers.
\end{whybox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Merkle trees are the same authenticated data structure behind Git commit histories, BitTorrent piece verification, and blockchain block headers (Chapter~36). Here the tree provides a direct security reduction: ``the log cannot silently alter or omit entries'' reduces to ``$\Hash$ is collision-resistant,'' the property sized via birthday bounds in Chapter~14.
\end{connectionbox}

\subsection{How CT plugs into TLS}

When a CA submits a certificate to a CT log, the log returns a \textbf{Signed Certificate Timestamp} (SCT): a signed promise to merge the certificate into its Merkle tree within a fixed deadline called the \emph{Maximum Merge Delay} (typically 24 hours). Browsers require every publicly trusted certificate to arrive with SCTs from multiple independent logs --- embedded inside an X.509 extension, sent in the TLS handshake, or attached via OCSP stapling --- and reject the connection otherwise (Figure~\ref{fig:ch24-ct-flow}). The division of labor is clear: the browser checks only that the certificate was \emph{logged} (via SCTs), while domain monitors scanning the public logs check whether the certificate was \emph{legitimate}. Since Chrome and Safari made CT mandatory in 2018, public search engines such as \texttt{crt.sh} index virtually every publicly trusted certificate on the web.

CT monitoring has caught real mis-issuance across the industry: certificates with malformed extensions, certificates issued for domains the requester did not control, and accidental leaks of internal corporate hostnames. Persistent CT findings also uncovered systematic validation failures at Symantec's CA division, leading browser vendors to distrust Symantec's roots in 2017--2018. Public, auditable logs replaced unverifiable self-reporting as the basis for keeping CAs in a root store.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Let's Encrypt and ACME: automating trust}
\label{ch24:acme}

For two decades, obtaining a certificate meant paying a CA, emailing paperwork, and pasting key files into web forms. Because setting up HTTPS was slow and manual, much of the web ran on unencrypted HTTP. Launched in 2015 as a nonprofit CA, Let's Encrypt made certificates free and automatic, helping move the web from mostly HTTP to mostly HTTPS within a few years.

The protocol behind that automation is \textbf{ACME} (Automatic Certificate Management Environment), through which server software proves domain control and obtains a certificate without human intervention.

\subsection{Domain validation: proving you control the name}

A CA must not hand \texttt{bank.example}'s certificate to an arbitrary requester. For a \emph{domain-validated} (DV) certificate, the CA checks one property: \emph{does the requester control this domain right now?} Under ACME, the client first registers an \emph{account key pair} $(pk_{\mathrm{acct}}, sk_{\mathrm{acct}})$ with the CA. The CA sends a random challenge string (\texttt{token}), and the client computes a \emph{key authorization} joining that token with a \emph{thumbprint} (a SHA-256 hash) of $pk_{\mathrm{acct}}$, then publishes the result where only the domain's operator could place it:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{HTTP-01}: serve at \texttt{http://\allowbreak bank.example/\allowbreak.well-known/\allowbreak acme-challenge/\allowbreak<token>} a file containing $\text{token} \concat \text{``\texttt{.}''} \concat \operatorname{thumbprint}(pk_{\mathrm{acct}})$. The CA fetches that URL over HTTP; only whoever controls the web server for \texttt{bank.example} can place files there.
\item \textbf{DNS-01}: publish a DNS text (\texttt{TXT}) record at \texttt{\_acme-challenge.\allowbreak bank.example} holding the SHA-256 hash of that same key authorization. Only someone who controls the domain's DNS zone can add that record, which also lets DNS-01 validate wildcard names like \texttt{*.bank.example}.
\item \textbf{TLS-ALPN-01}: using TLS \emph{Application-Layer Protocol Negotiation} (ALPN, a handshake extension that selects the application protocol), answer a dedicated TLS handshake on port~443 (the standard HTTPS port) with a self-signed certificate carrying the hash of the key authorization.
\end{enumerate}

Binding the response to $pk_{\mathrm{acct}}$ via the thumbprint prevents a network eavesdropper who sees \texttt{token} from claiming the challenge under their own account key. Once the CA verifies the challenge, the client submits a \textbf{Certificate Signing Request} (CSR) --- a signed message containing the domain name and the server's public key, proving possession of the matching private key --- and the CA returns the signed certificate in seconds (Figure~\ref{fig:ch24-acme}).

\begin{trapbox}
Domain validation proves \emph{control of the domain name}, nothing more. A DV certificate for \texttt{bank-of-america-login.example} certifies only that its holder controls that lookalike domain, not that the site belongs to a real bank. The browser padlock means ``your connection to this domain name is encrypted and authenticated,'' never ``this business is honest.'' Phishing sites routinely obtain free DV certificates for deceptive domains. Higher-tier \emph{Organization-Validated} (OV) and \emph{Extended-Validation} (EV) certificates add checks of corporate registration records, though browsers removed the special EV company-name badge from the address bar after studies showed users did not notice when it disappeared.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{implementbox}
With an ACME client such as \texttt{certbot}, requesting and renewing a production certificate takes one command. Check your CA's rate limits before scripting against production endpoints, and keep private keys on the server that uses them.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
# Using certbot (a standard ACME client) with the HTTP-01 challenge.
# Generates a key pair, proves domain control, downloads the certificate,
# and installs a scheduled background task (cron job) to renew before 90 days.
sudo certbot certonly --standalone -d bank.example -d www.bank.example

# Exact HTTP-01 key authorization (JWK = JSON Web Key format; base64url = URL-safe Base64):
#   key_authorization = token || "." || base64url(SHA256(account_public_key_JWK))
# served at http://bank.example/.well-known/acme-challenge/<token>
\end{lstlisting}
Automation is what lets Let's Encrypt issue \emph{short-lived} (90-day, and increasingly shorter) certificates: when renewal is automatic, frequent expiry causes no operational pain while shrinking the revocation exposure window from Section~\ref{ch24:revocation}.
\end{implementbox}

\section{The alternative: web of trust}
\label{ch24:wot}

Where the CA model concentrates trust in a few hundred root authorities, Pretty Good Privacy (PGP, created by Phil Zimmermann in 1991) took the opposite route: eliminate central authorities and let individuals vouch for one another directly. This decentralized graph is the \textbf{web of trust}.

In PGP, any user can sign another person's public key to state ``I verified that this key belongs to this person.'' If you meet Bob, check his key's hash fingerprint in person, and sign his key, anyone who trusts \emph{you} as a careful verifier can rely on your signature to accept Bob's key (Figure~\ref{fig:ch24-wot}).

Each user configures local \emph{trust levels}: for example, accepting a key signed by one fully trusted introducer or by two marginally trusted ones. The underlying cryptography is the same digital signature verification used in X.509; what changes is the trust \emph{topology}, replacing a shallow tree rooted at preinstalled CAs with a peer-to-peer social graph.

Zimmermann released PGP as free software in 1991, partly in reaction to proposed US legislation mandating government decryption access. Because US law then regulated strong cryptography as munitions, federal prosecutors investigated Zimmermann for arms export after PGP spread abroad. To establish a legal defense, MIT Press published the complete PGP source code as a printed hardcover book --- protected under the First Amendment for lawful export, then scanned back into computers overseas --- and the investigation closed without charges in 1996. From its origin, the web of trust expressed a political principle alongside a technical one: secure communication should not depend on institutional gatekeepers.

\begin{whybox}
The web of trust's main advantage and main limitation come from the same design choice. \textbf{Advantage}: there is no single point of failure, no DigiNotar capable of forging for everyone, and no central authority to breach or coerce. \textbf{Limitation}: personal endorsement chains do not scale to strangers. When you visit an online store run by people outside your social network, no short chain of in-person key signings connects you to them. The CA model won on the public web because preinstalled trust anchors let you authenticate millions of strangers immediately, leaving the PGP web of trust primarily to software signing (such as Linux package maintainers) and small communities.
\end{whybox}

\begin{applicationbox}
Modern encrypted messengers such as Signal, WhatsApp, and iMessage combine ideas from all three binding models. By default they use TOFU with alerts: your phone accepts a contact's key on first message and warns you if that key changes, while offering optional out-of-band \emph{safety numbers} (verified by scanning a QR barcode in person) for high-risk conversations. Increasingly they also add \emph{key transparency} (deployed in WhatsApp and Apple's iMessage Contact Key Verification, building on Keybase): the service records every username-to-key mapping in an append-only Merkle tree, applying Section~\ref{ch24:ct}'s Certificate Transparency design to messaging keys so silent key substitution becomes detectable.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Putting it together: what your browser does}
\label{ch24:together}

When you type \texttt{https://bank.example} and press Enter, the checks developed across this chapter run in milliseconds:

\begin{enumerate}
\item The server sends its leaf certificate and intermediate CA certificate(s) during the TLS handshake.
\item Your browser builds a chain from the leaf up to a root in its local root store, verifying each signature under the public key above it.
\item It checks every link: validity timestamps, \texttt{Issuer}/\allowbreak\texttt{Subject} matching, \texttt{cA = TRUE} and the \texttt{keyCertSign} usage on all CA certificates, plus path-length and name constraints.
\item It checks that \texttt{bank.example} matches an entry in the leaf's SAN extension.
\item It checks revocation status, typically against a locally pushed snapshot (CRLSet or CRLite) or a stapled OCSP response.
\item It verifies valid \textbf{SCTs} proving the certificate was submitted to Certificate Transparency logs.
\item Only after every check passes does the browser use the leaf's public key to authenticate the ephemeral Diffie--Hellman key exchange and display the padlock.
\end{enumerate}

The mathematical primitives from earlier chapters --- hashes, signatures, and Diffie--Hellman key exchange --- carry proofs of security under clear hardness assumptions. Binding keys to real-world names and revoking stolen keys cannot be solved by math alone, which is why PKI requires careful engineering around fallible authorities, cached state, and public audit logs.

\begin{recoverybox}
The chapter in brief: a certificate is a signed claim binding a public key to a name, and certificate chains reduce trusting millions of servers to trusting a small local store of root keys. Real-world Web PKI failures have come not from broken signature math, but from parser bugs, skipped constraint checks, and CAs signing what they should not --- motivating CAA, short-lived ACME certificates, and public Merkle-tree logs in Certificate Transparency.
\end{recoverybox}

With symmetric ciphers, hashes, MACs, signatures, key exchange, and certificates in place, we have all the pieces of a real-world secure channel. In Chapter~25, we see how Transport Layer Security (TLS) combines them to verify a server's certificate, agree on forward-secret session keys, and encrypt web traffic.

\section*{Exercises}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Sign and verify a toy certificate]
Using the toy CA with $n = 3233$, $e = 17$, $d = 2753$, suppose Bob's certificate body hashes to $m = 65$. Compute the CA's signature $\sigma = m^d \bmod n$ by square-and-multiply, then verify by computing $\sigma^e \bmod n$ and checking it equals $65$. Show your intermediate squarings.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Why the chain order matters]
A client receives a chain (leaf, intermediate, root) but validates it \emph{top-down}, checking the root is trusted first and then descending, and forgets to verify that each certificate's issuer field equals the subject of the one above it. Describe a concrete forgery: how can an attacker who owns one legitimately-signed leaf splice in a certificate of their choosing? Which check in Section~\ref{ch24:chains} stops it?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The \texttt{cA} bit, exploited]
Spell out, step by step, the attack from Section~\ref{ch24:misissuance} in which a client fails to check \texttt{BasicConstraints}. Start from the attacker buying a normal leaf certificate for \texttt{attacker.example} and end with the client accepting a forged certificate for \texttt{bank.example}. At which exact step would a correct \texttt{cA} check abort the chain?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Merkle audit path]
A CT log has eight certificates $c_1,\dots,c_8$ at the leaves of a balanced Merkle tree (leaf $i$ is $h_i = \Hash(c_i)$, parents are hashes of concatenated children). Write the exact audit path (the list of sibling hashes, in order from leaf to root) needed to prove that $c_5$ is in the log, and write the sequence of hash computations a verifier performs to recover the root.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Revocation tradeoffs]
Compare CRLs, plain OCSP, and OCSP stapling along four axes: freshness, client privacy, latency added to the handshake, and behavior under an active network attacker who can drop packets. For each mechanism, identify the single property that makes it inadequate \emph{on its own}, and explain why ``Must-Staple'' is needed to close the gap.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Soft-fail is self-defeating]
Explain precisely why an active man-in-the-middle holding a \emph{stolen but revoked} certificate is undisturbed by a browser that does OCSP checking with soft-fail. Then argue why hard-fail OCSP is not a usable fix on its own, and how short-lived certificates and pushed revocation sets (CRLSet/CRLite) sidestep the dilemma.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[ACME challenge binding]
In the HTTP-01 challenge, the published value is
\[
\text{token} \concat \text{``\texttt{.}''} \concat \text{base64url}\bigl(\Hash(\text{account public key})\bigr),
\]
rather than the token alone. Construct the attack that would succeed if the response were \emph{only} the bare token (think about a passive eavesdropper on the network, or a shared-hosting neighbor), and explain how binding to the account key defeats it.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[DV is not identity]
A user sees a valid padlock on \texttt{https://\allowbreak secure-\allowbreak bank-\allowbreak login.\allowbreak example} and concludes the site is their bank. Exactly what does the certificate prove, and what does it not prove? Explain why a DV certificate for this domain is trivial for a phisher to obtain, and what additional mechanism (if any) could have warned the user.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Trust is an OR]
Argue formally why, in the CA model, adding a new trusted CA can only \emph{decrease} or hold constant the security of the name-to-key binding for an existing domain, never increase it. Then explain how CAA records and name constraints reintroduce an \emph{AND}-like restriction, and why CT helps even though it prevents nothing.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Web of trust at scale]
Estimate, with a back-of-envelope model, how many pairwise key-signings would be needed for a PGP-style web of trust to give every pair of $N$ Internet users a short verification path (say, length $\le 3$). Compare with the CA model's requirement (each user trusts a constant number of roots). Use this to explain, quantitatively, why the web of trust never scaled to the public web. (Hint: think about the diameter and density of random graphs from the probability companion.)
\end{exercise}
