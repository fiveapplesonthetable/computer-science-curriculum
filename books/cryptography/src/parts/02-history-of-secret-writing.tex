\chapter{A Short History of Secret Writing}

In Chapter~1, we saw that modern cryptography demands precise definitions of secrecy and computationally bounded adversaries. Before building the mathematics of those modern guarantees, we need to understand where they came from -- and why intuitive attempts to hide secrets failed. For most of human history, hiding a message meant moving letters around. There were no
computers, no notion of a key space (the set of all possible keys) measured in bits, and no proofs. Clever
people on one side tried to keep a secret, and equally clever people on the other side
tried to read it. Every cipher in this
chapter was, in its day, considered strong by serious people, and every one of them was
eventually torn open. The way each one fell teaches a lesson that survives into the modern
schemes we build later in this book: where the secret lives, why reusing it is fatal,
and why ``it looks scrambled to me'' is not a security argument.

Working these classic breaks by hand -- counting letter frequencies, spotting repeated
patterns, and testing guessed words -- builds the habits you need to reason about modern
attacks. The mathematical tools change; the way an attacker thinks does not.

\section{The cast and the rules of the game}
\label{ch02:cast}

Recall from Chapter~1 our cast of characters and the four standard threat models. \textbf{Alice} wants to send a message to \textbf{Bob}; \textbf{Eve} sits on the wire between them and copies down everything that passes. Alice and Bob share some secret in advance; Eve does not have it. The question is always the same: can Eve recover the message anyway?

It helps to view classical ciphers in terms of these threat models, because almost every historical failure came from underestimating Eve's access.

\begin{definition}[Attack models in historical context]
\label{ch02:attack-models}
In Chapter~1, we classified an attacker by what information she commands. In historical cryptanalysis, these models emerge in direct sequence:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Ciphertext-only.} Eve sees only encrypted messages. This was the default position for ancient military couriers and classical frequency analysis.
  \item \emph{Known-plaintext.} Eve sees some plaintext/ciphertext pairs (for example, she guesses that a message ends ``Heil Hitler'' or begins ``WEATHER REPORT'').
  \item \emph{Chosen-plaintext.} Eve can get messages of her choosing encrypted under the unknown key.
  \item \emph{Chosen-ciphertext.} Eve can get ciphertexts of her choosing decrypted (or at least learn whether they decrypt validly).
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{focusbox}
A modern scheme must survive chosen-plaintext (and usually chosen-ciphertext) attacks.
Every classical cipher in this chapter dies to a ciphertext-only attack -- the weakest model
there is. Keep that gap in mind; it measures how far the field traveled.
\end{focusbox}

One design rule governs every failure that follows: Kerckhoffs's principle.

\begin{formalbox}
\textbf{Kerckhoffs's principle (1883).} A cipher should remain secure even if everything
about the system except the key is public knowledge. The Dutch linguist Auguste Kerckhoffs
laid out six rules for military ciphers in the \emph{Journal des sciences militaires}; this
is the one that endured. Claude Shannon restated it crisply: ``the enemy knows the system.''
The practical reading: do not bet your secret on the secrecy of your \emph{method}. Methods
leak, get captured, get reverse-engineered, or get sold. Only the key should be secret, and
the key should be easy to change. Nearly every disaster in this chapter is a violation of
this rule.
\end{formalbox}

\section{The Caesar cipher: shift and pray}
\label{ch02:caesar}

The oldest cipher most people know is named for Julius Caesar, who according to Suetonius
shifted each letter of his military dispatches three places down the alphabet. \texttt{A}
becomes \texttt{D}, \texttt{B} becomes \texttt{E}, and so on, wrapping \texttt{X}, \texttt{Y},
\texttt{Z} around to \texttt{A}, \texttt{B}, \texttt{C}.

To write this down mathematically, number the alphabet $\texttt{A}=0, \texttt{B}=1,
\ldots, \texttt{Z}=25$, and let $m$, $c$, and $k$ in $\{0, 1, \ldots, 25\}$ stand for the
plaintext letter, the ciphertext letter, and the secret shift. Encryption and decryption
then use \emph{arithmetic modulo $26$} -- dividing by $26$ and keeping the remainder in
$\{0, 1, \ldots, 25\}$, so $26$ wraps back to $0$, $27$ to $1$, and $-1$ to $25$:
\[
  c \equiv m + k \pmod{26}, \qquad m \equiv c - k \pmod{26},
\]
where the congruence symbol $\equiv$ with $\pmod{26}$ means the two sides leave the same
remainder when divided by $26$ (Chapter~3 develops modular arithmetic in full). Caesar used
$k=3$. The key is the single number $k$, and there are only $26$ possible shifts (one of
which, $k=0$, leaves the message unchanged).

\begin{example}[Encrypting by hand]
\label{ch02:caesar-example}
Take the plaintext \texttt{ATTACK} and $k=3$. Here $(m + 3) \bmod 26$ denotes the remainder
in $\{0, \ldots, 25\}$ when $m + 3$ is divided by $26$:
\[
\begin{array}{c|cccccc}
\text{plain}  & \texttt{A} & \texttt{T} & \texttt{T} & \texttt{A} & \texttt{C} & \texttt{K}\\
m             & 0 & 19 & 19 & 0 & 2 & 10\\
(m+3) \bmod 26  & 3 & 22 & 22 & 3 & 5 & 13\\
\text{cipher} & \texttt{D} & \texttt{W} & \texttt{W} & \texttt{D} & \texttt{F} & \texttt{N}
\end{array}
\]
So \texttt{ATTACK} encrypts to \texttt{DWWDFN}. To decrypt, subtract $3$ modulo $26$:
$\texttt{D} \to \texttt{A}$, $\texttt{W} \to \texttt{T}$, and you are back.
\end{example}

A shift cipher is easiest to picture as two concentric alphabet rings: line up the plaintext
\texttt{A} on the inner ring with the ciphertext letter $k$ positions around on the outer ring,
and reading off any plaintext letter gives its ciphertext. Figure~\ref{ch02:fig-caesar-wheel}
shows the $k=3$ alignment of Example~\ref{ch02:caesar-example}.

\subsection{Why it fails: there is nowhere for the secret to hide}

The Caesar cipher has a key space of size $26$. An attacker tries all of them.

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Brute force in 25 tries.} Eve intercepts \texttt{WKHWUHDVXUHLVEXULHG}. She writes out
all $25$ non-zero backward shifts of the first few letters and reads down the list for English:
\[
\begin{array}{rl}
k=1: & \texttt{VJGVTGCUWTG\dots}\\
k=2: & \texttt{UIFUSFBTVSF\dots}\\
k=3: & \texttt{THETREASURE\dots}\quad\leftarrow\text{readable}
\end{array}
\]
Shift back by $k=3$ and the message reads \texttt{THETREASUREISBURIED}. She needed no
insight into Alice's choice of key; she only needed to try $25$ shifts.
\end{attackbox}

The lesson recurs throughout cryptography: \emph{the number of possible keys must
be too large to enumerate.} Twenty-six is far too few. Even by hand a person clears all $25$
shifts in under a minute. If the key space is small, nothing else in the design matters.

\begin{connectionbox}
``Too large to enumerate'' is a statement about counting, which is the subject of the
companion combinatorics chapters. A modern $128$-bit key has $2^{128}\approx 3.4\times10^{38}$
possibilities. Even if you tested a trillion ($10^{12}$) keys per second on each of a billion
($10^{9}$) machines -- $10^{21}$ keys per second in total -- exhausting the key space would
take about $1.1\times 10^{10}$ years, roughly the $1.4\times 10^{10}$-year age of the universe.
That gap, from $26$ keys to $2^{128}$, is what makes brute force hopeless against a modern
cipher. We make this computational security model precise in Chapter~8.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{General substitution: a huge key that still loses}
\label{ch02:substitution}

The obvious fix for a tiny key space is to make it enormous. Instead of shifting the whole
alphabet by one fixed amount, pick an arbitrary \emph{permutation}: a secret one-to-one
rearrangement mapping each plaintext letter to a distinct ciphertext letter. Because a single
scrambled alphabet is used for the entire message, this is called a \emph{monoalphabetic
substitution cipher}.

A worked key might be:
\[
\begin{array}{c|cccccccccccccccccccccccccc}
\text{plain}  & \texttt{A}&\texttt{B}&\texttt{C}&\texttt{D}&\texttt{E}&\texttt{F}&\texttt{G}&\texttt{H}&\texttt{I}&\texttt{J}&\texttt{K}&\texttt{L}&\texttt{M}&\texttt{N}&\texttt{O}&\texttt{P}&\texttt{Q}&\texttt{R}&\texttt{S}&\texttt{T}&\texttt{U}&\texttt{V}&\texttt{W}&\texttt{X}&\texttt{Y}&\texttt{Z}\\
\text{cipher} & \texttt{Q}&\texttt{W}&\texttt{E}&\texttt{R}&\texttt{T}&\texttt{Y}&\texttt{U}&\texttt{I}&\texttt{O}&\texttt{P}&\texttt{A}&\texttt{S}&\texttt{D}&\texttt{F}&\texttt{G}&\texttt{H}&\texttt{J}&\texttt{K}&\texttt{L}&\texttt{Z}&\texttt{X}&\texttt{C}&\texttt{V}&\texttt{B}&\texttt{N}&\texttt{M}
\end{array}
\]
Now the key is the entire bottom row. There are $26$ choices for where \texttt{A} goes, $25$
remaining choices for \texttt{B}, $24$ for \texttt{C}, and so on down to $1$ for \texttt{Z},
giving $26$ factorial ($26! = 26 \cdot 25 \cdots 2 \cdot 1$) possible keys:
\[
  26! = 403{,}291{,}461{,}126{,}605{,}635{,}584{,}000{,}000 \approx 4 \times 10^{26}.
\]
Because $2^{88} \approx 3.1 \times 10^{26}$, that key space corresponds to about $88$ bits of
key. Brute force is hopeless -- you cannot try $4\times10^{26}$ alphabets by hand or, in
1500, by any means at all. For centuries people believed this made the cipher unbreakable.
They were wrong, and the reason is the central idea of classical cryptanalysis.

\subsection{Frequency analysis: the key space is irrelevant if the cipher leaks}

A monoalphabetic substitution maps every \texttt{E} in the plaintext to the
same ciphertext letter, every \texttt{T} to the same letter, and so on. It scrambles
\emph{which} symbol stands for \texttt{E}, but it cannot hide \emph{how often} that symbol
appears. English is lopsided (Figure~\ref{ch02:fig-freq-hist}): \texttt{E} accounts for about
$12.7\%$ of letters, \texttt{T} about $9.1\%$, and \texttt{A} about $8.2\%$, while \texttt{J},
\texttt{Q}, \texttt{X}, and \texttt{Z} are each well under $1\%$. The cipher carries this
fingerprint straight through.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Imagine a room where everyone swaps name tags while nobody changes
height. If you know the tallest person in the room is Eve, you find Eve by looking for the
tallest person, no matter what her name tag says. Letter frequencies are heights the substitution
cannot change. Count the most common ciphertext symbol; in a few paragraphs of English it is
almost always \texttt{E}. The $88$-bit key did not help, because we never had to guess it --
we read it off the statistics.
\end{intuitionbox}

\paragraph{Al-Kindi and the birth of frequency analysis (c.~850 CE).} The earliest surviving description of frequency
analysis is by the Arab polymath Abu Yusuf al-Kindi, in a manuscript on deciphering encrypted
messages written around 850 CE. His insight: count the symbols, line them up against the known
frequencies of the language, and the substitution unravels. This was the
first general cipher-breaking technique in history, predating the Renaissance European ciphers
it would later demolish by six centuries, and the first use of \emph{statistics} to break encryption.

\subsection{Breaking one by hand}
\label{ch02:break-substitution}

Suppose Eve intercepts the following ciphertext (spaces are kept here so you can follow
word boundaries; stripping them slows the manual work slightly, not the method):

\begin{quote}\ttfamily
ZG WT GK FGZ ZG WT ZIQZ OL ZIT JXTLZOGF
\end{quote}

\textbf{Step 1: count letters.} Tally every symbol (ignoring spaces, there are $30$ letters):
\[
\begin{array}{l}
\texttt{Z}: 7 \quad \texttt{G}: 5 \quad \texttt{T}: 4 \quad \texttt{W}: 2 \quad \texttt{F}: 2
\quad \texttt{I}: 2 \quad \texttt{O}: 2 \quad \texttt{L}: 2\\
\texttt{K}: 1 \quad \texttt{Q}: 1 \quad \texttt{J}: 1 \quad \texttt{X}: 1
\end{array}
\]
\texttt{Z} leads with $7$ occurrences, followed by \texttt{G} with $5$ and \texttt{T} with $4$.
In English, the leaders are \texttt{E} and \texttt{T}, with \texttt{A} and \texttt{O} close behind.

\textbf{Step 2: combine counts with word patterns.} Raw frequency gives a starting hypothesis;
short words and repeated patterns test it. In the ciphertext, the two-letter word \texttt{ZG}
appears twice and the three-letter word \texttt{ZIT} appears once. The most common English
two-letter words are \texttt{TO}, \texttt{OF}, \texttt{IN}, \texttt{IS}, \texttt{IT}, and
\texttt{BE}; the most common three-letter word is \texttt{THE}. A three-letter word starting
with the most frequent ciphertext symbol is a strong candidate for \texttt{THE}: \texttt{ZIT}
matches \texttt{THE} under $\texttt{Z} \to \texttt{T}$, $\texttt{I} \to \texttt{H}$, and
$\texttt{T} \to \texttt{E}$. That assignment agrees with the counts (\texttt{Z} first,
\texttt{T} third) and turns the repeated word \texttt{ZG} into \texttt{T?}. The only common
two-letter English word starting with \texttt{T} is \texttt{TO}, forcing $\texttt{G} \to
\texttt{O}$ -- and \texttt{G} was the second most frequent symbol, matching \texttt{O}'s high
rank in English.

\textbf{Step 3: propagate guesses.} Substitute $\texttt{Z} \to \texttt{t}$, $\texttt{I} \to
\texttt{h}$, $\texttt{T} \to \texttt{e}$, and $\texttt{G} \to \texttt{o}$ throughout, using
lowercase for recovered plaintext letters and uppercase for still-unknown ciphertext letters
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch02-constraint-prop}):
\begin{quote}\ttfamily
to We oK Fot to We thQt OL the JXeLtOoF
\end{quote}
The phrase \texttt{to We} repeats with \texttt{Fot} between the two copies. \texttt{Fot} fits
\texttt{not} ($\texttt{F} \to \texttt{n}$), and ``to be or not to be'' then identifies
$\texttt{W} \to \texttt{b}$ (making \texttt{We} into \texttt{be}) and $\texttt{K} \to
\texttt{r}$ (making \texttt{oK} into \texttt{or}). Substitute all three across the line:
\begin{quote}\ttfamily
to be or not to be thQt OL the JXeLtOon
\end{quote}
Now \texttt{thQt} has the shape \texttt{th?t}, forcing $\texttt{Q} \to \texttt{a}$
(\texttt{that}). After \texttt{the}, the eight-letter word \texttt{JXeLtOon} has pattern
\texttt{??e?t?on}, which matches \texttt{question} via $\texttt{J} \to \texttt{q}$,
$\texttt{X} \to \texttt{u}$, $\texttt{L} \to \texttt{s}$, and $\texttt{O} \to \texttt{i}$.
Those last two assignments simultaneously turn \texttt{OL} into \texttt{is}, completing the
plaintext:
\begin{quote}\ttfamily
to be or not to be that is the question
\end{quote}

We never tried even one of the $4\times10^{26}$ keys. We recovered the substitution
letter by letter, using frequencies to seed guesses and word structure to confirm or refute
them. A few hundred characters of ciphertext is plenty; with a thousand, the attack is
mechanical.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why the giant key space did not save the cipher.} Security is not the same as ``many
keys.'' The substitution cipher leaks a statistic -- the frequency of each letter -- that is
preserved exactly under the secret permutation. An attacker who can read off the answer never
has to search the key space at all. The modern restatement: a cipher is broken if the
ciphertext reveals \emph{any} information about the plaintext beyond its length. Counting
letters reveals an enormous amount. This is precisely the property Shannon would later forbid
with his definition of perfect secrecy, and which modern ciphers achieve by making the output
statistically indistinguishable from random bits.
\end{whybox}

\begin{implementbox}
A toy frequency counter. This sorts ciphertext letters by how often they appear, which is the
seed of the manual attack above.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
# LEARNING ONLY -- this is a teaching toy, never use it to protect anything.
from collections import Counter

ENGLISH = "etaoinshrdlcumwfgypbvkjxqz"  # roughly most-to-least common

def frequency_attack(ciphertext):
    letters = [c for c in ciphertext.upper() if c.isalpha()]
    counts = Counter(letters)
    # most common ciphertext letter -> guess it is 'E', and so on
    ranked = [c for c, _ in counts.most_common()]
    guess = {}
    for cipher_letter, plain_letter in zip(ranked, ENGLISH):
        guess[cipher_letter] = plain_letter
    decoded = "".join(guess.get(c, c.lower()) for c in ciphertext.upper())
    return decoded, guess

text = "ZG WT GK FGZ ZG WT ZIQZ OL ZIT JXTLZOGF"
print(frequency_attack(text))
# A first pass; you then hand-correct using word patterns, exactly as above.
\end{lstlisting}
Automated solvers add \emph{bigram} (two-letter pair, such as \texttt{TH} or \texttt{HE}) and
\emph{trigram} (three-letter sequence, such as \texttt{THE} or \texttt{ING}) statistics along
with dictionary matching, starting from this same single-letter histogram. Any scheme that
preserves letter proportions preserves the language's statistical signature.
\end{implementbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Where you still meet substitution.} The newspaper ``cryptoquote'' puzzle is a
monoalphabetic substitution, solvable by the method above. More seriously, naive ``encryption''
that maps each byte to a fixed other byte -- some legacy file formats, toy obfuscation in
malware, or \texttt{ROT13} (the Caesar shift with $k=13$, used on online forums to hide
spoilers) -- is a substitution cipher and falls to frequency analysis or known-plaintext
inspection. Whenever a system claims to ``encrypt'' yet a repeated input always produces the
same output, you are looking at a substitution-style leak. Modern block-cipher modes of
operation (Chapter~13) inject fresh randomness into every encryption to eliminate this
determinism.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{The Vigen\`ere cipher: stacking shifts to flatten the fingerprint}
\label{ch02:vigenere}

If monoalphabetic substitution fails because each plaintext letter always maps to the same
ciphertext letter, the natural fix is a \emph{polyalphabetic cipher}: one that switches among
several substitution alphabets from position to position. The Vigen\`ere cipher does this with a
repeating keyword whose length $\ell$ is called the cipher's \emph{period}. It was described by
Giovan Battista Bellaso in 1553, later misattributed to Blaise de Vigen\`ere, and earned the
nickname \emph{le chiffre ind\'echiffrable} -- the indecipherable cipher -- because it resisted
frequency analysis for three centuries.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Three hundred years of false confidence.} From the mid-1500s until the mid-1800s the
Vigen\`ere cipher was widely believed unbreakable. Charles Babbage broke it around 1854 but did
not publish (some think at the request of British intelligence during the Crimean War). The
Prussian infantry officer Friedrich Kasiski published the method independently in 1863, and it
bears his name. This chapter makes the point repeatedly: a cipher being unbroken for
a long time is not a proof that it is secure. It may only mean nobody clever has published yet.
\end{focusbox}

\subsection{How it works}

Pick a keyword, say \texttt{KEY}. Write it repeatedly under the plaintext, and shift each
plaintext letter by the corresponding key letter (treating $\texttt{A}=0$ as ``no shift,''
$\texttt{B}=1$, and so on). Formally, with key letters $k_0, k_1, \ldots, k_{\ell-1}$ of period
$\ell$, the $i$-th plaintext letter $m_i$ (counting positions from $i = 0$) encrypts to
ciphertext letter $c_i$ via
\[
  c_i \equiv m_i + k_{(i \bmod \ell)} \pmod{26},
\]
where $i \bmod \ell$ is the remainder when $i$ is divided by $\ell$, cycling through
$0, 1, \ldots, \ell - 1$. It is a Caesar cipher whose shift cycles through the keyword.

\begin{example}[Encrypting with \texttt{KEY}]
\label{ch02:vig-encrypt}
Encrypt \texttt{ATTACKATDAWN} with keyword \texttt{KEY} ($\texttt{K}=10,\ \texttt{E}=4,\
\texttt{Y}=24$, so $\ell = 3$).
\[
\begin{array}{c|cccccccccccc}
\text{plain} & \texttt{A}&\texttt{T}&\texttt{T}&\texttt{A}&\texttt{C}&\texttt{K}&\texttt{A}&\texttt{T}&\texttt{D}&\texttt{A}&\texttt{W}&\texttt{N}\\
m            & 0&19&19&0&2&10&0&19&3&0&22&13\\
\text{key}   & \texttt{K}&\texttt{E}&\texttt{Y}&\texttt{K}&\texttt{E}&\texttt{Y}&\texttt{K}&\texttt{E}&\texttt{Y}&\texttt{K}&\texttt{E}&\texttt{Y}\\
k            & 10&4&24&10&4&24&10&4&24&10&4&24\\
(m+k) \bmod 26 & 10&23&17&10&6&8&10&23&1&10&0&11\\
\text{cipher}& \texttt{K}&\texttt{X}&\texttt{R}&\texttt{K}&\texttt{G}&\texttt{I}&\texttt{K}&\texttt{X}&\texttt{B}&\texttt{K}&\texttt{A}&\texttt{L}
\end{array}
\]
Result: \texttt{KXRKGIKXBKAL}.
\end{example}

Consider the three \texttt{T}'s in the plaintext (positions $1$, $2$, and $7$, counting from
$0$). The first became \texttt{X}, the second \texttt{R}, and the third \texttt{X} again --
because the key letters under them were \texttt{E}, \texttt{Y}, and \texttt{E}. The same
plaintext letter no longer maps to a single ciphertext letter; it splits according to where the
key cycle catches it. (The four \texttt{A}'s at positions $0$, $3$, $6$, and $9$, by contrast,
all fall on a key-\texttt{K} position and so all become \texttt{K} -- a reminder that the
flattening is statistical, not guaranteed letter by letter.) On a long Vigen\`ere ciphertext,
the single-letter histogram is much flatter than English, stalling al-Kindi's direct attack.

\begin{intuitionbox}
A Vigen\`ere cipher of period $\ell$ is $\ell$ independent Caesar ciphers interleaved:
positions $0, \ell, 2\ell, \ldots$ all use key letter $k_0$; positions $1, \ell+1, 2\ell+1,
\ldots$ all use $k_1$; and so on. If you knew where one of these ``columns'' lived, you would
be back to a single Caesar cipher, solvable in $25$ tries. So breaking Vigen\`ere reduces to
one step: \emph{find the key length $\ell$.} Once $\ell$ is known, the cipher splits into $\ell$
easy Caesar problems.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Kasiski and Babbage: finding the key length}
\label{ch02:kasiski}

The Vigen\`ere cipher hides the key, yet it cannot hide a coincidence. Suppose a common word
like \texttt{THE} appears twice in the plaintext, and both occurrences happen to fall against
the \emph{same} positions of the keyword. Then both encrypt to the \emph{same} three-letter
ciphertext trigram. The distance between those two identical trigrams in the ciphertext must be
a whole number of keyword lengths -- an integer multiple of $\ell$.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why repeated patterns leak the key length.} If plaintext position $i$ and position $j$
hold the same letters \emph{and} line up with the same key letters, their ciphertexts match.
Matching key positions means $i \equiv j \pmod{\ell}$, so the spacing $j-i$ is a multiple of
$\ell$. Every aligned repeat hands you a number divisible by $\ell$. Collect several such
spacings; $\ell$ divides all of them, so it divides their \emph{greatest common divisor} ($\gcd$,
the largest integer that divides every spacing with zero remainder). Checking the $\gcd$ and its
factors is the \emph{Kasiski test}.
\end{whybox}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Kasiski test, worked.} Suppose Eve scans a long ciphertext for repeated trigrams and
finds \texttt{WMV} at positions $5$, $21$, and $53$ (counting from $0$), plus \texttt{GTH} at
positions $9$ and $25$.

Spacings between repeats:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\texttt{WMV}: &\quad 21-5 = 16, \quad 53-21 = 32, \quad 53-5 = 48,\\
\texttt{GTH}: &\quad 25-9 = 16.
\end{aligned}
\]
Now take the greatest common divisor of those spacings:
\[
  \gcd(16, 32, 48, 16) = 16.
\]
Assuming none of the repeats was an unaligned fluke, the key length $\ell$ divides $16$, giving
$\ell \in \{1,2,4,8,16\}$ (Figure~\ref{ch02:fig-kasiski}). A period of $1$ is a single Caesar
cipher (ruled out by the flat letter counts), leaving $\{2, 4, 8, 16\}$ -- with $\ell = 16$ (the
$\gcd$ itself) and $\ell = 8$ as the leading candidates. Eve slices the ciphertext for each
candidate and checks whether the resulting columns show English-like letter counts.
\end{attackbox}

Once Eve confirms $\ell = 8$, she splits the ciphertext into $8$ columns by position modulo $8$.
Each column is a single Caesar cipher, which she solves either by trying all $26$ shifts or by
lining up that column's letter counts against English. The most common letter in column $j$ is
usually the encryption of \texttt{E}, pinning down $k_j$.

\begin{example}[Solving one column]
\label{ch02:vig-column}
Suppose column $0$ (positions $0,8,16,\ldots$) has \texttt{R} ($17$) as its most frequent
letter. Guessing that \texttt{R} encrypts \texttt{E} ($4$) gives
\[
  k_0 \equiv 17 - 4 = 13 \pmod{26},
\]
so $k_0 = 13 = \texttt{N}$. The first keyword letter is \texttt{N}. Repeating that deduction on
the remaining seven columns recovers the full keyword -- say \texttt{NIGHTOWL}. If a column's
most frequent letter looks wrong in context (in a short column, \texttt{T} or \texttt{A} can
outnumber \texttt{E} by chance), Eve tries the top two or three candidates for that column and
keeps the keyword that yields readable English.
\end{example}

\subsection{The Friedman test: counting coincidences}
\label{ch02:friedman}

In 1920 William Friedman introduced a quantitative way to find the key length without hunting
for repeated substrings by eye. It rests on a single statistic: the \emph{index of coincidence}.

\begin{definition}[Index of coincidence]
\label{ch02:ioc-def}
Given a text of length $n \ge 2$ with letter counts $n_{\texttt{A}}, n_{\texttt{B}}, \ldots,
n_{\texttt{Z}}$, the \emph{index of coincidence} ($\mathrm{IC}$) is the probability that two
positions chosen uniformly at random without replacement hold the same letter:
\[
  \mathrm{IC} = \frac{\sum_{x=\texttt{A}}^{\texttt{Z}} n_x (n_x - 1)}{n(n-1)}.
\]
Here $n(n-1)$ counts all ordered pairs of distinct positions in the text, and $n_x(n_x - 1)$
counts the ordered pairs in which both positions hold letter $x$.
\end{definition}

For text drawn uniformly at random over $26$ letters, any two positions match with probability
$1/26 \approx 0.0385$. For English, uneven letter frequencies push that collision probability up
to $\sum p_x^2 \approx 0.0667$ (where $p_x$ is the frequency of letter $x$, led by
$p_{\texttt{E}}^2 \approx 0.127^2 \approx 0.016$). A monoalphabetic substitution preserves the
English value because renaming letters leaves the set of counts $\{n_x\}$ unchanged, so its
$\mathrm{IC}$ sits near $0.066$. A Vigen\`ere ciphertext with a long period mixes many shifts
and drops toward the uniform baseline $0.0385$.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why the index of coincidence finds the key length.} Slice the ciphertext into $\ell$
columns. When $\ell$ equals the true period (or any multiple of it), every column is a single
Caesar shift of English and therefore has $\mathrm{IC} \approx 0.066$. When $\ell$ does not
divide the period, each column mixes several shifts and its $\mathrm{IC}$ drops toward $0.0385$.
Sweeping $\ell = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ and plotting the average column $\mathrm{IC}$, the true period
is the \emph{first} value of $\ell$ that jumps up to the English line (Figure~\ref{fig:ch02-friedman-sweep}).

Friedman also gave a closed-form estimate directly from the whole-ciphertext $\mathrm{IC}$
before slicing. Pick any position in a ciphertext of length $n$: among the $n-1$ other
positions, roughly $n/\ell - 1$ lie in the \emph{same} column (matching with probability
$\approx 0.0667$) and the remaining $n - n/\ell$ lie in \emph{different} columns (matching at
the random rate $1/26 \approx 0.0385$):
\[
  \mathrm{IC} \approx \frac{n/\ell - 1}{n - 1}(0.0667) + \frac{n - n/\ell}{n - 1}(0.0385).
\]
Multiplying through by $n - 1$ and solving for $\ell$ (using $0.0667 - 0.0385 = 0.0282$) gives
\[
  \ell \approx \frac{0.0282\, n}{(n-1)\,\mathrm{IC} - 0.0385\, n + 0.0667}.
\]
\end{whybox}

\begin{connectionbox}
The index of coincidence is a collision probability: the chance that two independently chosen
items land in the same bucket. That same quantity drives the \emph{birthday paradox} (in a room
of just $23$ people, two share a birthday more than half the time because all pairs are checked)
and hash-function collisions, covered in the companion probability chapters and revisited in
Chapter~14. The Friedman test asks ``are there too many coincidences here to be uniform
noise?'' -- answered with the same pair-counting combinatorics that bounds a birthday attack.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{implementbox}
Index of coincidence and a key-length sweep.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
# LEARNING ONLY -- toy code for understanding, not for protecting real data.
def index_of_coincidence(text):
    text = [c for c in text.upper() if c.isalpha()]
    n = len(text)
    if n < 2:
        return 0.0
    from collections import Counter
    counts = Counter(text)
    return sum(v * (v - 1) for v in counts.values()) / (n * (n - 1))

def guess_key_length(ciphertext, max_len=20):
    letters = [c for c in ciphertext.upper() if c.isalpha()]
    for ell in range(1, max_len + 1):
        columns = ["".join(letters[i::ell]) for i in range(ell)]
        avg_ic = sum(index_of_coincidence(col) for col in columns) / ell
        print(f"key length {ell:2d}: avg IC = {avg_ic:.4f}")
        # English-like (~0.066) flags the true length; random-like is ~0.038
\end{lstlisting}
Run this on a Vigen\`ere ciphertext and the true key length is the row where the average index
of coincidence spikes toward $0.066$. Then break each column as a Caesar cipher.
\end{implementbox}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{``What if I use a long keyword?''} Lengthening the Vigen\`ere keyword raises the amount
of ciphertext needed, without changing the outcome. Whenever a key is shorter than the message
and repeats, Kasiski and Friedman find the period once enough ciphertext accumulates. A
Vigen\`ere key is safe \emph{only} when it is at least as long as the message, chosen uniformly
at random, and never reused -- at which point it is no longer Vigen\`ere, it is the one-time pad
(Chapter~9). The same rule holds for every \emph{keystream} (a sequence of key values combined
position by position with a message): \textbf{a repeating keystream is a broken keystream.} That
applies whether the repeat comes from a Vigen\`ere keyword, a reused nonce (Chapter~1), or a reused
\emph{initialization vector} (IV, the public starting value that seeds encryption of a message).
\end{trapbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{The same bug, a century later.} Modern \emph{stream ciphers} encrypt binary data by
combining each message bit $m$ with a keystream bit $k$ using bitwise XOR
($\xor$, defined in Chapter~1; it is addition modulo~$2$). Because any bit XORed with itself cancels ($k \xor k = 0$), encrypting two
messages $m_1$ and $m_2$ under the \emph{same} keystream $k$ -- the ``two-time pad'' bug
shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ch02-keystream-reuse} -- lets an attacker XOR the ciphertexts and erase
the key completely:
\[
  c_1 \xor c_2 = (m_1 \xor k) \xor (m_2 \xor k) = m_1 \xor m_2.
\]
Language and format redundancy then separate $m_1$ and $m_2$, just as column statistics broke
Vigen\`ere. The 2001 break of WiFi's Wired Equivalent Privacy (WEP) exploited IV reuse in the
RC4 stream cipher, and Microsoft's Point-to-Point Tunneling Protocol (PPTP) fell to the same
mistake. Never reuse a keystream.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Rotor machines and Enigma: mechanizing the substitution}
\label{ch02:enigma}

By the early twentieth century, cryptographers understood that a fixed substitution was fatal
and that you wanted the substitution to change with every letter. Doing that by hand with a
long keyword is slow and error-prone. The breakthrough was mechanical: build a machine whose
internal wiring \emph{is} a substitution, and make it step to a new substitution after every
keypress. These are \emph{rotor machines}, and the most famous is the German Enigma.

\begin{historybox}
\textbf{Enigma.} Patented by Arthur Scherbius around 1918 and adopted by the German military through the 1920s and 30s, Enigma was the primary cipher of the German army (\emph{Wehrmacht}), air force (\emph{Luftwaffe}), and navy (\emph{Kriegsmarine}) in World War~II. Its operators believed the $10^{23}$ rotor-and-plugboard configurations made it unbreakable. They were wrong: Polish mathematicians Marian Rejewski, Jerzy R\'o\.zycki, and Henryk Zygalski broke it in 1932, and after Poland shared its methods and replica machines with Britain and France in July 1939, Alan Turing, Gordon Welchman, and thousands of others at Bletchley Park industrialized the attack. Purpose-built machines (the Polish \emph{bomba}, the British \emph{Bombe}) turned cryptanalysis into an engineering discipline and shortened the war by years. Afterward, Britain kept Bletchley's work classified until the 1970s, obscuring Poland's breakthrough while selling captured rotor machines to former allies without revealing they could be read.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{What the machine does}

A rotor is a disk with $26$ contacts on each face, wired so that current entering at one
letter exits at some scrambled other letter -- a fixed substitution baked into copper. Stack
three rotors in series and you \emph{compose} three substitutions (applying the first, then the
second to its output, then the third). After each keypress, the rightmost rotor turns one
position, shifting its substitution before the current flows. When the right rotor completes a
full $26$-step turn, a notch on its rim kicks the middle rotor forward one step (like an
odometer), and the middle rotor's notch eventually kicks the left. Enigma added two more
elements, traced in Figure~\ref{ch02:fig-enigma-wiring}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item A \emph{reflector} at the far end that connected the $26$ contacts in $13$ fixed pairs,
        sending the current back through all three rotors along a different path. This made
        encryption and decryption identical: if pressing \texttt{A} lit \texttt{W} at a given
        setting, pressing \texttt{W} at that same setting lit \texttt{A}.
  \item A \emph{plugboard} (\emph{Steckerbrett}) at the front where patch cables swapped up to
        $10$ pairs of letters on the way in and again on the way out, multiplying the number of
        possible keys by more than $10^{14}$.
\end{itemize}

The daily key specified which three rotors to load and in what order, their starting window

these choices gave roughly $10^{23}$ configurations -- about $77$ bits. By the brute-force
logic of Section~\ref{ch02:caesar}, that key space was far beyond exhaustive search. Enigma fell
anyway, and the reasons show how real systems fail.

\subsection{The flaws that sank it}
\label{ch02:enigma-flaws}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{No letter ever encrypts to itself.} Because the reflector pairs distinct contacts, the
output letter never equals the input letter. Codebreakers exploited this by sliding a guessed
plaintext snippet -- called a \emph{crib} -- along the ciphertext and immediately discarding
every alignment where the crib and ciphertext shared a letter at the same position
(Figure~\ref{ch02:fig-enigma-crib}). A single match rules out that offset outright. Because
routine German traffic carried predictable phrases -- morning reports contained \texttt{WETTER}
(``weather''), messages ended \texttt{HEILHITLER} -- crib-dragging eliminated most candidate
positions before a single rotor setting was tested.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Operator habits and key reuse.} Procedure required each message to carry a fresh
three-letter rotor start, encrypted under the daily setting. Early rules had the operator type
this three-letter \emph{message key twice} (for example, \texttt{QWE QWE}) as a transmission
check. Typing it twice tied ciphertext positions $1$ and $4$, $2$ and $5$, and $3$ and $6$ to
the same three plaintext letters -- the same repeated-key leak that sank Vigen\`ere. Rejewski
converted those paired positions into permutation equations and solved for the rotor wiring.
Operators also reused favorite start letters (\texttt{CIL} for a girlfriend's initials,
\texttt{BER} for Berlin), picked adjacent plugboard letters, and opened messages with
predictable phrases. Each habit handed the codebreakers an opening.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why ``no self-encryption'' is a leak.} A secure cipher's output should behave like a
uniformly random string, independent of the plaintext except for length. Enigma's ciphertext
violated uniformity: the event ``ciphertext letter equals plaintext letter'' had probability
$0$, whereas uniform random letters collide $1/26$ of the time. That missing $1/26$ leaked
information about the plaintext into every single character of ciphertext.
\end{whybox}

\begin{intuitionbox}
The Bombe never searched all $10^{23}$ keys. Once a crib aligned cleanly under a ciphertext
segment, repeated letters in the pair formed closed electrical loops through the rotor stack
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch02-bombe-loop}). Guessing a single plugboard partner for one letter in the
loop forced the plugboard partners of the next letters around the cycle; if the chain returned
to the starting letter with a different partner, that guess contradicted itself. When all $26$
plug guesses for that letter contradicted, the rotor position itself was impossible -- allowing
the Bombe to test the $26^3 = 17{,}576$ rotor positions per rotor order at electrical speed
while ignoring the $10^{14}$ plugboard combinations until the final step.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The Polish head start.} Enigma is often told solely as a Bletchley Park story, yet the
foundational break was Polish. In 1932 Marian Rejewski combined the doubled-message-key flaw
with French intelligence documents to reconstruct Enigma's internal rotor wiring. His key tool
came from algebra (Chapter~5): if you follow where a permutation sends each letter ($\texttt{A}
\to \texttt{G} \to \texttt{M} \to \texttt{A}$, and so on), the alphabet decomposes into closed
loops of various lengths. Swapping letter pairs on the plugboard relabels the letters inside
those loops without changing the \emph{lengths} of the loops, allowing Rejewski to strip the
unknown plugboard out of the equations and solve for the rotors alone. Poland built
electromechanical \emph{bomba} machines and read German traffic for years before handing its
methods and replica machines to Britain and France in July 1939, weeks before the invasion.
The lesson for a working cryptographer: the mathematics that breaks your design may already sit
in someone's notebook.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Why Enigma still matters to you.} Enigma's downfall was the \emph{system} surrounding
the rotors: predictable message openings that supplied cribs, a reflector quirk (no
self-encryption), key-management rules that repeated message keys, and operators who chose
guessable settings. Modern breaches follow the same pattern. The 2014 Heartbleed memory-read bug
in OpenSSL, the WEP break in WiFi, Sony's PlayStation~3 signing-key leak from reusing a random
nonce in the Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA), and hundreds of published
software vulnerability reports -- known as Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVEs) -- all
left the underlying cipher math intact while breaking the surrounding implementation or
procedure. When you deploy cryptography, an attacker can target the protocol, the random-number
generator, how keys are generated, rotated, and retired, and the human operators alongside the
cipher itself. You cannot judge a cipher in isolation from how people deploy and operate it.
\end{applicationbox}

\subsection{A tiny rotor by hand}
\label{ch02:toy-rotor}

To see how mechanical rotation changes a substitution, build a one-rotor toy on the five-letter
alphabet $\texttt{A}, \texttt{B}, \texttt{C}, \texttt{D}, \texttt{E} = 0,1,2,3,4$. Give the
rotor the fixed internal wiring
\[
  S: \quad \texttt{A}\to\texttt{C},\ \texttt{B}\to\texttt{E},\ \texttt{C}\to\texttt{A},\
  \texttt{D}\to\texttt{B},\ \texttt{E}\to\texttt{D},
\]
so as a function on $\{0,1,2,3,4\}$ we have $S(0)=2$, $S(1)=4$, $S(2)=0$, $S(3)=1$, and
$S(4)=3$. When the rotor has stepped $i$ clicks forward (starting at $i = 0$), fixed input slot
$m_i$ lines up with rotor contact $(m_i + i) \bmod 5$, travels across the copper wire to output
contact $S\bigl((m_i + i) \bmod 5\bigr)$, and exits at fixed lamp slot $\bigl(S((m_i + i) \bmod
5) - i\bigr) \bmod 5$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ch02-toy-rotor-step}):
\[
  c_i = \bigl(S\bigl((m_i + i)\bmod 5\bigr) - i\bigr)\bmod 5.
\]

Encrypt \texttt{BAD} $= (1, 0, 3)$ step by step:
\begin{align*}
i=0:\quad c_0 &= \bigl(S\bigl((1+0)\bmod 5\bigr) - 0\bigr)\bmod 5 = S(1) = 4 = \texttt{E},\\
i=1:\quad c_1 &= \bigl(S\bigl((0+1)\bmod 5\bigr) - 1\bigr)\bmod 5 = (S(1) - 1)\bmod 5 = 3 = \texttt{D},\\
i=2:\quad c_2 &= \bigl(S\bigl((3+2)\bmod 5\bigr) - 2\bigr)\bmod 5 = (S(0) - 2)\bmod 5 = 0 = \texttt{A}.
\end{align*}
So \texttt{BAD} encrypts to \texttt{EDA}. All three plaintext letters pass through the
\emph{same} physical wiring $S$, yet because the rotor advances after each keypress, the
effective alphabet mapping shifts every time (for example, \texttt{AAA} encrypts to
\texttt{CDD}). A one-way rotor also allows a letter to encrypt to itself whenever a wire runs
straight across ($S(u)=u$, as Enigma's real Rotor~I did at contact \texttt{S}); it was
specifically Enigma's \emph{reflector} that forced $c_i \neq m_i$ at every step regardless of
how the rotors were wired.

\begin{implementbox}
The toy rotor, in code, so you can watch the substitution change at each step.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
# LEARNING ONLY -- a 5-letter cartoon of a rotor, not a real cipher.
S = [2, 4, 0, 1, 3]          # the fixed wiring (a permutation of 0..4)
S_inv = [S.index(j) for j in range(5)]

def enc(plain):
    out = []
    for i, ch in enumerate(plain):
        m = ord(ch) - ord('A')
        c = (S[(m + i) % 5] - i) % 5
        out.append(chr(c + ord('A')))
    return "".join(out)

def dec(cipher):
    out = []
    for i, ch in enumerate(cipher):
        c = ord(ch) - ord('A')
        m = (S_inv[(c + i) % 5] - i) % 5
        out.append(chr(m + ord('A')))
    return "".join(out)

print(enc("BAD"))        # -> EDA
print(dec(enc("BAD")))   # -> BAD
\end{lstlisting}
Add more rotors (compose several permutations, each stepping at a different rate) and a
reflector, and you have the skeleton of Enigma. Add a plugboard (a fixed swap applied before
and after) and you have its shape. None of this is secure -- it is here so the machine stops
being a black box.
\end{implementbox}

\section{What three thousand years of failure teaches}
\label{ch02:lessons}

Every cipher in this chapter
was built by capable people, trusted by serious institutions, and broken by attackers who
ignored the designers' assumptions. Five lessons carry forward into modern cryptography.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Five lessons that outlived their ciphers.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{The key space must be too large to search} -- yet a large key space guarantees
        nothing on its own (Section~\ref{ch02:substitution}, Section~\ref{ch02:enigma}).
        Caesar lost to brute force; substitution and Enigma had huge key spaces and lost
        anyway, because the attacker never searched them.
  \item \emph{Ciphertext must not leak statistics about plaintext.} Letter frequencies sank
        substitution; the missing $1/26$ self-map sank Enigma; repeated patterns and column
        coincidences sank Vigen\`ere. Modern security definitions formalize this requirement:
        ciphertext must be indistinguishable from random bits.
  \item \emph{Reuse is the enemy.} A repeating Vigen\`ere keyword, a doubled Enigma message key,
        and a reused stream-cipher keystream are the same mistake. Keys and keystreams must not
        repeat.
  \item \emph{Kerckhoffs was right: assume the enemy knows the system.} Every cipher here was
        eventually understood by the attacker; only key secrecy ever mattered, and key
        \emph{management} -- generation, distribution, rotation, operator discipline -- was
        where systems broke.
  \item \emph{Unbroken is not proven.} ``It looks scrambled and nobody has broken it'' is not a
        security argument. Vigen\`ere stood three centuries before Babbage and Kasiski broke it.
        Confidence comes from analysis and proof, not from the absence of a published attack.
\end{enumerate}
\end{focusbox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
This history also shows a broader change in method. The early ciphers were defended by
\emph{obscurity and effort} -- surely no one will try all the shifts, surely no one will count
the letters, surely no one will notice the doubled key. Each defense was an appeal to the
attacker's laziness or ignorance. The modern field replaces that with something sturdier: we
state precisely what the attacker can do, we define what ``broken'' means, and we prove
-- or reduce to a hard mathematical problem -- that breaking the cipher is as hard as solving
that problem. Keep the lessons of Caesar, Vigen\`ere, and Enigma in mind: every one of them
looked ``visibly fine'' to its builders.
\end{aestheticbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Each modern primitive answers one of the failures in this chapter. Substitution's frequency leak
motivates Shannon's \emph{perfect secrecy} and the one-time pad (Chapter~9), while Vigen\`ere's
repeating keystream motivates \emph{stream ciphers} and the rule against keystream reuse
(Chapter~10). Enigma's mechanized, stepping substitution is the ancestor of the \emph{block
cipher} and its repeated rounds of substitution and mixing (Chapter~11), and the ``large key
space, broken anyway'' lesson explains why we define security against efficient
(polynomial-time) adversaries rather than counting keys.

Every cipher here also shared the same arithmetic: Caesar shifted letters around a ring of $26$
positions ($c \equiv m + k \pmod{26}$), Vigen\`ere cycled through shifts, and Enigma stepped
contacts around a $26$-letter dial. Chapter~3 puts this clockwork remainder arithmetic on firm
mathematical footing -- modular addition, modular multiplication, and modular inverses (dividing
cleanly modulo $n$) -- the arithmetic every modern cipher uses.
\end{connectionbox}

\section*{Exercises}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Caesar by hand]
\label{ch02:ex-caesar}
Encrypt the plaintext \texttt{CRYPTO} with a Caesar shift of $k=7$. Then decrypt the
ciphertext \texttt{OLSSV} (also encrypted with $k=7$) back to plaintext. Show the numeric steps
modulo $26$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Brute-forcing Caesar]
\label{ch02:ex-brute}
You intercept \texttt{IBWFGVO}. Knowing only that it is a Caesar cipher of a two-word English
phrase (spaces removed), find the key and the plaintext by trying shifts. How many shifts would
you need to test in the worst case, and why is $26$ a fatal key-space size?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Frequency attack]
\label{ch02:ex-freq}
The following is a monoalphabetic substitution of an English sentence:
\texttt{ZIT DTF QKT QSS OF ZIT ZTFZ}. Count the letters, identify the likely encryption
of \texttt{E} and \texttt{T}, use the short words, the repeated three-letter word
\texttt{ZIT}, and the doubled-letter word \texttt{QSS}, and recover the plaintext. State the
substitution map for every ciphertext letter that appears.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Why $26!$ does not help]
\label{ch02:ex-keyspace}
The monoalphabetic key space has size $26! \approx 4\times10^{26}$, about $88$ bits. Explain in
two or three sentences why this huge number provides no security against frequency analysis, and
connect your explanation to the requirement that ciphertext must not leak statistics about
plaintext.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Vigen\`ere encryption]
\label{ch02:ex-vig-enc}
Encrypt \texttt{MEETATDAWN} with the Vigen\`ere keyword \texttt{LEMON}. Write the key letters
under the plaintext, give the numeric shifts, and produce the ciphertext. Then verify by
decrypting your own output.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Kasiski test]
\label{ch02:ex-kasiski}
In a Vigen\`ere ciphertext you find the trigram \texttt{XQR} repeated at positions $4$, $22$, and
$40$, and the trigram \texttt{PLM} repeated at positions $7$ and $31$. Compute all pairwise
distances, take their greatest common divisor, and list the candidate key lengths. Which would
you try first, and why?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Index of coincidence]
\label{ch02:ex-ioc}
A ciphertext of length $n=200$ has letter counts that yield $\sum_x n_x(n_x-1) = 2600$. Compute
the index of coincidence. Is this text closer to English ($\approx 0.066$) or to random
($\approx 0.038$)? What does that suggest about whether it is a monoalphabetic substitution or a
Vigen\`ere cipher with a long key?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Two-time pad / keystream reuse]
\label{ch02:ex-twotime}
Alice encrypts two English messages $m_1$ and $m_2$ with the \emph{same} Vigen\`ere keyword (or
equivalently the same keystream), producing $c_1$ and $c_2$. Show that $c_1 - c_2 \equiv m_1 -
m_2 \pmod{26}$ position by position, so the key cancels. Explain how an attacker recovers
$m_1$ and $m_2$ from $m_1 - m_2$ using language statistics, and name a modern system that
suffered this exact failure.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Enigma's fatal quirk]
\label{ch02:ex-enigma-self}
The Enigma reflector guaranteed that no letter ever encrypts to itself. Suppose you suspect a
ciphertext segment \texttt{LXGPQ} is the encryption of the crib \texttt{ANGRI} (a fragment of
\texttt{ANGRIFF}). Check whether this alignment is possible under Enigma, and if not, explain
how this single rule lets a codebreaker discard crib positions in bulk before testing any rotor
setting.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Toy rotor]
\label{ch02:ex-rotor}
Using the five-letter toy rotor of Section~\ref{ch02:toy-rotor} with
$S(0)=2,S(1)=4,S(2)=0,S(3)=1,S(4)=3$ and the stepping rule $c_i = (S((m_i+i)\bmod 5)-i)\bmod 5$,
encrypt the plaintext \texttt{CAB}. Then decrypt your result with the inverse rule to confirm
you recover \texttt{CAB}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Design a defense]
\label{ch02:ex-design}
For each of the three breaks in this chapter -- frequency analysis on substitution, the Kasiski
test on Vigen\`ere, and crib-dragging on Enigma -- name the single property of the cipher that
made the attack possible, and state the modern design principle (from the five lessons in
Section~\ref{ch02:lessons}) that eliminates it. Keep each answer to one or two sentences.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Prove a small fact about shifts]
\label{ch02:ex-prove}
Show that composing two Caesar ciphers, first a shift by $a$ then a shift by $b$, is the same as
a single Caesar shift by $(a+b)\bmod 26$. Conclude that the set of Caesar shifts forms a group
under composition -- meaning any two shifts combine into another shift, the shift $k=0$ leaves
every letter unchanged, and every shift $a$ has an inverse shift $(-a)\bmod 26$ that undoes it --
and explain why ``double Caesar'' adds no security at all.
\end{exercise}
