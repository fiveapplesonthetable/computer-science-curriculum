\chapter{Finite Fields}

In Chapter~5 we saw that a field is a set where you can add, subtract, multiply, and divide, and where ordinary school algebra keeps working: zero times anything is zero, $a(b+c)=ab+ac$, and every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse (reciprocal). The rational numbers $\Q$, the reals $\R$, and the complex numbers $\C$ are all fields. They are also infinite, and a computer cannot store an arbitrary real number. Cryptography runs on machines that manipulate fixed-size chunks of bits, so we need fields with finitely many elements: \emph{finite fields}.

Modern cryptography relies on two families of finite fields. The first is $\F_p = \Zn{p}$ for a prime $p$: integer arithmetic modulo a prime, where Diffie--Hellman key exchange and elliptic-curve cryptography operate. The second, $\F_{2^n}$, has elements that are $n$-bit strings. Its addition is bitwise XOR ($\xor$, Chapter~1), which adds corresponding bits modulo~$2$ with no carries between positions, and its multiplication lets you divide by every nonzero bit-string. The Advanced Encryption Standard (AES), the symmetric cipher that encrypts most web and disk traffic, works in $\F_{2^8}$, where each field element is a single $8$-bit byte.

Every finite field has a prime-power number of elements $q = p^n$, any two finite fields of the same size have the same structure up to relabeling their elements, and we can construct every one of them using polynomials and long division. By the end of the chapter you will build $\F_{2^8}$ from scratch and multiply and invert bytes in it by hand.

\section{What a Field Is, Quickly}
\label{ch06:what-is-field}

Recall from Chapter~5 what makes a set with addition and multiplication a field.

\begin{definition}[Field]
\label{ch06:def-field}
A \emph{field} is a set $\F$ with two operations, addition $+$ and
multiplication $\cdot$, such that:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $(\F, +)$ is an abelian group with identity $0$ (you can add and
        subtract, and addition commutes: $a + b = b + a$);
  \item $(\F \setminus \set{0}, \cdot)$, where $\F \setminus \set{0}$ means all
        elements of $\F$ except $0$, is an abelian group with identity $1$
        (every nonzero element $a$ has a multiplicative inverse $a^{-1}$ with
        $a \cdot a^{-1} = 1$, so you can multiply and divide);
  \item multiplication distributes over addition: $a\cdot(b+c) = a\cdot b +
        a\cdot c$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

Every nonzero element having a multiplicative inverse is what separates a field
from a general ring such as $\Zn{n}$ for composite $n$. In $\Zn{6}$ the element
$2$ has no inverse: there is no $y$ with $2y \equiv 1 \pmod 6$, because $2$ and
$6$ share the factor $2$. So $\Zn{6}$ is a ring, not a field. When the modulus
is prime, every nonzero element is coprime to it, every nonzero element has an
inverse, and we get a field.

\begin{intuitionbox}
A field is a setting in which the equation $ay = b$ always has a unique
solution $y = a^{-1}b$ whenever $a \neq 0$. Without multiplicative inverses you
cannot divide, and cryptographic steps that must be reversed during decryption
or recovery would fail. Decrypting an AES block, splitting a secret key across
several shares (Shamir secret sharing, Chapter~32), and correcting transmission
errors with Reed--Solomon codes all depend on dividing inside a finite field.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{The first family: $\F_p$}

\begin{theorem}[Prime moduli give fields]
\label{ch06:thm-fp}
For a prime $p$, the ring $\Zn{p} = \set{0,1,\dots,p-1}$ with addition and
multiplication modulo $p$ is a field. We write it $\F_p$ (or $\mathrm{GF}(p)$,
read ``Galois field of size $p$,'' also called order $p$).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$\Zn{p}$ is already a commutative ring with $1$. We only need multiplicative
inverses for nonzero elements. Take $a \in \set{1,\dots,p-1}$. Since $p$ is
prime and $0 < a < p$, we have $\gcd(a,p)=1$. By B\'ezout's identity (from the
extended Euclidean algorithm in Chapter~4) there are integers $u,v$ with
$ua + vp = 1$. Reducing modulo $p$ gives $ua \equiv 1 \pmod p$, so $u \bmod p$
is the multiplicative inverse of $a$. Every nonzero element is invertible, so
$\F_p$ is a field.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Inverses in $\F_7$]
\label{ch06:ex-f7}
In $\F_7$, what is $3^{-1}$? We want $3y \equiv 1 \pmod 7$. Testing $y=5$ gives
$3\cdot 5 = 15 = 2\cdot 7 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$, so $3^{-1} = 5$. The full
inverse table for $\F_7$ is
\[
1^{-1}=1,\quad 2^{-1}=4,\quad 3^{-1}=5,\quad 4^{-1}=2,\quad 5^{-1}=3,\quad
6^{-1}=6.
\]
Here $6 \equiv -1 \pmod 7$ is its own inverse, as is $1$; the remaining
elements pair up. Division by any nonzero element now works: $2/3$ in $\F_7$
means $2 \cdot 3^{-1} \equiv 2 \cdot 5 = 10 \equiv 3 \pmod 7$.
\end{example}

\begin{connectionbox}
Computing $a^{-1}$ in $\F_p$ with the extended Euclidean algorithm takes
$O(\log p)$ division steps (see the algorithms companion for the step-count
analysis). A second method uses Fermat's little theorem: $a^{p-1}\equiv 1
\pmod p$, so $a^{-1} \equiv a^{p-2} \pmod p$, computed by square-and-multiply
exponentiation in $O(\log p)$ multiplications. Euclid's algorithm is usually
faster, whereas $a^{p-2}$ can execute the exact same sequence of operations
regardless of $a$'s value (\emph{constant-time} execution). That uniformity
prevents \emph{side-channel attacks}, where an adversary infers secret values
by measuring how long a computation takes (Chapter~37).
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Why Prime Powers, and Nothing Else}
\label{ch06:prime-powers}

We now have a field $\F_p$ for every prime $p$. Can a finite field have $6$
elements, or $4$?

\begin{theorem}[Classification of finite fields]
\label{ch06:thm-classification}
A finite field has $q = p^n$ elements for some prime $p$ and integer
$n \geq 1$. Conversely, for every prime power $p^n$ there is a finite field
with $p^n$ elements, and any two finite fields with the same number of
elements are isomorphic --- structurally identical up to relabeling their
elements.
\end{theorem}

Proving existence and uniqueness takes more machinery, but showing why the size
must be a prime power $p^n$ takes only two steps.

\begin{whybox}
Let $\F$ be a finite field. Repeatedly add the multiplicative identity $1$ to
itself: $1,\ 1+1,\ 1+1+1,\ \dots$ Because $\F$ is finite, this sequence must
eventually repeat, so some positive number of $1$s sums to $0$. Let $p$ be the
smallest positive integer with
\[
\underbrace{1 + 1 + \cdots + 1}_{p\text{ copies}} = 0.
\]
This integer $p$ is called the \emph{characteristic} of $\F$. It must be prime:
if $p = ab$ with $1 < a,b < p$, then distributing multiplication over addition
gives
\[
\bigl(\underbrace{1 + \cdots + 1}_{a}\bigr)
\bigl(\underbrace{1 + \cdots + 1}_{b}\bigr)
= \underbrace{1 + \cdots + 1}_{ab = p} = 0,
\]
a product of two nonzero elements equal to zero. That cannot happen in a field,
because a zero divisor never has a multiplicative inverse.

Now the $p$ multiples $\set{0,1,\dots,p-1}$ sitting inside $\F$ form a
\emph{subfield} --- a subset that is itself a field under the same addition and
multiplication --- isomorphic to $\F_p$. Pick a minimal list of elements
$e_1, e_2, \dots, e_n \in \F$ (a \emph{basis}) such that every element of $\F$
can be written uniquely as a combination
\[
c_1 e_1 + c_2 e_2 + \cdots + c_n e_n \qquad (c_i \in \F_p).
\]
In linear algebra, a set whose elements are uniquely specified by lists of
scalars $(c_1,\dots,c_n)$ from $\F_p$ in this way is called a \emph{vector
space} over $\F_p$ of \emph{dimension} $n$. Each of the $n$ coordinates $c_i$
has $p$ independent choices in $\F_p$, giving $p \cdot p \cdots p = p^n$
elements in all: $\abs{\F} = p^n$.
\end{whybox}

That counting argument also shows how to build $\F_{p^n}$: represent elements
as $n$-tuples of coordinates in $\F_p$ and define a multiplication that has no
zero divisors. Polynomials provide that multiplication.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Why is there no field with $6$ elements? Because $6 = 2\cdot 3$ mixes two
different primes. As Figure~\ref{ch06:fig-prime-powers} shows, fields exist at
sizes $4=2^2$, $8=2^3$, $9=3^2$, and $16=2^4$, while $6$, $10$, $12$, $14$, and
$15$ are permanent gaps that no construction can fill.
\end{intuitionbox}

\section{Polynomials over a Field}
\label{ch06:polynomials}

To build $\F_{p^n}$ we repeat the construction of $\F_p$ one level up. For
$\F_p$, we started with the integers $\Z$, chose a prime $p$, and kept only
remainders modulo $p$; the primality of $p$ made that ring of remainders a
field.

Now replace integers with \emph{polynomials}. Fix a base field $\F_p$ and write
$\F_p[x]$ for the set of polynomials in one formal variable $x$ with
\emph{coefficients} $a_i$ in $\F_p$:
\[
\F_p[x] = \set{\, a_{d}x^{d} + \cdots + a_1 x + a_0 : a_i \in \F_p,\ d \ge 0 \,}.
\]
Here $x$ is an algebraic placeholder rather than an unknown number to solve
for. You add two polynomials by adding matching coefficients modulo $p$, and
multiply them by expanding brackets and reducing every coefficient modulo $p$.
This ring $\F_p[x]$ mirrors the integers $\Z$ in three ways:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Degree.} Every nonzero polynomial $f$ has a \emph{degree},
        written $\deg f$: the highest power of $x$ with a nonzero coefficient.
        Degree measures size for polynomials the way absolute value measures
        size for integers. A polynomial whose highest-degree term has
        coefficient $1$ (such as $x^2 + x + 1$) is called \emph{monic}.
  \item \textbf{Division with remainder.} Given any $f \in \F_p[x]$ and any
        nonzero $g \in \F_p[x]$, polynomial long division produces unique
        polynomials $q$ (the quotient) and $r$ (the remainder) such that
        $f = qg + r$, with either $r = 0$ or $\deg r < \deg g$.
  \item \textbf{Irreducible polynomials.} A polynomial of degree $\ge 1$ is
        \emph{irreducible} over $\F_p$ if it cannot be factored as a product of
        two polynomials in $\F_p[x]$ of smaller positive degree; otherwise it
        is \emph{reducible}. Irreducible polynomials play the role of prime
        numbers in $\F_p[x]$.
\end{itemize}

For a polynomial $m(x)$ of degree $n \ge 1$, we write $\F_p[x]/(m(x))$ (read
``$\F_p[x]$ modulo $m(x)$,'' also called the \emph{quotient} of $\F_p[x]$ by
$m(x)$) for the set of remainders of degree $< n$ under addition and
multiplication modulo $m(x)$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Each ingredient of modular integer arithmetic has a polynomial counterpart:
\[
\Z \leftrightarrow \F_p[x], \qquad
\text{prime } p \leftrightarrow \text{irreducible } m(x), \qquad
\Zn{p} \leftrightarrow \F_p[x]/(m(x)).
\]
Working modulo a prime integer gave a field of size $p$; working modulo a
degree-$n$ irreducible polynomial over $\F_p$ gives a field of size $p^n$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Irreducibility with small numbers}

Over $\F_2$, the only coefficients are $0$ and $1$, so every nonzero polynomial
is automatically monic and corresponds to a bit-string recording which powers
of $x$ appear. For example, $x^3 + x + 1 = 1\cdot x^3 + 0\cdot x^2 + 1\cdot x +
1$ is the bit-string $1011$.

Recall that $r \in \F_2$ is a \emph{root} of $f(x)$ if substituting $x = r$
gives $f(r) = 0$. By long division, $r$ is a root if and only if $(x - r)$
divides $f(x)$. Over $\F_2$ there are only two possible roots to test:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $f(0) = 0$ if and only if the constant term $a_0$ is $0$ (so $x$ is a
        factor);
  \item $f(1) = 0$ if and only if $f$ has an even number of nonzero terms,
        because in $\F_2$ adding an even number of $1$s gives $0$ (so $x + 1$
        is a factor).
\end{itemize}

\begin{example}[Cataloguing low-degree irreducibles over $\F_2$]
\label{ch06:ex-irreducibles}
Figure~\ref{ch06:fig-f2-irreducibles} runs both root tests on degree~$2$ and
degree~$3$.

\emph{Degree 1 (linear):} $x$ and $x+1$ cannot split into smaller positive
degrees, so both are irreducible.

\emph{Degree 2 (quadratic):} the four candidates are $x^2$, $x^2+1$, $x^2+x$,
and $x^2+x+1$. Any reducible quadratic must split into two degree-$1$ factors
and therefore have a root in $\F_2$. Here $x^2$ and $x^2+x = x(x+1)$ have root
$0$, and $x^2+1 = (x+1)^2$ has root $1$. Only $x^2+x+1$ has no root in $\F_2$
($0+0+1=1$ and $1+1+1=1$), making it the unique irreducible quadratic over
$\F_2$; it builds $\F_4$.

\emph{Degree 3 (cubic):} if a degree-$3$ polynomial factors into smaller
positive degrees, the factor degrees must sum to $3 = 1 + 2$, so one factor has
degree~$1$ and gives a root in $\F_2$. Of the eight cubics over $\F_2$, six fail
$f(0)=0$ or $f(1)=0$, leaving two irreducible cubics: $x^3+x+1$ and
$x^3+x^2+1$. Either one builds $\F_8$.
\end{example}

\begin{walkbox}
Is $f(x) = x^4 + x + 1$ irreducible over $\F_2$? First check roots: $f(0)=1$
and $f(1)=1+1+1=1$, so $f$ has no degree-$1$ factor. A degree-$4$ polynomial
without a root could still factor into two irreducible quadratics ($4 = 2 + 2$).
The only irreducible quadratic over $\F_2$ is $x^2+x+1$, and squaring it gives
$(x^2+x+1)^2 = x^4 + x^2 + 1 \neq x^4+x+1$. With neither a degree-$1$ nor a
degree-$2$ factor, $x^4+x+1$ is irreducible. It builds $\F_{16}$ and is also
used in cyclic redundancy check (CRC) error-detecting codes.
\end{walkbox}

\section{Building $\F_4$ by Hand}
\label{ch06:build-f4}

We start by building the smallest prime-power extension field, $\F_4$, in full;
$\F_{2^8}$ uses the same construction on $8$-bit strings.

Take $\F_2$ and the irreducible polynomial $m(x) = x^2 + x + 1$. Define
\[
\F_4 = \F_2[x] / (m(x)),
\]
the remainders of polynomials over $\F_2$ modulo $m(x)$. Because $\deg m = 2$,
every remainder has degree $< 2$, leaving four elements ($2^2 = 4$):
\[
\F_4 = \set{\, 0,\ 1,\ x,\ x+1 \,}.
\]
Books often rename the remainder $x$ as $\alpha$ and call $\alpha$ ``a root of
$m$,'' because inside $\F_4$ reducing modulo $m(x)$ sets $x^2 + x + 1 = 0$ ---
the polynomial that had no root in $\F_2$ acquires one in the larger field
$\F_4$.

Addition XORs the two-bit coefficient pairs ($00, 01, 10, 11$):
\[
x + (x+1) = 1, \qquad (x+1) + (x+1) = 0, \qquad 1 + 1 = 0.
\]

Multiplication multiplies the polynomials over $\F_2$ and reduces modulo
$m(x)$. Setting $m(x) = 0$ gives the replacement rule
\[
x^2 + x + 1 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^2 = x + 1,
\]
since $-1 = +1$ in $\F_2$. Replacing every $x^2$ with $x+1$ gives the nontrivial
products:
\begin{align*}
x \cdot x &= x^2 = x + 1, \\
x \cdot (x+1) &= x^2 + x = (x+1) + x = 1, \\
(x+1)(x+1) &= x^2 + 1 = (x+1) + 1 = x.
\end{align*}

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{c|cccc}
$\cdot$ & $0$ & $1$ & $x$ & $x + 1$ \\
\hline
$0$     & $0$ & $0$ & $0$     & $0$ \\
$1$     & $0$ & $1$ & $x$     & $x + 1$ \\
$x$     & $0$ & $x$ & $x + 1$ & $1$ \\
$x + 1$ & $0$ & $x + 1$ & $1$ & $x$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

From the $1$s in the multiplication table, $x^{-1} = x+1$ and $(x+1)^{-1} = x$.
Every nonzero element has an inverse, so $\F_4$ is a field of size $4$ ---
unlike the integer ring $\Zn{4}$, where $2 \cdot 2 \equiv 0 \pmod 4$ makes $2$
a zero divisor.

\begin{trapbox}
$\F_4$ is \emph{not} $\Zn{4}$. As Figure~\ref{ch06:fig-f4-vs-z4} compares, in
$\Zn{4}$ we have $1+1 = 2$ and $2+2 = 0$, so addition steps around a single
cycle of length $4$. In $\F_4$, every element added to itself is $0$
(characteristic $2$); its additive group is $\Zn{2} \times \Zn{2}$, the group
of bit pairs added coordinate-wise modulo $2$. Both $\Zn{4}$ and $\F_4$ are
four-element rings, but only $\F_4$ is a field. Confusing $\mathrm{GF}(2^n)$
with $\Zn{2^n}$ is one of the most common mistakes in cryptographic code.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{historybox}
These fields are named after \'Evariste Galois (1811--1832), who published ``Sur la th\'eorie des nombres'' in 1830 introducing finite fields of prime-power size $p^n$ by adjoining roots of irreducible polynomials modulo $p$. In 1893 Eliakim Hastings Moore proved that all finite fields of a given size $p^n$ are isomorphic and coined the name \emph{Galois field}. Engineering standards usually write $\mathrm{GF}(q)$ where algebra texts write $\F_q$: AES specifications say $\mathrm{GF}(2^8)$ and mathematicians say $\F_{256}$ for the same field. Today $\mathrm{GF}(2^8)$ drives AES's byte substitution box (S-box) and $\mathrm{GF}(2^{128})$ drives the polynomial message-authentication step (GHASH) inside AES in Galois/Counter Mode (AES-GCM). For public-key discrete logarithms, however, faster attacks in characteristic~$2$ --- \emph{quasi-polynomial} algorithms whose cost grows much more slowly than exponential --- have pushed modern protocols away from $\F_{2^n}$ toward prime fields $\F_p$ and elliptic curves over $\F_p$.
\end{historybox}

\section{The Field AES Uses: $\F_{2^8}$}
\label{ch06:f256}

A byte is eight bits, so byte-oriented cryptography calls for a field of
$256 = 2^8$ elements. Identify an $8$-bit byte $b_7 b_6 \cdots b_1 b_0$ with
the polynomial of degree at most $7$
\[
b_7 x^7 + b_6 x^6 + \cdots + b_1 x + b_0 \in \F_2[x].
\]
We write bytes in hexadecimal with the prefix \texttt{0x}, where each base-$16$
digit ($0$--$9$, $\texttt{A}$--$\texttt{F}$) stands for four bits: for example,
\texttt{0x57} is $0101\,0111$ ($\texttt{5} = 0101$, $\texttt{7} = 0111$), which
corresponds to $x^6 + x^4 + x^2 + x + 1$.

To turn these $256$ bytes into a field, pick an irreducible polynomial of
degree $8$ over $\F_2$ and reduce modulo it. AES fixes
\[
\boxed{\ m(x) = x^8 + x^4 + x^3 + x + 1\ }
\qquad(\texttt{0x11B} \text{ in hexadecimal, } 1\,0001\,1011_2),
\]
called the Rijndael polynomial after Rijndael, the cipher designed by Joan
Daemen and Vincent Rijmen that became AES. Thus
\[
\F_{2^8} = \F_2[x]/(m(x)),
\]
whose elements are the $256$ bytes, where addition is bitwise XOR and
multiplication is polynomial multiplication over $\F_2$ followed by reduction
modulo $m(x)$ (Figure~\ref{ch06:fig-f28-poly}).

\begin{whybox}
Why is $x^8+x^4+x^3+x+1$ irreducible, and why this choice? You can verify
irreducibility by trial division: $m(0)=1$ and $m(1)=1$, so it has no root in
$\F_2$, and dividing by every irreducible polynomial of degree $2$, $3$, and
$4$ over $\F_2$ leaves a nonzero remainder each time. There are $30$ irreducible
polynomials of degree $8$ over $\F_2$. The AES designers chose one with only
five nonzero terms so that modular reduction takes few XOR gates. All $30$
choices produce isomorphic fields of size $256$
(\autoref{ch06:thm-classification}); only the bit-level reduction mask changes.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{Addition is free}

Adding two elements of $\F_{2^8}$ adds their coefficients modulo $2$, which is
bitwise XOR. No carries cross from one bit to the next, and the degree never
exceeds $7$, so no modular reduction is needed:
\[
\texttt{0x53} \xor \texttt{0xCA} = \texttt{0x99}.
\]
In hardware this takes eight parallel XOR gates. Subtraction is identical to
addition because $-1 \equiv 1 \pmod 2$.

\begin{applicationbox}
Carry-free arithmetic is why hardware designers favor $\mathrm{GF}(2^n)$ and
why AES runs at gigabytes per second. Ordinary integer addition must propagate
carries across bit positions; $\F_{2^8}$ addition has none, and AES's
\texttt{MixColumns} step (which mixes each four-byte column of the cipher
state) reduces to a few shifts and XORs. Likewise, Galois/Counter Mode (GCM),
used in Transport Layer Security (TLS) connections, computes its
\emph{authentication tag} --- a short cryptographic check value proving the
ciphertext was not modified in transit --- by multiplying $128$-bit blocks in
$\F_{2^{128}}$ modulo a degree-$128$ irreducible polynomial. Carry-less
multiplication is straightforward to \emph{pipeline} in silicon (overlapping
consecutive operations across clock cycles), and modern CPUs provide a
dedicated carry-less multiply instruction (\texttt{PCLMULQDQ} on x86) for it.
\end{applicationbox}

\subsection{Multiplication, worked by hand}

To multiply two bytes in $\F_{2^8}$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Multiply their polynomials over $\F_2$ (a \emph{carry-less multiply}:
        combine partial products with XOR rather than integer addition).
  \item If the product has degree $\geq 8$, divide it by $m(x)$ using
        polynomial long division over $\F_2$ and keep the remainder.
\end{enumerate}

Consider $\texttt{0x57} \cdot \texttt{0x83}$ in $\F_{2^8}$, the worked example
from the Rijndael specification, where $\texttt{0x57} = x^6 + x^4 + x^2 + x + 1$
and $\texttt{0x83} = x^7 + x + 1$.

\textbf{Step 1: carry-less polynomial product.} Multiply the first polynomial
by each term of $x^7 + x + 1$:
\begin{align*}
(x^6+x^4+x^2+x+1)\cdot x^7
  &= x^{13}+x^{11}+x^{9}+x^{8}+x^{7},\\
(x^6+x^4+x^2+x+1)\cdot x
  &= x^{7}+x^{5}+x^{3}+x^{2}+x,\\
(x^6+x^4+x^2+x+1)\cdot 1
  &= x^{6}+x^{4}+x^{2}+x+1.
\end{align*}
XOR the three rows: any power of $x$ that appears an even number of times
cancels modulo $2$. The pairs of $x^7$, $x^2$, and $x$ terms cancel, leaving
\[
p(x) = x^{13}+x^{11}+x^{9}+x^{8}+x^{6}+x^{5}+x^{4}+x^{3}+1.
\]
Because $\deg p = 13 \ge 8$, we reduce modulo $m(x)$.

\textbf{Step 2: reduce modulo $m(x) = x^8+x^4+x^3+x+1$.} Since $m(x) \equiv 0$,
we have $x^8 \equiv x^4 + x^3 + x + 1$, so $x^k \equiv x^{k-8}(x^4 + x^3 + x +
1)$ for every $k \ge 8$. In particular,
\begin{align*}
x^{13} &\equiv x^{5}(x^4 + x^3 + x + 1) = x^{9}+x^{8}+x^{6}+x^{5},\\
x^{11} &\equiv x^{3}(x^4 + x^3 + x + 1) = x^{7}+x^{6}+x^{4}+x^{3}.
\end{align*}
Substitute these into the unreduced product $p(x)$ (the \emph{dividend}, or
polynomial being divided), canceling pairs of identical terms at each step:
\begin{align*}
p(x) &= x^{13} + x^{11} + x^{9} + x^{8} + x^{6} + x^{5} + x^{4} + x^{3} + 1 \\
     &\equiv (x^{9}+x^{8}+x^{6}+x^{5}) + x^{11} + x^{9} + x^{8} + x^{6} + x^{5} + x^{4} + x^{3} + 1
       && \text{(replace $x^{13}$)} \\
     &= x^{11} + x^{4} + x^{3} + 1
       && \text{($x^9, x^8, x^6, x^5$ cancel)} \\
     &\equiv (x^{7}+x^{6}+x^{4}+x^{3}) + x^{4} + x^{3} + 1
       && \text{(replace $x^{11}$)} \\
     &= x^{7} + x^{6} + 1
       && \text{($x^4, x^3$ cancel).}
\end{align*}
The remainder has degree $7 < 8$ and corresponds to the byte $1100\,0001_2 =
\texttt{0xC1}$. Therefore, in $\F_{2^8}$,
\[
\texttt{0x57} \cdot \texttt{0x83} = \texttt{0xC1}.
\]

\begin{walkbox}
On paper, Step~2 is fastest as bit-string long division where subtraction is
XOR (Figure~\ref{ch06:fig-poly-longdiv}). Write the $14$-bit dividend for
$p(x)$. Its leftmost $1$ sits at degree $13$; align the $9$-bit modulus
$m(x) = \texttt{1\,0001\,1011}$ under bit $13$ (that is $x^5 \cdot m(x)$) and
XOR. That clears bit $13$ along with bits $9,8,6,5$, leaving bit $11$ as the
new leading $1$. Align $m(x)$ under bit $11$ ($x^3 \cdot m(x)$) and XOR again.
All remaining $1$-bits now sit at degree $7$ or below, giving \texttt{0xC1}.
\end{walkbox}

\subsection{The doubling trick: \texttt{xtime}}

General multiplication takes several steps, but multiplying a byte $b$ by the
single element $x$ (the byte \texttt{0x02}) is simple enough to have its own
name in AES: \texttt{xtime}. Multiplying $b_7 x^7 + \cdots + b_0$ by $x$ shifts
every bit one position to the left. Write $b \ll 1$ for this $8$-bit left shift
(shifting bits up by one position and dropping the old top bit $b_7$):
\begin{itemize}
  \item If $b_7 = 0$, the shifted polynomial still has degree $\le 7$, so
        $b \ll 1$ is already reduced.
  \item If $b_7 = 1$, shifting produced an $x^8$ term; dropping bit $8$ and
        XORing with \texttt{0x1B} ($x^4+x^3+x+1$) reduces it modulo $m(x)$.
\end{itemize}
In symbols,
\[
\texttt{xtime}(b) =
\begin{cases}
b \ll 1, & \text{if } b_7 = 0,\\[2pt]
(b \ll 1) \xor \texttt{0x1B}, & \text{if } b_7 = 1.
\end{cases}
\]

\begin{example}[\texttt{xtime} in action]
\label{ch06:ex-xtime}
For $\texttt{0x57} = 0101\,0111$, the top bit is $b_7 = 0$, so shift left:
$\texttt{xtime}(\texttt{0x57}) = 1010\,1110 = \texttt{0xAE}$.

For $\texttt{0xAE} = 1010\,1110$, the top bit is $b_7 = 1$. Shifting $8$ bits
left drops the leading $1$ to leave $0101\,1100 = \texttt{0x5C}$, and XORing
with \texttt{0x1B} gives $\texttt{0x5C} \xor \texttt{0x1B} = \texttt{0x47}$.
Thus $\texttt{0x57}\cdot \texttt{0x04} = \texttt{0x47}$, since multiplying by
$\texttt{0x04} = x^2$ applies \texttt{xtime} twice.
\end{example}

Every multiplication in $\F_{2^8}$ builds out of \texttt{xtime} and XOR. To
multiply $b$ by any byte $c$, write $c$ in binary; each \emph{set bit} (each
bit equal to $1$) at position $k$ represents a term $x^k$, and $b \cdot x^k$ is
$k$ applications of \texttt{xtime}. XOR together the terms for the set bits of
$c$. This is the carry-less version of \emph{Russian-peasant multiplication}
(the shift-and-add algorithm that multiplies integers by repeatedly doubling
one factor and halving the other). Figure~\ref{ch06:fig-xtime} traces four
steps of \texttt{xtime} starting from $b = \texttt{0x57}$.

\begin{example}[$\texttt{0x57}\cdot\texttt{0x13}$ via \texttt{xtime}]
\label{ch06:ex-mul13}
Since $\texttt{0x13} = 0001\,0011_2 = x^4 + x + 1$, we read $b\cdot 1$,
$b\cdot x$, and $b\cdot x^4$ for $b=\texttt{0x57}$ from the \texttt{xtime}
chain:
\begin{align*}
b\cdot 1 &= \texttt{0x57},\\
b\cdot x &= \texttt{xtime}(\texttt{0x57}) = \texttt{0xAE},\\
b\cdot x^2 &= \texttt{xtime}(\texttt{0xAE}) = \texttt{0x47},\\
b\cdot x^3 &= \texttt{xtime}(\texttt{0x47}) = \texttt{0x8E},\\
b\cdot x^4 &= \texttt{xtime}(\texttt{0x8E}) = \texttt{0x07}.
\end{align*}
XORing the terms for $x^4$, $x$, and $1$ gives
\[
\texttt{0x07} \xor \texttt{0xAE} \xor \texttt{0x57} = \texttt{0xFE},
\]
so $\texttt{0x57}\cdot\texttt{0x13} = \texttt{0xFE}$. AES's \texttt{MixColumns}
step multiplies only by the small constants $\texttt{0x01}$, $\texttt{0x02}$,
and $\texttt{0x03} = x + 1$, so every entry takes at most one \texttt{xtime}
and one XOR.
\end{example}

\begin{implementbox}
A reference $\F_{2^8}$ multiplier in Python. \textbf{For learning only} --- the
\texttt{if} branches depend on the operand bits, making execution time vary
with secret data. Production AES implementations instead use \emph{bitsliced
logic} (evaluating Boolean gates across whole registers with branch-free
bitwise operations) or dedicated CPU instructions such as \texttt{AES-NI}
(Intel and AMD's Advanced Encryption Standard New Instructions) and
\texttt{PCLMULQDQ}.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
def gf_mul(a, b):
    """Multiply two bytes in GF(2^8) with the AES polynomial 0x11B."""
    p = 0
    for _ in range(8):
        if b & 1:          # current low bit of b set -> add a
            p ^= a         # addition is XOR
        b >>= 1
        hi = a & 0x80      # remember top bit before shifting
        a = (a << 1) & 0xFF
        if hi:             # overflow past x^7 -> reduce mod 0x11B
            a ^= 0x1B
    return p

def xtime(b):
    """Multiply by x (the element 0x02)."""
    b <<= 1
    if b & 0x100:
        b ^= 0x11B
    return b & 0xFF

assert gf_mul(0x57, 0x83) == 0xC1   # the worked example above
assert gf_mul(0x57, 0x13) == 0xFE
assert xtime(0x57) == 0xAE
\end{lstlisting}
The loop scans the eight bits of \texttt{b} from lowest to highest while
\texttt{a} steps through the \texttt{xtime} chain; the assertions check the two
hand examples above.
\end{implementbox}

\subsection{Finding inverses in $\F_{2^8}$}

AES's \texttt{SubBytes} step (its S-box) is built around the multiplicative
inverse in $\F_{2^8}$: each nonzero byte $a$ maps to $a^{-1}$ (with $0 \mapsto
0$ by convention), and the resulting $8$-bit vector then undergoes an
\emph{affine bit transformation} --- multiplication by a fixed invertible
$8\times 8$ matrix over $\F_2$ followed by XOR with the constant byte
\texttt{0x63}. Two algorithms compute $a^{-1}$ in $\F_{2^8}$, matching the two
methods for $\F_p$:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Extended Euclid on polynomials.} Run the extended Euclidean
        algorithm on the byte's polynomial $a(x)$ and $m(x)$ to find
        polynomials $u(x), v(x)$ with $u(x)a(x) + v(x)m(x) = 1$; then $u(x)$ is
        $a^{-1}$, by the same argument as \autoref{ch06:thm-fp}.
  \item \textbf{Fermat-style exponentiation.} The nonzero elements of
        $\F_{2^8}$ form a multiplicative group of size $255$, so $a^{255} = 1$
        for every nonzero $a$ by Lagrange's theorem, giving $a^{-1} = a^{254}$.
        Compute $a^{254}$ by square-and-multiply in $\F_{2^8}$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{example}[An inverse]
\label{ch06:ex-inverse}
In $\F_{2^8}$ with the AES modulus, $\texttt{0x53}^{-1} = \texttt{0xCA}$:
multiplying $\texttt{0x53} = x^6 + x^4 + x + 1$ by $\texttt{0xCA} = x^7 + x^6 +
x^3 + x$ and reducing modulo $m(x)$ leaves the remainder $1$ (\texttt{0x01}).
\end{example}

\begin{trapbox}
Do not guess field inverses by inspection, and do not confuse a factor in a
product with an inverse. Compute $a^{-1}$ via polynomial extended Euclid or
$a^{254}$, or precompute all $256$ outputs once into the AES S-box table. In
software that handles secret keys, however, looking up
\texttt{table[secret\_byte]} in RAM leaks information about
\texttt{secret\_byte} through CPU cache timing
(Chapter~37); constant-time implementations use \texttt{AES-NI} hardware or
branch-free bitsliced circuits instead of RAM table lookups.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{implementbox}
Compute the multiplicative inverse in $\F_{2^8}$ via $a^{254}$. \textbf{For
learning only.}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
def gf_inv(a):
    """Multiplicative inverse in GF(2^8): a^254, since |group| = 255."""
    if a == 0:
        return 0          # AES defines inv(0) = 0 by convention
    result, base, e = 1, a, 254
    while e:              # square-and-multiply for exponent 254
        if e & 1:
            result = gf_mul(result, base)
        base = gf_mul(base, base)
        e >>= 1
    return result

assert gf_inv(0x53) == 0xCA           # 0x53 * 0xCA == 1 in GF(2^8)
assert gf_mul(0x53, gf_inv(0x53)) == 1
\end{lstlisting}
Because $254 = 1111\,1110_2$, the loop performs eight squarings and seven
multiplications in $\F_{2^8}$.
\end{implementbox}

\section{The Multiplicative Group Is Cyclic}
\label{ch06:cyclic}

The nonzero elements of a finite field $\F_q$ form an abelian group under
multiplication, written $\F_q^{*} = \F_q \setminus \set{0}$, of size $q-1$.

\begin{theorem}[The multiplicative group is cyclic]
\label{ch06:thm-cyclic}
For any finite field $\F_q$, the multiplicative group $\F_q^{*}$ is cyclic of
order $q-1$: there exists a \emph{generator} (also called a \emph{primitive
element}, or a \emph{primitive root} when $q = p$) $g \in \F_q^{*}$ whose
powers sweep through every nonzero element:
\[
\F_q^{*} = \set{\, g^0, g^1, g^2, \dots, g^{q-2} \,}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{intuitionbox}
``Cyclic'' means a single element $g$, raised to successive powers, visits all
$q-1$ nonzero elements before returning to $1$. That single-cycle structure is
what makes discrete logarithms well behaved: every $h \in \F_q^{*}$ has a
unique exponent $k \in \set{0,\dots,q-2}$ with $g^k = h$, rather than the group
breaking into separate small loops.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{example}[A generator of $\F_7^{*}$]
\label{ch06:ex-gen7}
Here $\F_7^{*} = \set{1,2,3,4,5,6}$ has size $6$. Modulo $7$, the powers of
$g=3$ are
\[
3^1 \equiv 3,\quad 3^2 \equiv 2,\quad 3^3 \equiv 6,\quad
3^4 \equiv 4,\quad 3^5 \equiv 5,\quad 3^6 \equiv 1.
\]
All six nonzero elements appear, so $3$ is a generator. By contrast, $2$ is
not: $2^1 \equiv 2$, $2^2 \equiv 4$, $2^3 \equiv 1$, and then the powers
repeat, so $2$ has order $3$ and reaches only half the group. By Lagrange's
theorem, every element order divides $q-1=6$, giving orders in $\set{1,2,3,6}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A generator of $\F_8^{*}$]
\label{ch06:ex-gen8}
Build $\F_8 = \F_2[x]/(x^3+x+1)$, where $x^3 = x+1$. Is $x$ a generator of
$\F_8^{*}$? Its order must divide $q-1 = 7$. Since $7$ is prime, the only
divisors are $1$ and $7$; because $x \neq 1$, $\ord(x) = 7$ and $x$ generates
the whole group:
\begin{align*}
x^1 &= x, & x^2 &= x^2, & x^3 &= x+1, & x^4 &= x^2+x, \\
x^5 &= x^2+x+1, & x^6 &= x^2+1, & x^7 &= 1.
\end{align*}
Whenever $q-1$ is prime, every element of $\F_q^{*}$ except $1$ is a generator.
Figure~\ref{ch06:fig-cycle8} arranges these seven powers around a circle: each
clockwise step multiplies by $x$ modulo $x^3+x+1$.
\end{example}

\begin{whybox}
Why is $\F_q^{*}$ always cyclic? In any field, a degree-$d$ polynomial such as
$t^d - 1$ has at most $d$ roots, because each root $r$ factors out $(t - r)$ by
polynomial long division. Thus for every divisor $d$ of $q-1$, at most $d$
elements of $\F_q^{*}$ satisfy $t^d = 1$. Any finite abelian group with at most
$d$ solutions to $t^d = 1$ for every $d$ is cyclic. For a non-field ring like
$\Zn{8}$, the root bound fails: $t^2 - 1 \equiv 0 \pmod 8$ has four solutions
($1, 3, 5, 7$), exceeding degree $2$, which is why $\Zns{8}$ is not cyclic.
\end{whybox}

\begin{connectionbox}
By Lagrange's theorem (Chapter~5), every element order in $\F_q^{*}$ divides
$q-1$. In the AES field $\F_{2^8}$, $q-1 = 255 = 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 17$, so orders
divide $255$. There $x = \texttt{0x02}$ has order $51$ rather than $255$ and is
not a generator; $\texttt{0x03} = x+1$ has order $255$ and generates all $255$
nonzero bytes. Chapters~18 and~20 build public-key protocols inside large
cyclic groups where computing $g \mapsto g^k$ is fast while recovering $k$ from
$g^k$ is infeasible.
\end{connectionbox}

\subsection{Why AES lives in $\F_{2^8}$, summarized}
\label{ch06:why-aes}

Block ciphers must resist two standard attack families (Chapter~11):
\emph{linear cryptanalysis}, which looks for XOR equations that approximate how
input, output, and key bits relate, and \emph{differential cryptanalysis},
which tracks how fixed XOR differences in the input propagate to the output.
They also need \emph{diffusion}, meaning a change in one input byte spreads
quickly across many output bytes, while keeping every round reversible for
decryption. Figure~\ref{ch06:fig-aes-field-ops} shows the two AES round steps
built from $\F_{2^8}$:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Every byte is a field element.} Since $2^8 = 256$, the field
        elements match the $256$ possible byte values with none left over.
  \item \textbf{Addition is XOR.} Combining state bytes with key bytes or
        partial sums uses carry-free bitwise XOR.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{SubBytes} uses $a \mapsto a^{-1}$ in $\F_{2^8}$.} Field
        inversion does not obey $f(a \xor b) = f(a) \xor f(b)$, giving strong
        nonlinearity against linear and differential attacks while remaining a
        reversible one-to-one map on bytes.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{MixColumns} multiplies by an invertible $4\times 4$
        matrix over $\F_{2^8}$.} Its entries are $\texttt{0x01}$,
        $\texttt{0x02}$, and $\texttt{0x03}$, so mixing a four-byte column uses
        only \texttt{xtime} and XOR while making every output byte depend on
        all four input bytes.
\end{itemize}

\begin{attackbox}
What happens if you replace $\F_{2^8}$ multiplication in AES with integer
multiplication modulo $256$? The ring $\Zn{256}$ has zero divisors ($128 \cdot
2 \equiv 0 \pmod{256}$), and every even byte lacks a multiplicative inverse:
the S-box map $a \mapsto a^{-1}$ is undefined on half the inputs, and
\texttt{MixColumns} ceases to be invertible, breaking decryption. The same
failure occurs if you replace $m(x)$ with a \emph{reducible} degree-$8$
polynomial $g(x)h(x)$: then $g(x)\cdot h(x) \equiv 0$ in $\F_2[x]/(m(x))$,
creating nonzero bytes that multiply to $0$. Irreducibility of
$x^8+x^4+x^3+x+1$ is what guarantees every step can be undone.
\end{attackbox}

\section{The Frobenius Map and Square Roots}
\label{ch06:frobenius}

In a field of characteristic $p$, raising elements to the $p$-th power respects
both addition and multiplication. Recall that a map $\phi$ from a field to
itself is a \emph{field homomorphism} if $\phi(a+b) = \phi(a)+\phi(b)$ and
$\phi(a\cdot b) = \phi(a)\cdot\phi(b)$ for all $a,b$, and a \emph{field
automorphism} if it is also a bijection (Chapter~4).

\begin{theorem}[Freshman's dream / Frobenius]
\label{ch06:thm-frobenius}
In any field of characteristic $p$, for all elements $a$ and $b$,
\[
(a+b)^p = a^p + b^p.
\]
On $\F_{p^n}$, the map $\phi(a) = a^p$ is a field automorphism (the
\emph{Frobenius} map) that \emph{fixes} the base subfield $\F_p$ --- meaning
$\phi(a) = a$ if and only if $a \in \F_p$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
By the binomial theorem, $(a+b)^p = \sum_{k=0}^{p} \binom{p}{k} a^{p-k} b^k$,
where $\binom{p}{k} = \frac{p!}{k!(p-k)!}$ and $p! = 1 \cdot 2 \cdots p$. For
every middle term $0 < k < p$, the prime $p$ divides the numerator $p!$ but
none of the factors in $k!(p-k)!$, so $p$ divides $\binom{p}{k}$. In
characteristic $p$, any multiple of $p$ equals $0$, so all middle terms vanish
and $(a+b)^p = a^p + b^p$. Since $(ab)^p = a^p b^p$ holds in every commutative
ring, $\phi(a) = a^p$ is a homomorphism; furthermore, $\phi(a) = \phi(b)$
implies $(a-b)^p = 0$, forcing $a = b$, so $\phi$ is injective and therefore a
bijection on the finite set $\F_{p^n}$.

In characteristic $2$ the identity becomes $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + b^2$: squaring is
XOR-linear. Squaring a polynomial over $\F_2$ doubles every exponent without
cross terms, spreading its bits into the even degree slots before a fixed XOR
reduction modulo $m(x)$, which is faster than a general multiplication.
\end{whybox}

\begin{example}[Squaring and Frobenius orbits in $\F_8$]
\label{ch06:ex-square}
In $\F_8 = \F_2[x]/(x^3+x+1)$, where $x^3 = x+1$, squaring drops all cross
terms ($2x = 0$):
\begin{align*}
(x+1)^2 &= x^2 + 1, \\
(x^2+x)^2 &= x^4 + x^2 = x(x+1) + x^2 = (x^2 + x) + x^2 = x.
\end{align*}
Repeatedly applying the Frobenius map $\phi(a) = a^2$ traces each element's
\emph{Frobenius orbit} (Figure~\ref{ch06:fig-frobenius-f8}): for instance,
$x \mapsto x^2 \mapsto x^2 + x \mapsto x$. Because $\abs{\F_8} = 2^3$, three
applications return every element to itself: $\phi^3(a) = a^{8} = a$, the
identity map $\mathrm{id}(a) = a$.
\end{example}

\begin{connectionbox}
Because $a \mapsto a^2$ is a bijection on $\F_{2^n}$, every element $a \in
\F_{2^n}$ has a \emph{unique} square root, given explicitly by
\[
\sqrt{a} = a^{2^{\,n-1}}.
\]
To check this, square the right-hand side: $(a^{2^{n-1}})^2 = a^{2^n} = a$,
using $a^{2^n - 1} = 1$ for nonzero $a$ by Lagrange's theorem (and $0^{2^n} =
0$). Over prime fields $\F_p$ with odd $p$, by contrast, half the nonzero
elements have two square roots ($\pm r$) and half have none; finding them uses
the Tonelli--Shanks algorithm (a randomized polynomial-time method due to
Alberto Tonelli and Daniel Shanks), needed in Chapter~21 to recover an
elliptic-curve point's $y$-coordinate from $y^2$ and in Appendix~A to decrypt
Rabin ciphertexts $c \equiv m^2 \pmod{pq}$.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Summary}
\label{ch06:summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item A \textbf{finite field} exists for every prime-power size $q = p^n$ and
        for no other finite sizes, and is unique up to isomorphism
        (\autoref{ch06:thm-classification}).
  \item $\F_p = \Zn{p}$ is a field because every nonzero residue is coprime
        to $p$ and therefore invertible (\autoref{ch06:thm-fp}).
  \item $\F_{p^n}$ is constructed as $\F_p[x]/(m(x))$ for an
        \textbf{irreducible} polynomial $m(x)$ of degree $n$ over $\F_p$.
        Irreducibility prevents zero divisors and guarantees multiplicative
        inverses.
  \item In $\F_{2^n}$, elements are $n$-bit strings, addition is bitwise XOR,
        and multiplication is carry-less polynomial multiplication followed by
        reduction modulo $m(x)$. \textbf{AES uses} $\F_{2^8}$ with $m(x) =
        x^8+x^4+x^3+x+1$, where multiplication reduces to \texttt{xtime}
        (an $8$-bit left shift, XORed with \texttt{0x1B} when bit $7$ was $1$)
        plus XOR.
  \item The \textbf{multiplicative group} $\F_q^{*}$ is cyclic of order
        $q-1$ (\autoref{ch06:thm-cyclic}); its generators underpin discrete-log
        cryptography, and field inversion underpins the AES S-box.
  \item The \textbf{Frobenius} map $a \mapsto a^p$ is a field automorphism
        (\autoref{ch06:thm-frobenius}); in $\F_{2^n}$, squaring is XOR-linear
        and every element has the unique square root $a^{2^{n-1}}$.
\end{itemize}

AES, GCM, Diffie--Hellman, elliptic curves, and secret sharing all perform
their arithmetic inside finite fields.

Before we can define cryptographic security formally, we need one more
mathematical tool: probability. Chapter~7 introduces sample spaces, randomness,
entropy, and the birthday bound, which underlie Shannon's definition of perfect
secrecy and the symmetric ciphers of Part~II.

\section*{Exercises}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Inverses in $\F_{11}$]
\label{ch06:ex-inv11}
Compute the multiplicative inverse of each of $2,3,5,7$ in $\F_{11}$ using
the extended Euclidean algorithm. Verify each by multiplying back to $1$.
Which element of $\F_{11}^{*}$, besides $1$, is its own inverse?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[No field of size 6]
\label{ch06:ex-no6}
Prove directly that there is no field with $6$ elements. (Hint: what must the
characteristic be, and what does the vector-space counting argument after
\autoref{ch06:thm-classification} force?)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Build $\F_9$]
\label{ch06:ex-f9}
Verify that $x^2+1$ has no root in $\F_3 = \set{0,1,2}$ and is therefore
irreducible over $\F_3$. Construct $\F_9 = \F_3[x]/(x^2+1)$, list its $9$
elements, and compute $(x+1)(x+2)$ and $(2x+1)^{-1}$ inside it.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Irreducible hunting]
\label{ch06:ex-irred}
Find all monic irreducible polynomials of degree $4$ over $\F_2$ (over $\F_2$,
monic means degree~$4$, so leading term $x^4$). Start from all $16$ degree-$4$
polynomials, discard those with a root in $\F_2$, and discard
$(x^2+x+1)^2$. How many irreducibles remain, and what are they?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Multiply in the AES field]
\label{ch06:ex-aesmul}
Compute the following products in $\F_{2^8}$ with $m(x)=x^8+x^4+x^3+x+1$,
showing both the carry-less product and the reduction:
(a) $\texttt{0x02}\cdot\texttt{0x87}$;\quad
(b) $\texttt{0x03}\cdot\texttt{0x6E}$;\quad
(c) $\texttt{0x57}\cdot\texttt{0x13}$ (check that you get \texttt{0xFE}).
Verify each answer with \texttt{gf\_mul}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\texttt{xtime} chain]
\label{ch06:ex-xtimechain}
Starting from \texttt{0x01}, apply \texttt{xtime} repeatedly until you return
to \texttt{0x01}. How many steps does it take? What does that count tell you
about $\ord(\texttt{0x02})$ in $\F_{2^8}^{*}$, and is $x = \texttt{0x02}$ a
generator?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Generator search]
\label{ch06:ex-gensearch}
In $\F_{2^8}$, verify that $\texttt{0x03} = x+1$ is a primitive element by
showing that its order is $255$ and not any \emph{proper divisor} of $255$ (a
positive divisor strictly smaller than $255$). Explain why checking
$\texttt{0x03}^{255/r} \neq 1$ for the three prime factors
$r \in \set{3,5,17}$ is sufficient.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Break a broken cipher]
\label{ch06:ex-break}
Suppose an implementation of ``AES'' replaces the irreducible modulus
$x^8+x^4+x^3+x+1$ with the reducible polynomial $x^8+1 = (x+1)^8$ over $\F_2$.
Exhibit two nonzero bytes whose product is $0$ in $\F_2[x]/(x^8+1)$, and
explain in one sentence why \texttt{MixColumns} can no longer be inverted.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Frobenius cycles]
\label{ch06:ex-frob}
In $\F_{16} = \F_2[x]/(x^4+x+1)$, compute the Frobenius orbit of $x$ under
$\phi(a)=a^2$: list $x, x^2, x^4, x^8, x^{16}$ reduced modulo $x^4+x+1$.
Confirm that $x^{16}=x$ and that the orbit has length $4 = \deg m$. Which
elements of $\F_{16}$ have orbit length $2$, and what subfield do they form
together with $0$ and $1$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Square roots via Frobenius]
\label{ch06:ex-sqrt}
Using $\sqrt{a} = a^{2^{\,n-1}}$ in $\F_{2^n}$, find the square root of
\texttt{0x53} in the AES field $\F_{2^8}$ by computing $\texttt{0x53}^{128}$,
and verify your result by squaring it. Explain why raising $a$ to $2^{n-1}$
produces $\sqrt{a}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Counting irreducibles]
\label{ch06:ex-count}
The number of monic irreducible polynomials of degree $n$ over $\F_2$ is given
by Gauss's necklace formula:
\[
N_2(n) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{d \mid n}\mu(n/d)\,2^{d},
\]
where the sum runs over all positive divisors $d$ of $n$, and $\mu(k)$ is the
\emph{M\"obius function}: $\mu(1) = 1$, $\mu(k) = (-1)^r$ when $k$ is a product
of $r$ distinct primes, and $\mu(k) = 0$ when $k$ is divisible by the square of
a prime (for instance, $\mu(2) = -1$ and $\mu(4) = \mu(8) = 0$). Use this
formula to confirm that there are $30$ irreducibles of degree $8$ over $\F_2$,
and write down $N_2(128)$ (the pool for GCM's field $\F_{2^{128}}$) as a single
closed-form expression.
\end{exercise}
