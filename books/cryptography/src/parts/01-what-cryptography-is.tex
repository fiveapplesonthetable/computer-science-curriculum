\chapter{What Cryptography Is}

How do you share a secret when anyone might be watching? Imagine you write a note, fold it, and slide it across a crowded room to a friend. Between your hand and theirs, a dozen people could read it, copy it, change a word, or replace the note entirely with one of their own. Cryptography is the science of making that slip of paper behave as if the room were empty -- as if only your friend could read it, any tampering would be detected, and a forged note could be told apart from a genuine one. It does this with mathematics rather than locks and walls, and its central claim is that a few hundred bits of carefully chosen numbers can defeat an adversary with more computing power than exists on Earth.

Before building any cipher, we have to answer a prior question: when someone calls a system ``secure,'' secure against whom, given what, and proven how?

\section{Four Goals: What Are We Actually Protecting?}
\label{ch01:four-goals}

When people first hear ``cryptography'' they think ``secret codes'' -- hiding the contents of a message. That is one goal, and only one of four. A message that nobody can read is useless if an attacker can swap it for a different message, or impersonate the sender, or convince a court they never sent something they did. Real systems usually need several of these four guarantees at once.

\begin{description}
  \item[Confidentiality.] Only the intended recipient learns the contents of the original message, called the \emph{plaintext}. What crosses the channel is an encrypted form called the \emph{ciphertext}, from which an eavesdropper who sees every bit learns nothing useful about the plaintext (ideally, nothing beyond its length).
  \item[Integrity.] The recipient can detect if the message was altered in transit -- even by one bit, even by an attacker who controls the entire network. On an open network an attacker can always flip bits; integrity means the recipient will \emph{notice} and reject the modified message.
  \item[Authentication.] The recipient can verify who sent the message -- confirming it came from Alice rather than an impostor. This comes in two forms: \emph{entity authentication} (confirming who is on the other end of a live session right now) and \emph{data-origin authentication} (confirming that this specific message came from that party).
  \item[Non-repudiation.] The sender cannot later deny having sent the message. If Alice signs a contract, she cannot turn around and tell a judge ``that wasn't me.'' Unlike the first three goals, non-repudiation must convince an independent \emph{third party}, not only the recipient.
\end{description}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Each goal prevents a different lie.
\emph{Confidentiality} stops the lie ``I read your message.'' \emph{Integrity} stops the lie ``the message said X'' when it said Y. \emph{Authentication} stops the lie ``I am Alice.'' \emph{Non-repudiation} stops the lie ``I never said that'' -- told by Alice herself, after the fact. The first three protect the communicating pair against an outsider. The fourth protects each member of the pair against the \emph{other} in a future dispute.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why non-repudiation is the odd one out.} The first three goals can be achieved when Alice and Bob share a single secret key $k$: an \emph{authentication tag} -- a short check value computed from the message and $k$ -- proves \emph{to Bob} that Alice sent the message, because nobody outside the pair knows $k$. That same tag proves nothing \emph{to a judge}, because Bob knows $k$ too and could have computed the tag himself. With shared-key authentication, Alice can claim ``Bob faked it,'' and the judge cannot tell who is lying. Non-repudiation therefore demands an \emph{asymmetry}: Alice must hold a secret that Bob does \emph{not} have, so that a valid tag could only have come from Alice. A \emph{digital signature} provides this through a matched pair of keys -- a private signing key only Alice holds, and a public verification key anyone (including the judge) can use to check the signature. Shared-key cryptography alone cannot achieve non-repudiation (\S\ref{ch01:sym-vs-asym}).
\end{whybox}

These four goals are independent: a system can satisfy any one without the others, and confusing them is a classic source of broken designs. A WiFi network that encrypts traffic (confidentiality) while letting an attacker flip bits undetected (no integrity) can be tricked into leaking data or redirecting packets, as happened to early wireless standards. An email carrying a digital signature (authentication and non-repudiation) sent unencrypted (no confidentiality) can be read by anyone along the path. Encryption alone provides neither integrity nor authentication.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{``I encrypted it, so it can't be tampered with.''} Encryption by itself does not detect tampering. Many encryption schemes are \emph{malleable}: an attacker who cannot read the plaintext can flip chosen bits of the ciphertext to cause predictable changes in the decrypted plaintext. In a one-time pad -- a cipher that combines each plaintext bit with a secret key bit (Chapters~9 and~10) -- flipping bit $i$ of the ciphertext flips bit $i$ of the decrypted plaintext. Confidentiality and integrity require distinct mechanisms, which modern designs combine into \emph{authenticated encryption} -- single schemes that encrypt and authenticate together (\S\ref{ch01:composition}).
\end{trapbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
When you load a banking site over Hypertext Transfer Protocol Secure (HTTPS), which runs HTTP over Transport Layer Security (TLS, Chapter~25), all four goals appear. \emph{Confidentiality}: nobody on the coffee-shop WiFi reads your password. \emph{Integrity}: the network cannot alter the page to inject a fake login form without detection. \emph{Authentication}: the bank presents a \emph{certificate} -- a digitally signed statement binding the bank's domain name to its public key, issued by a \emph{certificate authority} (an organization your browser is preconfigured to trust) -- proving you reached the real bank. \emph{Non-repudiation} appears in the back end, where digitally signed transaction records let the bank prove an instruction was authorized if a transfer is disputed.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{What Cryptography Is Not}
\label{ch01:what-its-not}

Three related ideas are often confused with cryptography.

\textbf{Cryptography is not steganography.} Steganography \emph{hides the existence} of a message -- invisible ink, bits tucked into the least-significant color bits of an image (changing pixel shades too slightly for the eye to see), or a microscopic photograph called a microdot. Cryptography hides the \emph{contents} without hiding that communication occurred: an eavesdropper sees every bit of the ciphertext, only not what it means. Steganography fails the moment the hiding method is discovered, and as we require in \S\ref{ch01:kerckhoffs}, we assume any fixed hiding method will eventually leak. Systems that need both encrypt first and conceal the ciphertext second.

\textbf{Cryptography is not encoding.} Base64 (which writes binary bytes as printable text characters), the American Standard Code for Information Interchange (ASCII), Morse code, and URL-encoding are \emph{encodings}: public, reversible formats built for transport or compatibility, with no secret key. Anyone can invert them; there is no secret to keep. Calling Base64 ``encryption'' is a mistake with real consequences -- teams have shipped ``encrypted'' configuration files that were only Base64-encoded, readable by anyone in one command. Without a key, there is no cryptography.

\textbf{Cryptography is not a classical ``code.''} Historically a \emph{code} replaced whole words or phrases via a fixed codebook (``BLUEBIRD'' means ``attack at dawn''). Codes act on meaning; \emph{ciphers} act mechanically on symbols (letters or bits) regardless of meaning. Modern cryptography studies ciphers and their mathematical generalizations. The word ``code'' survives in unrelated terms like ``error-correcting code'' (which adds redundancy to fix transmission noise) and ``source code.''

\begin{trapbox}
``It's encrypted'' is a claim people make about anything they cannot read at a glance -- Base64 blobs, compressed files, binary formats, obfuscated source code. None of those use a secret key or resist a defined attacker, so none of them are encryption. Ask two questions: \emph{is there a secret key, and is there a stated adversary the scheme is built to defeat?} A ``no'' to either means there is no cryptography, however scrambled the output looks.
\end{trapbox}

\section{A Tiny Cipher, and Why It Already Fails}
\label{ch01:tiny-cipher}

To see why ``scrambling letters'' is not enough, encrypt a word by hand with the oldest cipher in the literature and watch how it breaks.

Take the \emph{shift cipher} (Caesar's cipher), shown in Figure~\ref{ch01:fig-caesar-wheel}. Number the alphabet $\texttt{A} = 0, \texttt{B} = 1, \dots, \texttt{Z} = 25$ and work \emph{modulo $26$} -- wrapping around from $25$ back to $0$ by taking the remainder after division by $26$, written $\bmod 26$ (for instance, $24 + 3 = 27$, which leaves remainder $1$). The secret key is a single shift $k \in \{0, 1, \dots, 25\}$. For a plaintext letter number $m \in \{0, \dots, 25\}$, the encryption function $\Enc_k$ shifts forward by $k$ and the decryption function $\Dec_k$ shifts a ciphertext letter number $c \in \{0, \dots, 25\}$ back by $k$:
\[
\Enc_k(m) = (m + k) \bmod 26, \qquad \Dec_k(c) = (c - k) \bmod 26,
\]
applied letter by letter. With $k = 3$, the plaintext \texttt{HELLO} maps as
\begin{align*}
\texttt{H}\,(7) &\mapsto (7 + 3) \bmod 26 = 10\,(\texttt{K}), &
\texttt{E}\,(4) &\mapsto (4 + 3) \bmod 26 = 7\,(\texttt{H}), \\
\texttt{L}\,(11) &\mapsto (11 + 3) \bmod 26 = 14\,(\texttt{O}), &
\texttt{O}\,(14) &\mapsto (14 + 3) \bmod 26 = 17\,(\texttt{R}),
\end{align*}
with both \texttt{L}s mapping to \texttt{O}, so the ciphertext is \texttt{KHOOR}. Subtracting $3$ modulo $26$ from each letter recovers \texttt{HELLO}.

The scheme is \emph{correct} -- decryption always inverts encryption -- which is the baseline requirement for any cipher and says nothing yet about security.

\begin{walkbox}
Before reading on, ask yourself: how many keys are there? If you intercepted \texttt{KHOOR} and knew only that a shift cipher was used (assuming, as in \S\ref{ch01:kerckhoffs}, that the attacker knows the algorithm), how long would it take you to read it?
\end{walkbox}

The \emph{keyspace} -- the set of all possible keys -- contains $26$ values, and $k=0$ leaves the text unchanged, leaving $25$ nontrivial shifts. An attacker tries them all. Trying every possible key in turn is called \emph{exhaustive key search} (or \emph{brute force}), and against a $26$-key cipher it finishes in seconds.

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Breaking the shift cipher two ways.} Intercept \texttt{KHOOR}.
\emph{Brute force:} shift back by $1,2,3,\dots$ and read off the candidate that forms English. Shifting back by $3$ gives \texttt{HELLO}; the other $24$ shifts give gibberish like \texttt{JGNNQ} or \texttt{GDKKN}. Twenty-five trials take under a minute by hand.
\emph{Frequency analysis:} for longer ciphertexts you do not even need to test keys. English letter frequencies are uneven -- \texttt{E} accounts for about $12.7\%$ of letters and \texttt{T} about $9.1\%$, whereas \texttt{Z} sits near $0.07\%$. As Figure~\ref{fig:ch01-freq-shift} shows, a shift cipher slides the entire letter-frequency bar chart sideways by $k$ positions without changing its shape. Find the tallest bar in the ciphertext, match it to \texttt{E}, and the horizontal offset gives $k$. The cipher fails because it \emph{preserves structure}: the statistical profile of the plaintext language passes straight through.
\end{attackbox}

The shift cipher is named for Julius Caesar, who according to Suetonius shifted his correspondence by three. It worked in an era when many adversaries were illiterate and systematic \emph{cryptanalysis} -- the study of breaking ciphers without knowing the key -- did not exist. Frequency analysis was first described by the polymath al-Kindi in 9th-century Baghdad, in a manuscript on deciphering encrypted messages. That one technique broke every cipher built on fixed single-letter replacement for the next thousand years (Chapter~2).

You might try to rescue the shift cipher by replacing the shift with an arbitrary \emph{permutation} -- a one-to-one rearrangement -- of all $26$ letters, forming the \emph{monoalphabetic substitution cipher}. Now any letter can map to any letter ($26$ choices for \texttt{A}, $25$ remaining for \texttt{B}, and so on), giving $26!$ (``$26$ factorial'') possible keys:
\[
26! = 26 \cdot 25 \cdot 24 \cdots 2 \cdot 1 \approx 4.03 \times 10^{26} \approx 2^{88.4}.
\]
Roughly four hundred septillion keys is far too many to search one by one, by hand or on a laptop.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{A huge keyspace does not imply security.} The substitution cipher has about $2^{88}$ keys and is solved over breakfast as a newspaper cryptogram. Why? A permutation, like a shift, maps each plaintext letter to one \emph{fixed} ciphertext letter, so it preserves the language's frequency counts: instead of sliding the bars of Figure~\ref{fig:ch01-freq-shift} in order, it shuffles which label sits under each bar while leaving the bar heights intact. The tallest bar is still almost certainly \texttt{E}, the most common two-letter pair (a \emph{digraph}) is likely \texttt{TH} or \texttt{HE}, and frequency analysis recovers the permutation letter by letter without searching the $2^{88}$ keys. \emph{A large keyspace is necessary for security, never sufficient.} What matters is whether the ciphertext leaks statistical structure -- which is why modern ciphers are built so their output cannot be told apart from uniform random bits.
\end{trapbox}

\section{The Cast: Alice, Bob, Eve, and Mallory}
\label{ch01:cast}

Cryptographers describe protocols using a standard cast of characters, where each name fixes a specific role and set of capabilities (Figure~\ref{ch01:fig-cast}).

\begin{description}
  \item[Alice and Bob] are the two parties who want to communicate securely. By convention Alice initiates and Bob responds. Even they do not fully trust each other when non-repudiation is at stake (\S\ref{ch01:four-goals}).
  \item[Eve] is the \emph{eavesdropper}. She is \emph{passive}: she sees everything that crosses the channel, copies it, stores it forever, and analyzes it at leisure, without altering, injecting, or deleting a bit. Eve models wiretaps, packet sniffers, and bulk traffic recording. Defeating Eve is the confidentiality problem.
  \item[Mallory] is the \emph{active} attacker -- the adversary in the middle (traditionally called a \emph{man-in-the-middle}, or MitM). Mallory can do everything Eve can, \emph{plus} modify messages, drop them, \emph{replay} old ones (re-sending a valid captured packet later, such as repeating a payment instruction), inject new ones, and impersonate either party. Defeating Mallory requires integrity and authentication beyond encryption. The name is a mnemonic: \textbf{Mal}icious, \textbf{Mal}leable, \textbf{M}an-in-the-\textbf{m}iddle.
  \item[Trent] is a \emph{trusted third party} -- an arbiter everyone agrees to believe, such as a certificate authority. Every trusted party is also a single point of failure and a high-value target, so good protocols minimize how much they trust Trent.
\end{description}

Multi-party protocols add \textbf{Carol} and \textbf{Dave} as extra honest participants. Later chapters introduce \textbf{Peggy} the \emph{prover} and \textbf{Victor} the \emph{verifier} (for zero-knowledge proofs, where Peggy convinces Victor she knows a secret without revealing it), along with \textbf{Sybil} (one attacker pretending to be many distinct participants at once).

\begin{focusbox}
The first question to ask about any attacker is \emph{passive or active?} A scheme can be confidential against Eve and yet broken by Mallory, because confidentiality says nothing about a message Mallory injected herself. Many protocol failures -- man-in-the-middle interception, packet replay, and \emph{downgrade attacks} (where an active attacker tricks two endpoints into negotiating an older, weaker cipher) -- happen when designers plan for Eve and forget Mallory.
\end{focusbox}

\section{Kerckhoffs's Principle: No Security Through Obscurity}
\label{ch01:kerckhoffs}

The ground rule of modern cipher design runs counter to first instinct: \emph{the security of a cryptographic system must depend only on the secrecy of the key, never on the secrecy of the algorithm.} You should be able to publish your entire design -- the cipher, the protocol, the source code, the fixed constants -- hand it to your enemy, and remain secure so long as the key stays secret.

Auguste Kerckhoffs, a Dutch linguist, stated six design rules for military ciphers in an 1883 article in the \emph{Journal des sciences militaires}. His second rule carries his name: a cipher must not require secrecy of its design, and falling into enemy hands must cause no harm. Founding the mathematical theory of secrecy in 1949, Claude Shannon restated it as ``the enemy knows the system'' -- \emph{Shannon's maxim}. Every modern standard -- the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES), the Rivest--Shamir--Adleman (RSA) cryptosystem, and TLS -- is public for that reason.

Three facts force this rule.

\textbf{Keys can be changed; algorithms cannot.} If your secret leaks and it was a key, you generate a fresh key in milliseconds. If your secret was the algorithm and it leaks, you must redesign, re-implement, re-deploy, and re-audit everything, often across deployed hardware you cannot recall. Secrets that are expensive to replace rarely get replaced in time.

\textbf{Secret algorithms do not get reviewed.} Confidence in a cipher comes from the failure of skilled cryptanalysts to break it. Keeping a design secret restricts review to the small team that built it -- the people least likely to spot their own blind spots. Public standards like AES survived years of open attack by independent researchers around the world before adoption.

\textbf{Obscurity always leaks.} Proprietary algorithms get reverse-engineered from compiled binaries, leaked by insiders, or extracted from chips. Treating the algorithm as secret stakes permanent security on a temporary condition.

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{A5/1 and the GSM phone network.} The A5/1 cipher protecting 2G Global System for Mobile Communications (GSM) voice calls was designed in secret in the late 1980s and kept unpublished as part of its defense. By 1999 the design had leaked and been reverse-engineered. Open analysis exposed severe structural flaws: by the late 2000s, attackers using precomputed lookup tables that trade disk space for fast key recovery (called \emph{rainbow tables}) could recover a call's temporary per-call \emph{session key} in seconds on desktop hardware. Once the obscurity vanished, little protection remained.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{trapbox}
Kerckhoffs's principle does \emph{not} say ``you must publish your internal source code'' or ``secrecy never helps.'' Keeping deployment details private can raise an attacker's cost as an extra layer (\emph{defense in depth}). The rule is narrower: you may never \emph{rely} on algorithm secrecy for security. If publishing your design breaks your system, your system was already broken; you had only not met a determined adversary yet.
\end{trapbox}

\paragraph{Enigma: a large keyspace defeated by structure and procedure.} The German military's Enigma machine of World War II drew its daily key from roughly $10^{23} \approx 2^{76.5}$ settings -- far beyond exhaustive search with 1940s hardware. Polish mathematicians led by Marian Rejewski broke it first, and Alan Turing's team at Britain's Bletchley Park built electromechanical \emph{bombes} to recover keys daily at scale. What defeated Enigma was not key counting: it was a structural flaw (the reflector wiring ensured no letter ever encrypted to itself, ruling out candidates immediately) combined with operator procedure (sending per-message key settings twice at the start of messages and encrypting predictable weather reports and military greetings). Even after the Allies held captured Enigma machines -- realizing Shannon's ``the enemy knows the system'' literally -- security rested on the daily key, and leaked structure gave that key away.

\section{Threat Models: Secure Against Whom, Given What?}
\label{ch01:threat-models}

``Is it secure?'' is an incomplete question. Secure against a casual observer with a laptop, or against a state intelligence agency with custom hardware and a court order? Secure against someone who only listens, or someone who can also choose which messages you encrypt? A \emph{threat model} specifies the adversary you are defending against: their goals, their access, and their computing budget. Without a threat model, ``secure'' has no testable meaning.

A threat model answers three questions.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{What is the attacker trying to do?} (Their \emph{goal}.) Read one message? Recover the secret key? Tell two candidate plaintexts apart? Forge one valid signature? Preventing a modest attacker goal gives a stronger guarantee: stopping the attacker from learning \emph{even one bit} about the plaintext is strictly stronger than stopping full plaintext recovery.
  \item \textbf{What can the attacker do?} (Their \emph{capabilities}.) Standard attack models, compared in Figure~\ref{fig:ch01-attack-models}, rank how much access the adversary has to encryption and decryption in action -- often modeled as an \emph{oracle}, a black box the attacker can query on chosen inputs without seeing the key inside:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \emph{Ciphertext-only attack (COA):} the attacker sees ciphertexts on the channel and nothing else. Surviving this is the bare minimum.
      \item \emph{Known-plaintext attack (KPA):} the attacker also sees some plaintext--ciphertext pairs $(m_i, c_i)$ they did not choose, such as standard protocol headers or predictable greetings.
      \item \emph{Chosen-plaintext attack (CPA):} the attacker can query an encryption oracle $\Enc_k(\cdot)$ to obtain ciphertexts for plaintexts \emph{of their choosing}. In practice an attacker can often get a web server or device to encrypt attacker-supplied data.
      \item \emph{Chosen-ciphertext attack (CCA):} the attacker can query both an encryption oracle $\Enc_k(\cdot)$ and a decryption oracle $\Dec_k(\cdot)$ on ciphertexts \emph{of their choosing} (except the target ciphertext itself). This models servers that process incoming ciphertexts and leak clues about the decrypted result.
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What resources does the attacker have?} Computing time, memory, \emph{side-channel} access (physical leaks from the hardware running the code, such as execution time, power draw, or electromagnetic radiation), and -- by Kerckhoffs's principle -- the full algorithm. Except when studying the one-time pad, we assume the attacker is \emph{computationally bounded}: limited to a large but finite number of operations, such as $2^{80}$ or $2^{100}$. An unbounded attacker capable of $2^{256}$ operations could brute-force any $256$-bit key, and no physical computer can approach that budget.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Design for a \emph{stronger} attacker than you expect to meet. A scheme that resists chosen-ciphertext attacks automatically resists passive eavesdropping; the reverse fails often. Real systems break when designers prove security against a polite attacker and deploy into a network facing an active one.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
The companion algorithms book makes ``computationally bounded'' quantitative using growth rates in the key length $n$ (in bits). A \emph{polynomial-time} algorithm takes at most a constant multiple of $n^c$ steps for some fixed exponent $c$ -- written $O(n^c)$ (``big-O of $n^c$'') -- whereas an \emph{exponential-time} search takes roughly $2^n$ steps, doubling every time $n$ increases by one bit. Modular exponentiation $g^e \bmod N$ (the core step in RSA and in Diffie--Hellman key exchange, Chapter~18) takes $O(\log_2 e)$ multiplications via repeated squaring -- proportional to the bit length of the exponent $e$ -- making legitimate encryption fast while attacks remain slow. The probability companion develops the \emph{birthday bound}, which shows why a cryptographic hash function (a function compressing arbitrary inputs into fixed-length fingerprints) needs $2n$ output bits to make accidental matches require roughly $2^n$ trials.
\end{connectionbox}

\subsection*{How big is $2^{128}$?}

Suppose you build a key-search chip that tests one key every nanosecond -- $10^9$ keys per second. Searching all $2^{128}$ keys takes
\begin{align*}
\frac{2^{128}\text{ keys}}{10^{9}\text{ keys/s}}
  &\approx \frac{3.4 \times 10^{38}}{10^{9}}\text{ s} = 3.4 \times 10^{29}\text{ s}, \\[3pt]
\frac{3.4 \times 10^{29}\text{ s}}{3.15 \times 10^{7}\text{ s/year}}
  &\approx 1.1 \times 10^{22}\text{ years}.
\end{align*}
That is nearly a trillion times the age of the universe ($\approx 1.4 \times 10^{10}$ years). Running a billion such chips in parallel cuts nine powers of ten and still needs $10^{13}$ years (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01-keyspace-scale}). Physics sets a floor too: Landauer's principle -- the thermodynamic law that flipping or erasing an irreversible bit dissipates a minimum amount of heat -- implies that counting through $2^{128}$ states at room temperature would consume far more energy than humanity generates. Exhaustive search cannot touch a randomly chosen $128$-bit key.

Modern cryptography rests on an engineered cost gap: legitimate users spend a fraction of a microsecond (say, $2^{10}$ to $2^{20}$ operations) while any attacker without the key faces an infeasible budget ($2^{128}$ operations). Every scheme in this book aims to build that gap and to \emph{prove} that no shortcut collapses it.
\end{focusbox}

\section{Randomness: The Resource Everything Rests On}
\label{ch01:randomness}

Every secure scheme consumes \emph{unpredictability}. Keys must be \emph{random} -- chosen by a physical or mathematical process whose outcome the attacker cannot predict or reproduce. A strong cipher fed a predictable key is no safer than no cipher at all, because the attacker predicts the key.

To measure this: if you generate a $128$-bit key by flipping $128$ \emph{uniform, independent} bits -- meaning each bit is $0$ or $1$ with equal probability $1/2$ (\emph{uniform}), and knowing any subset of bits gives zero information about the rest (\emph{independent}) -- the attacker faces all $2^{128}$ equally likely keys of Figure~\ref{fig:ch01-keyspace-scale}. Now suppose your random-number generator has a bug and only ever draws keys from a pool of $2^{20} \approx 10^6$ possibilities. The key is still $128$ bits \emph{long}, yet it lacks $128$ bits of unpredictability. An attacker who knows the generator's code (\S\ref{ch01:kerckhoffs}) searches the $2^{20}$ possible outputs in a millisecond. What protects a key is its \emph{entropy} -- the measure of genuine unpredictability, counted in bits, where $k$ bits of entropy correspond to $2^k$ equally likely possibilities -- rather than the number of bits used to store it.

\begin{intuitionbox}
A sound cipher cannot rescue predictable randomness. Many major real-world breaks leave the cipher untouched -- AES and RSA remain unbroken -- and instead exploit predictable keys, reused \emph{nonces} (per-message ``numbers used once,'' such as fresh random values or counters), or guessable \emph{seeds} (the initial secret inputs fed into a random-bit generator). When auditing a deployment, check the randomness source first.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{When the dice are loaded.} In 2008 a Debian Linux maintainer, silencing a memory-debugger warning, accidentally deleted a line that fed operating-system entropy into OpenSSL (a widely deployed open-source cryptography library). For nearly two years, keys generated on affected machines came from a pool of roughly $2^{15} = 32{,}768$ states -- determined largely by the \emph{process ID}, a small integer the operating system assigns to each running program. Keys formatted as full $2048$-bit RSA or Secure Shell (SSH) keys could be enumerated by an attacker in minutes, forcing administrators worldwide to \emph{revoke} (publicly mark invalid) and replace millions of keys. Embedded routers have suffered the same failure at first boot before collecting enough hardware noise, causing thousands of independent devices to generate identical RSA prime factors.
\end{attackbox}

Practical systems split random generation into two stages (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01-entropy-bottleneck}). A \emph{true random number generator} (TRNG) harvests physical noise such as thermal fluctuations or interrupt timing jitter into a short, high-entropy seed. A \emph{cryptographically secure pseudo-random number generator} (CSPRNG) then deterministically stretches that seed into a long bit stream that no efficient adversary can tell apart from true coin flips. Because the CSPRNG itself is deterministic, its output can never have more unpredictability than its seed: feed it $15$ bits of entropy and even a $2048$-bit key takes at most $2^{15}$ values.

\begin{trapbox}
Standard library random generators -- C's \texttt{rand()}, Python's \texttt{random} module, JavaScript's \texttt{Math.random()} -- are \emph{not} cryptographically secure. They are designed for simulations: fast, statistically even, and \emph{predictable}, because observing a handful of outputs lets an attacker reconstruct the internal state and predict every past and future draw. Never generate keys, nonces, or session tokens with them. Always call the operating system's cryptographic generator (\texttt{/dev/urandom}, \texttt{getrandom()}, Python's \texttt{secrets} or \texttt{os.urandom}, Java's \texttt{SecureRandom}). The \texttt{os.urandom} call in the listing of \S\ref{ch01:what-is-secure} uses the right source; that example breaks only because it reuses the output.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Saying a stretched seed ``looks random'' to every efficient adversary defines a \emph{pseudo-random generator} (Chapter~10 and Chapter~17), whose security reduces to a computational hardness assumption. The probability companion develops \emph{statistical distance} -- a measure of how far two probability distributions sit from each other -- to state ``indistinguishable from uniform,'' while the number-theory chapters introduce hard modular arithmetic problems (such as distinguishing squares from non-squares modulo a composite integer) from which provably secure generators can be built.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Symmetric vs.\ Asymmetric: A First Look}
\label{ch01:sym-vs-asym}

Every encryption scheme in this book follows the same three-algorithm syntax: one algorithm generates keys, one encrypts, and one decrypts.

\begin{definition}[Symmetric encryption scheme]
\label{ch01:def-scheme}
A symmetric (private-key) encryption scheme is a triple of algorithms $(\Gen, \Enc, \Dec)$ over a set of allowed plaintexts $\mathcal{M}$ (the \emph{message space}), a set of keys $\mathcal{K}$ (the \emph{key space}), and a set of ciphertexts $\mathcal{C}$ (the \emph{ciphertext space}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\Gen$ outputs a key $k \in \mathcal{K}$, typically chosen uniformly at random from $\mathcal{K}$, written $k \getsr \mathcal{K}$ (where $\getsr$ means ``sample uniformly at random'').
  \item $\Enc$ takes a key $k$ and message $m \in \mathcal{M}$ and outputs a ciphertext $c \gets \Enc_k(m)$, where $\gets$ records the output of a possibly randomized algorithm. For security against active or repeated-message attacks, $\Enc$ \emph{must} use fresh randomness (\S\ref{ch01:what-is-secure}).
  \item $\Dec$ takes a key $k$ and ciphertext $c \in \mathcal{C}$ and deterministically outputs a message $m = \Dec_k(c)$ (or a rejection symbol if $c$ is invalid).
\end{itemize}
The scheme is \emph{correct} if decryption inverts encryption: for every key $k$ that $\Gen$ can output (its \emph{support}) and every $m \in \mathcal{M}$,
\[
\Dec_k(\Enc_k(m)) = m.
\]
\end{definition}

Definition~\ref{ch01:def-scheme} pins down syntax and correctness while saying nothing about security: the shift cipher of \S\ref{ch01:tiny-cipher} satisfies every line of Definition~\ref{ch01:def-scheme} and fails against a one-minute attack. Cryptographic schemes split into two families according to how the encryption key relates to the decryption key (Figure~\ref{ch01:fig-sym-asym}).

\textbf{Symmetric (secret-key) cryptography.} Alice and Bob share one secret key $k$, which both encrypts and decrypts:
\[
c = \Enc_k(m), \qquad m = \Dec_k(c).
\]
Symmetric ciphers are fast -- modern processors encrypt gigabytes per second with AES -- and protect nearly all bulk data in transit and at rest. Their obstacle is the \emph{key-distribution problem}: before communicating, Alice and Bob must already share $k$ over a secure channel. Two people who meet in person can exchange $k$ beforehand, whereas a browser connecting to an unfamiliar website cannot.

\textbf{Asymmetric (public-key) cryptography.} Each participant generates a linked \emph{key pair}: a public key $pk$ published to the world, and a private (secret) key $sk$ kept to themselves. What one key does, only the other can undo. To encrypt a message to Bob, anyone uses Bob's public key $pk_B$, and only Bob can decrypt with his private key $sk_B$:
\[
c = \Enc_{pk_B}(m), \qquad m = \Dec_{sk_B}(c).
\]
For digital signatures the roles reverse: Alice signs with her private key $sk_A$, and anyone verifies with $pk_A$, achieving authentication and non-repudiation together. Public-key schemes -- RSA, Diffie--Hellman, and elliptic-curve cryptography (which builds groups out of points on algebraic curves over finite fields to get smaller keys than RSA, Chapter~21) -- solve key distribution with zero prior shared secrets, at the cost of running hundreds to thousands of times slower than symmetric ciphers.

\begin{intuitionbox}
\emph{Symmetric} encryption is a padlock with two identical keys held by Alice and Bob. \emph{Asymmetric} encryption is a locked street mailbox: anyone can drop a letter through the public slot ($pk_B$), while only the owner holding the brass key ($sk_B$) can open the door and read what is inside. Broadcasting the mailbox's location and slot dimensions on television does not help a bystander open the box.
\end{intuitionbox}

Deployed protocols combine both in \emph{hybrid} constructions (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01-hybrid}). Because public-key arithmetic is slow, endpoints run it once at the start of a connection to establish a fresh, shared symmetric session key $k$ across the untrusted network, and then hand off bulk data encryption to fast symmetric primitives like AES-GCM. Every HTTPS session works this way: an asymmetric handshake solves key distribution, and a symmetric cipher carries the megabytes.

\begin{historybox}
Until the 1970s, all known cryptography was symmetric, and key distribution depended on couriers, guarded codebooks, and physical meetings. The public breakthrough came in 1976 from Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman (building on ideas of Ralph Merkle), followed in 1977 by the RSA scheme of Ronald Rivest, Adi Shamir, and Leonard Adleman. Declassified archives later revealed that researchers at the British Government Communications Headquarters (GCHQ) -- James Ellis, Clifford Cocks, and Malcolm Williamson -- had found the same constructions between 1970 and 1973 under strict secrecy. Academic publication prevailed despite US export-control pressure, enabling web commerce and encrypted messaging. Today's frontier for public-key cryptography is post-quantum migration: because Shor's algorithm on a large quantum computer could factor integers and compute \emph{discrete logarithms} (recovering $x$ from $g^x \bmod p$) in polynomial time, standards are transitioning toward \emph{lattice-based} problems -- geometric questions on high-dimensional grids of points that resist known quantum algorithms (Chapter~35).
\end{historybox}

\section{What Does ``Secure'' Even Mean?}
\label{ch01:what-is-secure}

Cryptographic history is full of ciphers that experts \emph{felt} were secure until someone broke them. Modern cryptography replaces ``I cannot see how to break it'' with a testable mathematical statement: ``either no efficient attacker can win this security game, or winning it solves a problem mathematicians have tried and failed to solve for decades.''

\subsection*{Why intuition fails}

Consider a plausible-sounding argument: ``I combine my message with a pseudo-random \emph{keystream} -- a sequence of random-looking key bits $k$ as long as the message -- using \emph{exclusive-OR} (XOR, written $\xor$). Because $k$ looks random, the ciphertext looks random, so the attacker learns nothing.'' Here XOR compares two equal-length bit strings position by position, outputting $0$ where bits match ($0 \xor 0 = 0$, $1 \xor 1 = 0$) and $1$ where they differ ($0 \xor 1 = 1$, $1 \xor 0 = 1$). Because every bit cancels itself ($x \xor x = 0$) and XORing with $0$ leaves any bit unchanged ($x \xor 0 = x$), encryption $c = m \xor k$ is undone by XORing with $k$ a second time: $c \xor k = m \xor (k \xor k) = m$.

That argument implicitly assumes the attacker sees only \emph{one} ciphertext under $k$. Reuse the same keystream $k$ on two equal-length plaintexts $m_1$ and $m_2$ to produce $c_1 = m_1 \xor k$ and $c_2 = m_2 \xor k$, and an eavesdropper XORs the two ciphertexts together:
\begin{align*}
c_1 \xor c_2
  &= (m_1 \xor k) \xor (m_2 \xor k) &&\text{(definition of $c_1$ and $c_2$)} \\
  &= (m_1 \xor m_2) \xor (k \xor k) &&\text{(reordering bit-by-bit XOR)} \\
  &= m_1 \xor m_2 &&\text{(since $k \xor k = 0$)}.
\end{align*}
The secret keystream $k$ vanishes completely, leaving the XOR of the two plaintexts (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01-keystream-reuse}) -- enough to recover both messages when they are English text or structured network packets, and immediate total compromise if either plaintext is known. The intuition answered the wrong threat model: one message instead of two. Keystream reuse broke the Wired Equivalent Privacy (WEP) WiFi standard, wartime Soviet one-time-pad traffic decoded by the US--UK VENONA codebreaking project, and Microsoft's Point-to-Point Tunneling Protocol (PPTP).

\begin{trapbox}
``It looks random'' is not a security argument. ``I tried for an afternoon and couldn't break it'' is not a security argument. ``It has been deployed for years'' is not a security argument (A5/1 was). What counts is a precise claim against a stated adversary, backed either by a mathematical reduction or by years of open cryptanalysis under strong incentives. Anything less is hope.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection*{The three ingredients of a security definition}

Every modern security definition specifies three components together; omit any one and the claim loses meaning.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A security goal} -- what the attacker must fail to achieve. For encryption the standard goal is \emph{indistinguishability}: after choosing any two plaintexts $m_0$ and $m_1$ of equal bit-length ($|m_0| = |m_1|$, since standard encryption does not hide message length), the attacker cannot tell whether a challenge ciphertext encrypts $m_0$ or $m_1$ noticeably better than a fair coin toss. Indistinguishability rules out leaking \emph{any} partial information, such as whether a salary exceeds $\$100\text{k}$.
  \item \textbf{A threat model} -- what queries and access the attacker gets (\S\ref{ch01:threat-models}): eavesdropping only, chosen-plaintext queries, or chosen-ciphertext queries, along with a polynomial time bound.
  \item \textbf{A quantified bound} -- how close the attacker's winning probability stays to random guessing. Because any attacker can guess a key by sheer luck with tiny probability $2^{-n}$, we require the attacker's edge over guessing to be \emph{negligible}: a function of the key length $n$ that shrinks faster than $1/n^c$ for every constant $c > 0$.
\end{enumerate}

Combining the three: a scheme is \emph{secure} if every polynomial-time adversary operating within the threat model achieves at most a negligible advantage at the stated goal.

\begin{formalbox}
We formalize security as a game between a challenger and an adversary algorithm $\mathcal{A}$. The adversary $\mathcal{A}$ runs in \emph{probabilistic polynomial time} ($\PPT$) -- meaning $\mathcal{A}$ may toss random coins and runs in time bounded by a polynomial in the security parameter $n$ (typically the key length in bits). For a binary distinguishing game where random guessing wins with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ (writing $\Pr[\cdot]$ for probability), the scheme is secure if for every $\PPT$ adversary $\mathcal{A}$,
\[
\Pr[\,\mathcal{A} \text{ wins}\,] \le \frac{1}{2} + \negl(n),
\]
where $\negl(n)$ denotes a \emph{negligible} function: for every polynomial $p(n)$, $\negl(n) < 1/p(n)$ for all sufficiently large $n$ (for example, $2^{-n}$ is negligible, whereas $1/n^3$ is not). Chapters~8 and~9 develop this framework in full.
\end{formalbox}

Figure~\ref{ch01:fig-ind-game} traces the indistinguishability game step by step. The adversary $\mathcal{A}$ picks two equal-length messages $m_0, m_1$; the challenger generates a key $k \getsr \mathcal{K}$, flips a secret fair coin $b \getsr \{0,1\}$, encrypts $m_b$, and returns the challenge ciphertext $c^*$; finally $\mathcal{A}$ outputs a guess $b' \in \{0,1\}$ and wins if $b' = b$. When $\mathcal{A}$ sees only $c^*$, this defines \emph{indistinguishability against an eavesdropper} (IND-EAV); when $\mathcal{A}$ may also query an encryption oracle $\Enc_k(\cdot)$ on arbitrary messages before and after seeing $c^*$, it defines \emph{indistinguishability under chosen-plaintext attack} (IND-CPA).

Why subtract $\frac{1}{2}$ instead of quoting $\Pr[b'=b]$ directly? An adversary that ignores $c^*$ and outputs a random bit $b' \getsr \{0,1\}$ already wins half the time, so $\Pr[b'=b] = \frac{1}{2}$ means ``learned nothing,'' not ``always wrong.'' Cryptographers therefore measure $\mathcal{A}$'s \emph{advantage}, written $\Adv(\mathcal{A})$:
\[
\Adv(\mathcal{A}) = 2\Bigl|\Pr[b' = b] - \frac{1}{2}\Bigr|,
\]
twice the margin by which $\mathcal{A}$ beats blind guessing (the factor $2$ stretches the scale to run from $0$ to $1$; the absolute value is there because an algorithm that was consistently \emph{wrong}, say winning with probability $0.01$, could flip its answer to win with probability $0.99$). Winning with probability $0.5000001$ gives advantage $2 \times 10^{-7}$; winning with probability $0.75$ gives advantage $0.5$, a complete break; winning with probability $0.5$ gives advantage $0$.

\begin{example}[Why deterministic encryption loses the game]
\label{ch01:ex-deterministic}
Suppose $\Enc_k$ is \emph{deterministic}: encrypting the same plaintext under $k$ always produces the identical ciphertext (as in the shift cipher). Then $\mathcal{A}$ wins the IND-CPA game every time. First $\mathcal{A}$ picks any two distinct messages $m_0 \ne m_1$ of equal length, queries the encryption oracle on $m_0$ to learn $c_0 = \Enc_k(m_0)$, and submits $(m_0, m_1)$ as its challenge. On receiving $c^*$, $\mathcal{A}$ outputs $b' = 0$ if $c^* = c_0$ and $b' = 1$ otherwise. It wins with probability $1$, achieving the maximum possible advantage $\Adv(\mathcal{A}) = 2\bigl|1 - \frac{1}{2}\bigr| = 1$. \emph{CPA-secure encryption cannot be deterministic}: encrypting the same plaintext twice must (with overwhelming probability) yield different ciphertexts so an observer cannot tell when a message repeats.
\end{example}

\begin{connectionbox}
Why draw the line between polynomial fractions $1/n^c$ and negligible functions $\negl(n)$? Because of \emph{amplification}, developed in the companion probability book: if an efficient attack succeeds with probability $1/n^c$, repeating it a polynomial number of times boosts success close to $1$, whereas repeating a $2^{-n}$-probability event a polynomial number of times leaves the total probability negligible -- verifiable in one line via the \emph{union bound}, which states that the probability that at least one of $q$ events occurs is at most the sum of their individual probabilities ($q \cdot 2^{-n}$).
\end{connectionbox}

\subsection*{Two routes to confidence: perfect and computational security}

Security guarantees rest on one of two foundations.

\emph{Information-theoretic (perfect) security} guarantees that the ciphertext reveals zero information about the plaintext even to an adversary with unbounded computing power. Shannon proved that the one-time pad achieves this, at a steep price: the secret key must be at least as long as every message encrypted and can never be reused (Chapter~9).

\emph{Computational security} -- used by nearly every deployed protocol -- allows the ciphertext to determine the plaintext uniquely in principle, so long as recovering it requires more work than any $\PPT$ adversary can perform. Because complexity theory cannot yet prove unconditionally that problems like integer factoring require super-polynomial time, security proofs are \emph{conditional}: they show that if a well-studied mathematical problem $P$ is hard, then scheme $S$ is secure.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why reductions anchor modern cryptography.} Suppose we prove: ``any $\PPT$ adversary $\mathcal{A}$ that breaks encryption scheme $S$ can be converted into a $\PPT$ algorithm $\mathcal{B}$ that factors large integers.'' Then breaking $S$ is \emph{at least as hard} as factoring. Nobody has proved factoring hard unconditionally, yet a reduction concentrates all risk onto one public problem that number theorists have attacked for centuries without finding a fast classical algorithm. Rather than asking users to trust a designer's intuition, almost every theorem in this book takes the form \emph{if problem $P$ is hard, then scheme $S$ is secure}, proved by building a wrapper $\mathcal{B}$ around a hypothetical attacker $\mathcal{A}$ (Figure~\ref{ch01:fig-reduction}).
\end{whybox}

import os

def xor(a, b):
    return bytes(x ^ y for x, y in zip(a, b))

m1 = b"attack at dawn!!"
m2 = b"retreat at noon."   # same length as m1

# THE BUG: one keystream reused for two messages.
keystream = os.urandom(len(m1))
c1 = xor(m1, keystream)
c2 = xor(m2, keystream)

# The attacker never sees the key, yet:
leak = xor(c1, c2)
# leak == m1 XOR m2, completely independent of the key.
assert leak == xor(m1, m2)

# If the attacker guesses (or knows) m1, m2 falls out for free:
recovered_m2 = xor(leak, m1)
assert recovered_m2 == m2
print("recovered:", recovered_m2)   # b"retreat at noon."
\end{lstlisting}
Although \texttt{os.urandom} produced an unpredictable keystream, reusing it across two messages violated the one-time pad's single-use requirement. Security depends on matching both the threat model and the usage rules of the primitive.
\end{implementbox}

\section{The Security Mindset}
\label{ch01:mindset}

A builder asks how to make a system work when used properly; a cryptographer asks how it fails when someone tries to make it fail. The security mindset is the habit of hunting for the gap between what a designer assumed and what an adversary can do.

When you inspect any scheme, ask five questions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What is the secret, and what happens when it leaks?} If the answer is ``the algorithm,'' stop -- Kerckhoffs's principle rules that out.
  \item \textbf{Who is the attacker?} Passive Eve or active Mallory? Ciphertext-only or chosen-ciphertext? Name the strongest realistic adversary and test the scheme against \emph{that} one, not a convenient weaker one.
  \item \textbf{What is the security goal, stated as a win condition the attacker must fail to achieve?} Without a crisp win condition, you cannot tell a secure scheme from a broken one.
  \item \textbf{Where is the reduction or the public scrutiny?} Does security rest on a reduction to a hard problem like factoring, or on years of unbroken open cryptanalysis (as with AES)? Either can justify deployment, provided you know which one you are trusting.
  \item \textbf{What did the mathematical model leave out?} A proof about abstract inputs and outputs says nothing about a side channel -- timing variations, power draw, or distinct error messages -- that the model omitted.
\end{itemize}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Padding-oracle attacks: when the model and the deployment diverge.} Block ciphers like AES encrypt fixed-size blocks (for instance, $16$ bytes at a time), so a message whose length is not a multiple of $16$ has extra bytes appended in a standard pattern -- called \emph{padding} -- before encryption, and the receiver checks and strips that pattern after decrypting. A \emph{padding-oracle} attack targets a server that, on receiving a modified ciphertext, reveals -- via an error code or a response-time difference -- \emph{whether the decrypted bytes ended in valid padding}. Repeating queries and watching that single leaked bit per query lets an active attacker recover the plaintext byte by byte without knowing the key (Chapter~13). The cipher itself can be provably CPA-secure while the deployed server hands the attacker a chosen-ciphertext oracle that the CPA proof never modeled. Authenticated encryption closes the gap by rejecting tampered ciphertexts before decryption or padding checks ever run.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Cryptographic thinking is disciplined skepticism: not ``everything is dangerous,'' which leads to paralysis, rather ``write down every assumption, and assume the adversary exploits anything you left unstated.'' Once your guarantees are stated explicitly, test them by publishing the design and inviting skilled outsiders to break it.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
The Signal messaging protocol applies this mindset to key compromise itself. \emph{Forward secrecy} protects the past: if your phone is seized and its current keys extracted today, yesterday's messages stay unreadable because each message key was deleted right after use. \emph{Post-compromise security} (``future secrecy'') heals the future: even after a temporary key leak, fresh Diffie--Hellman exchanges mix into the session state through a one-way \emph{ratchet} -- a key-updating function that steps forward yet cannot be run backward -- restoring secrecy for subsequent messages (Chapter~27). Both properties come from asking ``what if the attacker gets the key anyway?'' before writing a line of protocol code.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Composing the Goals: A Single Message, Several Guarantees}
\label{ch01:composition}

Real messages usually need confidentiality and integrity together, and the order in which you combine two secure primitives determines whether the combined system is safe. Suppose Alice wants to send Bob a message $m$ that Eve cannot read and Mallory cannot alter undetected. Alice has a CPA-secure encryption scheme $(\Enc_{k_1}, \Dec_{k_1})$ and a \emph{message authentication code} (MAC) -- a keyed function $t = \MAC_{k_2}(x)$ that computes a short authentication tag $t$ using an independent secret key $k_2$, accompanied by a verification check $\Vrfy_{k_2}(x, t)$ that recomputes the tag and rejects (outputting the failure symbol $\bot$, read ``bottom'') whenever $x$ or $t$ was modified. Writing $a \concat b$ for concatenating bit strings $a$ and $b$ end to end, Alice has three natural ways to combine them (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01-three-compositions}):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Encrypt-and-MAC}: send $\Enc_{k_1}(m) \concat \MAC_{k_2}(m)$. Because the tag is computed directly on the plaintext $m$ and sent in the clear alongside the ciphertext, any deterministic MAC produces the same tag whenever Alice sends the same $m$ twice, leaking message repeats and potentially partial information about $m$. Encrypt-and-MAC therefore fails confidentiality.
  \item \textbf{MAC-then-encrypt}: compute $t = \MAC_{k_2}(m)$ and send $\Enc_{k_1}(m \concat t)$. Hiding $t$ inside the encryption avoids the plaintext-tag leak, yet Bob cannot see $t$ until \emph{after} he runs $\Dec_{k_1}$ on whatever ciphertext arrived off the network. Running decryption and padding removal on attacker-chosen ciphertexts before checking authenticity is the opening exploited by padding-oracle attacks (\S\ref{ch01:mindset}).
  \item \textbf{Encrypt-then-MAC}: compute $c \gets \Enc_{k_1}(m)$, then tag the \emph{ciphertext} via $t = \MAC_{k_2}(c)$, and send $c \concat t$ (Figure~\ref{ch01:fig-etm}). Bob runs $\Vrfy_{k_2}(c, t)$ on the ciphertext first; if verification fails, he outputs $\bot$ and drops the packet \emph{without ever calling $\Dec_{k_1}$}. With a CPA-secure cipher, a secure MAC, and independent keys $k_1, k_2$, Encrypt-then-MAC is provably CCA-secure (Chapters~15 and~16).
\end{itemize}

The broader lesson is that \emph{combining secure pieces does not automatically yield a secure system}. Modern libraries avoid manual composition errors by offering a single interface called \emph{authenticated encryption with associated data} (AEAD) -- such as AES in Galois/Counter Mode (AES-GCM) or ChaCha20-Poly1305 (which pairs the ChaCha20 stream cipher with the Poly1305 MAC) -- delivering confidentiality and integrity together in a proven order, while also authenticating unencrypted packet headers (``associated data''). In production code, reach for an AEAD cipher rather than bolting encryption and MACs together by hand.

\begin{applicationbox}
TLS~1.3, the protocol securing modern HTTPS traffic, permits \emph{only} AEAD ciphers (such as AES-GCM and ChaCha20-Poly1305) for record encryption. The designers deleted the older MAC-then-encrypt cipher suites from the standard altogether after a decade of padding-oracle vulnerabilities proved that leaving composition to implementers invited recurring breaks. Making the safe composition the only option eliminated an entire class of protocol bugs.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{What's Ahead}
\label{ch01:roadmap}

Chapter~2 traces the history of classical ciphers from Caesar through multi-alphabet ciphers and Enigma, showing in detail how leaked language patterns undid centuries of designs. Chapters~3 through~8 then build the mathematical tools the rest of the book relies on: modular arithmetic, prime numbers and greatest common divisors, algebraic structures (groups, rings, and finite fields -- finite number systems where addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division by nonzero elements all work cleanly), probability, and computational hardness. Part~II develops shared-key cryptography -- the one-time pad, stream and block ciphers including the Data Encryption Standard (DES) and AES, hash functions, MACs and hash-based MACs (HMAC), and AEAD -- while Part~III drops the shared-key assumption to build Diffie--Hellman key exchange, RSA, elliptic-curve schemes, and digital signatures. Part~IV assembles those pieces into deployed protocols: TLS, WiFi up through Wi-Fi Protected Access~3 (WPA3), Signal, SSH, cellular networks, and virtual private networks (VPNs). Across every chapter, keep asking the same three questions from this one: secure against whom, given what, and proven how?

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch01:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Classify the goal]
For each scenario, name which of the four goals (confidentiality, integrity, authentication, non-repudiation) is the \emph{primary} one at stake, and name any secondary goals also required.
(a) A software vendor publishes a download alongside a value so users can check the file was not corrupted or swapped.
(b) You want to email a colleague a document so that no eavesdropper can read it.
(c) A bank needs to prove in court that a customer authorized a wire transfer.
(d) A login server must be sure the client connecting holds the account password and is not an impostor.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Break a shift cipher by hand]
You intercept the ciphertext \texttt{WKHUHLVQRVHFXULWB} and know only that a shift cipher (\S\ref{ch01:tiny-cipher}) was used.
(a) Recover the key $k$ and the plaintext by exhaustive search over the $25$ nontrivial shifts. (Hint: the plaintext is an English sentence, spaces removed.)
(b) Explain why the size of the keyspace gave you no protection here.
(c) The substitution cipher has $26! \approx 2^{88.4}$ keys, yet is also broken quickly. Name the single property both ciphers share that makes frequency analysis work, and describe what a cipher's output must look like to defeat it.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Deterministic encryption is doomed]
Using the indistinguishability game of Figure~\ref{ch01:fig-ind-game} and Example~\ref{ch01:ex-deterministic}, prove that \emph{any} deterministic encryption scheme fails the game whenever the adversary can obtain the encryption of at least one message of its choosing. State the adversary's exact strategy and compute its winning probability and advantage. Then explain in one sentence what randomization buys you.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Eve versus Mallory]
Give a concrete example of a system that is secure against Eve (a passive eavesdropper) and broken by Mallory (an active attacker). Describe what Mallory does, and explain in one sentence why confidentiality alone never implies integrity.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Refute the obscurity argument]
A startup advertises: ``Our encryption is unbreakable because our algorithm is a closely guarded trade secret that no attacker has ever seen.'' Write a short rebuttal grounded in Kerckhoffs's principle. Give at least two distinct concrete failure modes their argument ignores, and explain why a published algorithm can be \emph{more} trustworthy than a secret one.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Feel the numbers]
A key-search machine tries $10^{12}$ keys per second (a trillion per second).
(a) How many years does it take to exhaust a $64$-bit keyspace ($2^{64}$ keys)?
(b) How many years to exhaust an $80$-bit keyspace?
(c) How many years to exhaust a $128$-bit keyspace, and how does that compare to the age of the universe ($\approx 1.4 \times 10^{10}$ years)?
Use $2^{10} \approx 10^{3}$ and one year $\approx 3.15 \times 10^{7}$ seconds. What do your answers say about why standards moved from $64$-bit to $128$-bit keys?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Break the reused keystream by hand]
Two $6$-bit messages are encrypted with the \emph{same} $6$-bit keystream $k$ by XOR. You intercept
$c_1 = 101101$ and $c_2 = 110011$.
(a) Compute $c_1 \xor c_2$ and explain why the keystream $k$ has disappeared from the result.
(b) Suppose you also learn that $m_1 = 011010$. Recover both $k$ and $m_2$.
(c) State, in terms of the threat model, which assumption the encryptor violated.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Rank the attack models]
Order these four attacker capabilities from weakest to strongest, and for each give a one-line real-world scenario in which an attacker would have that capability: ciphertext-only, known-plaintext, chosen-plaintext, chosen-ciphertext. Then explain why proving security against the \emph{strongest} model is the conservative choice.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Why ``can't recover the plaintext'' is too weak]
Consider an encryption scheme that hides every \emph{character} of a message except that the ciphertext always leaks the message's exact length and whether the first character is a vowel. Argue that this scheme could satisfy ``the attacker cannot recover the full plaintext'' yet still fail in practice. Tie your answer to why cryptographers use \emph{indistinguishability} (the attacker cannot tell $m_0$ from $m_1$) as the goal instead.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Reductions, conceptually]
Explain in your own words why a theorem of the form ``if factoring is hard, then this scheme is secure'' gives stronger assurance than a designer's claim of having tried and failed to break it. Identify what the theorem \emph{assumes} and what it \emph{proves}, and state what we would learn if someone found an efficient attack on the scheme.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Hybrid reasoning]
HTTPS uses slow public-key cryptography to establish a shared key, then fast symmetric cryptography (AES) for the session data.
(a) Why not use public-key cryptography for the whole conversation?
(b) Why not use symmetric cryptography for the whole conversation, skipping public-key entirely?
(c) What problem does the public-key step solve that pure symmetric cryptography cannot, for two parties who have never met?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Entropy versus length]
A system generates ``$256$-bit keys,'' yet a flaw causes the generator to seed itself only from the current time in whole seconds, drawn from a known $30$-day window.
(a) Roughly how many distinct keys can the system produce? Express your answer as a power of two.
(b) How many bits of \emph{effective} security does the $256$-bit key provide?
(c) An attacker can test $10^{9}$ keys per second. About how long does it take to try them all? Relate your answer to why \S\ref{ch01:randomness} distinguishes key \emph{length} from key \emph{entropy}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Order matters]
Alice wants both confidentiality and integrity and considers the three combinations in \S\ref{ch01:composition}: encrypt-and-MAC, MAC-then-encrypt, and encrypt-then-MAC.
(a) For MAC-then-encrypt, explain why Bob is forced to run decryption on attacker-supplied data before he can detect tampering, and connect this to the padding-oracle attack.
(b) For encrypt-then-MAC, explain why a forged ciphertext never reaches the decryption routine.
(c) State the general principle this illustrates about composing secure building blocks, and name the modern primitive (a single interface) that removes the choice from implementers.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Apply the mindset]
A messaging app claims end-to-end encryption. List five questions you would ask, drawn from \S\ref{ch01:mindset}, before believing the messages are protected against a determined adversary. For each question, state what a \emph{bad} answer would reveal about a hidden weakness.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Side channels versus the model]
A server runs a provably CPA-secure cipher, decrypts incoming ciphertexts, and replies with either ``ok'' or ``bad padding,'' with the ``bad padding'' reply arriving measurably faster.
(a) Which attack model from \S\ref{ch01:threat-models} does this behavior hand the attacker, regardless of what the proof assumed?
(b) Explain why the security proof can be mathematically sound while the deployment is still broken.
(c) Name the defense that closes this gap.
\end{exercise}
