\chapter{Probability and Information for Cryptography}

The previous chapters built our algebraic foundations: groups, rings, and finite fields define the deterministic rules of modular arithmetic. Deterministic rules alone cannot keep a secret, because secrecy depends on making the right values unpredictable. A key an attacker cannot guess, a ciphertext that looks like noise, a nonce (Chapter~1) that never repeats: every one of these is a statement about probability.

This chapter asks three questions: how do we measure the unpredictability of a secret, how many random draws does it take before two collide, and what does it mean for two sources of randomness to look the same to an attacker? Every space we work with is finite, so probability here reduces to careful counting.

\chapterline

\section{Why probability is the native language of secrecy}

Picture the standard cast from Chapter~1. Alice wants to send Bob a message. Eve sits on the wire and sees everything that crosses it. Can Eve learn something she should not?

Eve almost never has zero information and almost never has complete information. Probability measures partial information. When we say ``a $128$-bit key is secure,'' we do not mean Eve \emph{cannot} guess it; she can, with probability $2^{-128}$ per guess. That probability is small enough that a planet-sized computer running for the age of the universe will not stumble onto the key. Security is quantitative, and the quantity is a probability.

\begin{intuitionbox}
A secret is only as strong as it is unpredictable, and unpredictability can be measured with a number. A coin you have already seen land heads has zero unpredictability. A freshly flipped fair coin carries one bit of unpredictability. A key-generation routine manufactures unpredictability and hands it to you as bits.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Sample spaces, events, and uniform distributions}

A finite probability space is a finite set $\Omega$ (the \emph{sample space}) of possible outcomes, together with a function $\Pr$ assigning each outcome $\omega \in \Omega$ a probability $\Pr[\omega] \ge 0$. An \emph{event} $A \subseteq \Omega$ is any subset of outcomes, and the probabilities satisfy
\[
  \sum_{\omega \in \Omega} \Pr[\omega] = 1, \qquad \Pr[A] = \sum_{\omega \in A} \Pr[\omega].
\]

The distribution we use most often is the \emph{uniform} distribution. Writing $\abs{\Omega} = N$ for the number of elements in $\Omega$, the uniform distribution assigns $\Pr[\omega] = 1/N$ to every outcome $\omega \in \Omega$. For any event $A \subseteq \Omega$ of size $\abs{A}$,
\[
  \Pr[A] = \frac{\abs{A}}{\abs{\Omega}} = \frac{\text{favorable outcomes}}{\text{total outcomes}}.
\]
This count-and-divide rule is the basis of key generation: a uniformly random $k$-bit key is a uniform draw from $\Omega = \set{0,1}^k$, the set of all $k$-bit strings of $0$s and $1$s, which has size $2^k$.

\begin{example}[A four-bit key]
\label{ch07:fourbit-key}
Let the key space be $\Omega = \set{0,1}^4$, so $N = 2^4 = 16$ keys from $0000$ to $1111$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ch07-sample-space-grid}). Under the uniform distribution each key has probability $1/16$. Eve guesses one key. The event ``Eve guesses right'' contains one outcome, so $\Pr[\text{correct}] = 1/16 = 0.0625$. The event ``the key starts with $1$'' contains the $8$ keys $1000, \dots, 1111$, so its probability is $8/16 = 1/2$. Scaling up to $128$ or $256$ bits changes only the exponents, not the arithmetic.
\end{example}

We write $k \getsr \set{0,1}^{\ell}$ to mean ``sample $k$ uniformly at random from the set of $\ell$-bit strings.'' Almost every security proof in this book begins by assuming a secret value was drawn this way, and many real-world breaks begin when a generator fails to do so.

\subsection{Conditional probability and Bayes}

Eve's knowledge changes as she observes ciphertexts or hardware leaks. The probability of event $A$ given that event $B$ occurred is the \emph{conditional probability}:
\[
  \Pr[A \mid B] = \frac{\Pr[A \cap B]}{\Pr[B]}, \qquad \text{defined when } \Pr[B] > 0,
\]
where $A \cap B$ is the event that both $A$ and $B$ occur. Read the ratio as restricting $\Omega$ to the outcomes where $B$ happened, then asking what fraction of that restricted set also belongs to $A$. Multiplying through by $\Pr[B]$ gives $\Pr[A \cap B] = \Pr[A \mid B]\,\Pr[B]$, and swapping the roles of $A$ and $B$ gives \emph{Bayes' rule}:
\[
  \Pr[A \mid B] = \frac{\Pr[B \mid A]\,\Pr[A]}{\Pr[B]}.
\]
Here $\Pr[A]$ is the \emph{prior} probability before observing $B$, $\Pr[B \mid A]$ is the \emph{likelihood} of observing $B$ when $A$ holds, and $\Pr[A \mid B]$ is the \emph{posterior} probability after updating on $B$. Bayes' rule describes how an attacker sharpens a key guess from leaked data, and it states what ``a ciphertext reveals nothing'' means formally: every message is equally likely before and after seeing the ciphertext ($\Pr[M = m \mid C = c] = \Pr[M = m]$, Chapter~9).

\begin{example}[A side channel updates a guess]
\label{ch07:bayes-sidechannel}
Return to the uniform four-bit key of Example~\ref{ch07:fourbit-key}. A noisy power-analysis probe (a sensor measuring tiny fluctuations in the chip's power draw during encryption) leaks one bit about the key's \emph{Hamming weight}, the number of $1$s in the bit string. The probe reports ``the weight is even'' and is right with probability $0.8$, wrong with probability $0.2$. As Figure~\ref{fig:ch07-sample-space-grid} shows, $8$ of the $16$ keys have even weight ($0000, 0011, \ldots, 1111$) and $8$ have odd weight, so before the leak each class has prior probability $1/2$.

Let $A$ be the event ``the true key has even weight,'' let $\bar A = \Omega \setminus A$ be the complement event ``$A$ does not occur'' (the key has odd weight), and let $B$ be the event ``the probe reports even.'' The likelihoods are $\Pr[B \mid A] = 0.8$ and $\Pr[B \mid \bar A] = 0.2$. Because every key falls into either $A$ or $\bar A$, the total probability of seeing report $B$ is $\Pr[B] = \Pr[B \mid A]\,\Pr[A] + \Pr[B \mid \bar A]\,\Pr[\bar A]$. Bayes' rule gives
\begin{align*}
  \Pr[B] &= 0.8 \cdot \frac{1}{2} + 0.2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 0.5, \\
  \Pr[A \mid B] &= \frac{\Pr[B \mid A]\,\Pr[A]}{\Pr[B]} = \frac{0.8 \cdot 0.5}{0.5} = 0.8.
\end{align*}
The leak moves Eve from $0.5$ to $0.8$ confidence that the weight is even. Conditioned on $B$, the $8$ even-weight keys share that $0.8$ probability equally, so guessing any one of them succeeds with probability $0.8 \cdot \frac{1}{8} = 0.10$, up from $1/16 = 0.0625$ before the leak (Figure~\ref{ch07:fig-bayes-update}). Side-channel attacks accumulate dozens of such Bayesian updates until a single key stands out.
\end{example}

\subsection{Independence}

Two events $A$ and $B$ are \emph{independent} if
\[
  \Pr[A \cap B] = \Pr[A]\cdot \Pr[B].
\]
When $\Pr[B] > 0$, this is equivalent to $\Pr[A \mid B] = \Pr[A]$: learning $B$ tells you nothing about $A$. More generally, events $A_1, \dots, A_n$ are mutually independent if the probability of any subcollection occurring together equals the product of their individual probabilities. Independence is what we demand of the bits coming out of a random source: each new bit should behave like a fresh fair coin, unaffected by all previous bits.

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{When ``random'' bits are not independent.} In 2012 two research teams scanned millions of RSA public keys from the public Internet and computed pairwise greatest common divisors (GCDs) of their moduli. Roughly $0.2\%$ of TLS and SSH keys shared a prime factor with another key, letting anyone factor both moduli in microseconds and recover the private keys. Embedded devices such as routers and firewalls had generated keys moments after boot, before the operating system's \emph{entropy pool} (its internal buffer of unpredictable timing noise) had collected enough variation. Separate devices drew from nearly identical starting states, so their ``random'' primes were \emph{correlated} -- statistically linked, so knowing something about one device's output narrowed down another's -- rather than independent. We revisit entropy starvation in Chapter~17.
\end{attackbox}

If $k_1, k_2, \dots, k_n$ are independent uniform bits ($\Pr[k_i = 0] = \Pr[k_i = 1] = 1/2$), then any specific $n$-bit string occurs with probability $(1/2)^n = 2^{-n}$, so $k_1 k_2 \cdots k_n$ is uniform over $\set{0,1}^n$. Independence turns $n$ fair coins into one uniform $n$-bit key. Lose independence and the effective key space shrinks, even if each individual bit still lands $0$ and $1$ equally often.

\section{Random variables and expectation}

A \emph{random variable} $X$ is a function from the sample space to the real numbers $\R$, written $X : \Omega \to \R$. When you roll a six-sided die, ``the number showing'' is a random variable; ``$1$ if the roll is even, else $0$'' is another. The \emph{expectation} (or mean) of $X$ is the probability-weighted average of its values:
\[
  \mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{\omega \in \Omega} \Pr[\omega]\, X(\omega) = \sum_{x} x \cdot \Pr[X = x],
\]
where the second sum runs over every number $x$ that $X$ takes on $\Omega$. Expectation is \emph{linear}: for any random variables $X, Y$ on $\Omega$ and any constant $c \in \R$,
\[
  \mathbb{E}[X + Y] = \mathbb{E}[X] + \mathbb{E}[Y], \qquad \mathbb{E}[cX] = c\,\mathbb{E}[X],
\]
with no independence required. Because linearity holds even when $X$ and $Y$ are correlated, we can find the expected sum of overlapping events by adding their individual expectations.

\begin{example}[Expected number of guesses]
\label{ch07:expected-guesses}
Eve attacks the $4$-bit key of Example~\ref{ch07:fourbit-key} by trying keys one at a time in a random order, without repeating a guess, until she hits the true key. Let $X$ be the number of guesses she makes. Each position $i \in \set{1,2,\dots,16}$ is equally likely to hold the true key, so $\Pr[X = i] = 1/16$. Using $\sum_{i=1}^{N} i = N(N+1)/2$,
\begin{align*}
  \mathbb{E}[X] &= \sum_{i=1}^{16} i \cdot \frac{1}{16} \\
                &= \frac{1}{16} \cdot \frac{16 \cdot 17}{2} = \frac{17}{2} = 8.5.
\end{align*}
For an $\ell$-bit key ($N = 2^\ell$), the same sum gives $(2^\ell + 1)/2 \approx 2^{\ell-1}$ guesses on average -- half the key space. For a $128$-bit key, brute force takes about $2^{127}$ expected guesses.
\end{example}

\subsection{The indicator trick}

For any event $A \subseteq \Omega$, the \emph{indicator random variable} $\mathbf{1}_A$ records whether $A$ occurred:
\[
  \mathbf{1}_A(\omega) =
  \begin{cases}
    1 & \text{if } \omega \in A, \\
    0 & \text{if } \omega \notin A.
  \end{cases}
\]
Its expectation equals the probability of the event:
\[
  \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_A] = 1 \cdot \Pr[A] + 0 \cdot \Pr[\bar A] = \Pr[A].
\]
If $A_1, \dots, A_m$ are events and $X = \mathbf{1}_{A_1} + \cdots + \mathbf{1}_{A_m}$ counts how many of them occur, linearity of expectation gives $\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{j=1}^{m} \Pr[A_j]$, even when the events overlap.

\section{Entropy: measuring unpredictability on average}

How many bits of unpredictability does a secret carry? Claude Shannon answered that question in 1948 with a quantity that measures average uncertainty.

\begin{historybox}
Claude Shannon at Bell Labs founded information theory in 1948 with ``A Mathematical Theory of Communication'' and mathematical cryptography in 1949 with ``Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems.'' Using entropy to measure average uncertainty, he proved that the one-time pad achieves perfect secrecy against an eavesdropper with unlimited computing power, along with a converse: any cipher that stays unbreakable against unlimited computation needs a key at least as long as the message. He borrowed the word ``entropy'' from thermodynamics on the advice of John von Neumann, who joked that it would give him an edge in debates because nobody knows what entropy is. Shannon left open whether short, reusable keys could still defeat an adversary restricted to polynomial-time computation.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{The definition and where it comes from}

Let $X$ be a random variable taking value $x$ with probability $p_x = \Pr[X = x]$, and write $\log_2 u$ for the base-$2$ logarithm (the exponent to which $2$ is raised to get $u$, so $\log_2 8 = 3$). The \emph{Shannon entropy} of $X$, measured in bits, is
\[
  H(X) = -\sum_{x} p_x \log_2 p_x = \sum_{x} p_x \log_2 \frac{1}{p_x},
\]
with the convention $0 \log_2 0 = 0$ so impossible outcomes contribute zero. The quantity $\log_2(1/p_x)$ is the \emph{surprise} (or \emph{information content}) of outcome $x$: rare outcomes ($p_x$ small) carry large surprise, while a certain outcome ($p_x = 1$) carries $\log_2 1 = 0$ surprise. Entropy is the expected surprise $\mathbb{E}[\log_2(1/p_X)]$ upon learning $X$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Think of $H(X)$ as the average number of yes/no questions an optimal questioner needs to identify $X$. If $X$ is uniform over $8$ values, splitting the remaining candidates in half each time nails $X$ in $3$ questions, matching $H(X) = \log_2 8 = 3$ bits. If one value is almost certain, asking ``is it that value?'' first usually finishes in one question, so $H(X)$ is much smaller.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Worked entropies on tiny distributions}

\begin{example}[Fair versus biased coin]
\label{ch07:coin-entropy}
A fair coin has $p_{\mathrm{H}} = p_{\mathrm{T}} = 1/2$:
\[
  H(X) = -\frac{1}{2} \log_2 \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2} \log_2 \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2}(1) + \frac{1}{2}(1) = 1\text{ bit}.
\]
Now bias the coin so heads lands with probability $p = 0.9$ and tails with $1 - p = 0.1$:
\begin{align*}
  H(X) &= -0.9\log_2 0.9 - 0.1\log_2 0.1 \\
       &\approx 0.9(0.152) + 0.1(3.322) \approx 0.137 + 0.332 = 0.469\text{ bits}.
\end{align*}
Figure~\ref{fig:ch07-binary-entropy} plots $H$ across all coin biases $p \in [0,1]$. The biased flip still produces one bit of output data, yet it carries less than half a bit of unpredictability: an attacker who guesses heads on every flip is right $90\%$ of the time. Weak token generators often fail because of this gap between bit length and entropy.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A loaded four-sided die]
\label{ch07:loaded-die}
Let $X$ take four outcomes $x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4$ with probabilities $\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{8}, \frac{1}{8}$. Then
\begin{align*}
  H(X) &= \frac{1}{2}\log_2 2 + \frac{1}{4}\log_2 4 + \frac{1}{8}\log_2 8 + \frac{1}{8}\log_2 8 \\
       &= \frac{1}{2}(1) + \frac{1}{4}(2) + \frac{1}{8}(3) + \frac{1}{8}(3) = \frac{7}{4} = 1.75\text{ bits}.
\end{align*}
A uniform four-sided die has entropy $\log_2 4 = 2$ bits, so the loading costs a quarter-bit. Figure~\ref{fig:ch07-question-trees} shows the corresponding yes/no question trees: asking first whether $X = x_1$ resolves the outcome in $1$ question half the time, lowering the average depth from $2$ questions to $1.75$.
\end{example}

\subsection{The properties that matter for crypto}

Three properties of Shannon entropy come up throughout the book.

\begin{theorem}[Entropy is maximized by the uniform distribution]
\label{ch07:max-entropy}
For any random variable $X$ over a set of size $N$, $0 \le H(X) \le \log_2 N$. Equality on the left holds if and only if $X$ takes a single value with probability $1$, and equality on the right holds if and only if $X$ is uniform over all $N$ values.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
Why the uniform distribution maximizes entropy. Suppose two outcomes have unequal probabilities $p > q$. Shifting a small amount of probability from the likelier outcome to the rarer one moves them closer together, and a derivative check on $-p\log_2 p - q\log_2 q$ (matching the arch of Figure~\ref{fig:ch07-binary-entropy}) shows this strictly increases the sum. The only distribution with no peak left to flatten gives every outcome probability $1/N$, where $H(X) = \sum_{x} \frac{1}{N}\log_2 N = \log_2 N$. Any departure from uniformity lowers entropy and hands the attacker an advantage.
\end{whybox}

Second, entropy across independent sources \emph{adds}. Writing $H(X, Y)$ for the \emph{joint entropy} of the pair $(X, Y)$ treated as a single outcome in the product space, independence of $X$ and $Y$ gives $H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y)$. Sixteen independent fair coins carry $16$ bits of entropy, so $\ell$ independent uniform bits carry $\ell$ bits of entropy.

Third, the \emph{data-processing inequality} for entropy states that no deterministic function $f$ can create entropy: $H(f(X)) \le H(X)$. Applying a deterministic scramble (such as a hash function) to a low-entropy password leaves the result low-entropy, which is what password crackers exploit.

To track how much a ciphertext $C$ reveals about a message $M$, define the \emph{conditional entropy} $H(M \mid C)$ as the average entropy remaining in $M$ once $C$ is observed:
\[
  H(M \mid C) = \sum_{c} \Pr[C = c]\, H(M \mid C = c),
\]
where $H(M \mid C = c)$ is the entropy of the posterior distribution $\Pr[M = m \mid C = c]$. Observing $C$ can never increase average uncertainty, so $H(M \mid C) \le H(M)$. The drop in uncertainty,
\[
  I(M; C) = H(M) - H(M \mid C) \ge 0,
\]
is the \emph{mutual information} between $M$ and $C$: the average number of bits about $M$ leaked by $C$. Figure~\ref{ch07:fig-entropy-venn} contrasts a leaky cipher ($I(M; C) > 0$) with Shannon's condition for perfect secrecy ($I(M; C) = 0$, meaning $H(M \mid C) = H(M)$ because $M$ and $C$ are independent).

\section{Min-entropy: the paranoid measure}

Shannon entropy is an \emph{average}, and an average can mislead against an attacker who makes the single best guess. Suppose a $128$-bit key equals the all-zero string with probability $1/2$ and otherwise is uniform over the remaining $2^{128} - 1$ strings (each with probability $\frac{1}{2(2^{128}-1)}$). Its Shannon entropy is $65$ bits:
\[
  H(X) = \frac{1}{2}\log_2 2 + \frac{1}{2}\log_2\!\bigl(2(2^{128}-1)\bigr) \approx 0.5 + 64.5 = 65\text{ bits}.
\]
Yet an attacker who guesses the all-zero key wins half the time on the first try. By Shannon entropy the key looks strong; in practice it is broken. We need a measure that reports the worst case rather than the average.

\begin{definition}[Min-entropy]
\label{ch07:min-entropy-def}
The \emph{min-entropy} of $X$, where $p_x = \Pr[X = x]$, is
\[
  H_\infty(X) = -\log_2 \Bigl( \max_x \Pr[X = x] \Bigr) = \min_x \log_2 \frac{1}{p_x}.
\]
\end{definition}

Find the single most likely outcome, call its probability $p_{\max} = \max_x p_x$, and report $-\log_2 p_{\max}$. Because the best single guess aims at that outcome and succeeds with probability $p_{\max} = 2^{-H_\infty(X)}$, min-entropy directly bounds one-shot guessing:
\[
  \Pr[\text{best single guess is correct}] = 2^{-H_\infty(X)}.
\]
Like Shannon entropy, min-entropy adds across independent draws: if $X$ and $Y$ are independent, the largest joint probability is $\bigl(\max_x \Pr[X=x]\bigr)\bigl(\max_y \Pr[Y=y]\bigr)$, so $H_\infty(X, Y) = H_\infty(X) + H_\infty(Y)$. That direct connection to guessing probability is why \emph{randomness extractors} (procedures that distill nearly uniform bits from a biased physical source) and \emph{key-derivation functions} (which turn passwords or shared secrets into keys) specify their inputs in terms of min-entropy rather than Shannon entropy.

\begin{example}[The two measures disagree]
\label{ch07:hinf-vs-h}
Take the loaded die of Example~\ref{ch07:loaded-die} with probabilities $\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{8}, \frac{1}{8}$. Its Shannon entropy was $1.75$ bits. Its min-entropy depends only on the peak $p_{\max} = 1/2$:
\[
  H_\infty(X) = -\log_2 \frac{1}{2} = 1\text{ bit}.
\]
That is three-quarters of a bit lower. Because the top outcome lands half the time, a one-guess attacker wins with probability $1/2 = 2^{-1}$. On a uniform distribution every $p_x = 1/N$ and both measures equal $\log_2 N$. They diverge whenever the distribution has a peak, and the peak is what an attacker guesses first (Figure~\ref{ch07:loaded-die-bars}).
\end{example}

\begin{formalbox}
For every random variable $X$ on $N$ outcomes, $0 \le H_\infty(X) \le H(X) \le \log_2 N$. Min-entropy is the most conservative member of the R\'enyi entropy family $H_\alpha(X) = \frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log_2\!\bigl(\sum_x p_x^\alpha\bigr)$ ($\alpha > 0, \alpha \ne 1$), obtained in the limit $\alpha \to \infty$, which explains the $\infty$ subscript. When a cryptographic standard states that a seed (the secret starting input to a generator) needs ``$256$ bits of entropy,'' read it as min-entropy unless told otherwise.
\end{formalbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Special Publication 800-90B, the U.S.\ standard for entropy sources feeding random-number generators, is built around min-entropy. A hardware noise source is tested statistically to estimate its min-entropy per sample, and a \emph{conditioning} step (built from a hash function or a block cipher, a fixed-width keyed permutation) compresses many noisy samples into fewer bits close to full min-entropy. Using min-entropy catches a generator that is usually unpredictable yet occasionally emits one favored value.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{trapbox}
Having many bytes does not mean having much entropy. A megabyte of the digits of $\pi$ has eight million bits of length and zero entropy, because every bit is fixed in advance. Length is what you store; entropy is what the attacker has to guess. For any ``random'' key, ask what distribution produced it and what the largest probability of any single value is.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Guessing entropy: how many tries on average}

Min-entropy measures how often the \emph{first} guess wins. A complementary question asks: if an attacker tries candidates in the best order from most likely to least likely, how many guesses does it take on average to find $X$? That expected count is the \emph{guessing entropy} (or \emph{guesswork}).

Suppose $X$ takes $M$ possible outcomes, sorted from largest to smallest probability: $p_1 \ge p_2 \ge \cdots \ge p_M$. An optimal attacker tests them in that order, finding the $i$-th outcome on guess $i$, so the expected number of guesses is
\[
  G(X) = \sum_{i=1}^{M} i \cdot p_i.
\]

\begin{example}[Three ways to score the loaded die]
\label{ch07:guesswork}
For the die with sorted probabilities $\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{8}, \frac{1}{8}$, the guessing entropy is
\begin{align*}
  G(X) &= 1\cdot\frac{1}{2} + 2\cdot\frac{1}{4} + 3\cdot\frac{1}{8} + 4\cdot\frac{1}{8} \\
       &= \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} + \frac{3}{8} + \frac{1}{2} = \frac{15}{8} = 1.875\text{ guesses}.
\end{align*}
A uniform draw over $4$ values requires $G = (1+2+3+4)/4 = 2.5$ guesses on average. Three numbers now describe the same distribution: $H(X) = 1.75$ bits (average surprise), $H_\infty(X) = 1$ bit (worst-case single guess), and $G(X) = 1.875$ guesses (expected optimal search). Each answers a distinct operational question, so a security claim must name which one it means.
\end{example}

\begin{whybox}
Why guesswork is not $2^{H(X)}$. For a uniform key on $2^\ell$ values, $H(X) = \ell$ and $G(X) = (2^\ell + 1)/2 \approx 2^{\ell-1}$, so $2^{H(X)}$ tracks $G(X)$ up to a factor of two. On peaked distributions that rule of thumb breaks. James Massey (1994) proved the lower bound $G(X) \ge \frac{1}{4}\cdot 2^{H(X)} + 1$ whenever $H(X) \ge 2$, preventing $G(X)$ from falling far below $2^{H(X)}$, yet no matching upper bound exists. Put probability $0.9$ on one value and spread the remaining $0.1$ uniformly across $2^{100}$ values: Shannon entropy is only $H(X) \approx 10.5$ bits ($2^{H(X)} \approx 1450$), while the $10\%$ tail drags the mean search cost up to $G(X) \approx 0.1 \cdot 2^{99} \approx 2^{95.7} \gg 2^{H(X)}$ -- even though the attacker's first guess wins $90\%$ of the time. That example warns against trusting $G(X)$ blindly too: $G(X)$ averages all the way to $100\%$ recovery, so a tiny tail can inflate $G(X)$ while an offline password cracker (testing candidates locally with no server rate limit) breaks most accounts on the first few tries. To bound the chance an attacker wins within $q$ tries, min-entropy gives the clean bound $q \cdot 2^{-H_\infty(X)}$.
\end{whybox}

\section{The birthday paradox and collision security}

In a room of $23$ people, the chance that at least two share a birthday exceeds $1/2$. Most people guess closer to $183$ (half of $365$) because they picture matching \emph{one fixed} birthday rather than matching \emph{any pair} of people. This counting fact sets the output size of every cryptographic hash function and the lifetime of every randomly chosen nonce.

\subsection{Why 23 is enough}

Compute the probability of \emph{no} shared birthday and subtract from $1$. Place $n$ people one at a time onto $N = 365$ equally likely days, assuming independent uniform birthdays. The first person takes any day. The second avoids the first with probability $\frac{364}{365}$. The third avoids two taken days with probability $\frac{363}{365}$, and the $(k+1)$-st avoids $k$ taken days with probability $1 - \frac{k}{N}$. By independence of the draws, the probability that all $n$ birthdays are distinct is
\[
  \Pr[\text{all distinct}] = \prod_{k=1}^{n-1} \Bigl(1 - \frac{k}{N}\Bigr),
\]
so a collision (two or more draws landing on the same value) occurs with probability
\[
  \Pr[\text{collision}] = 1 - \prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\Bigl(1 - \frac{k}{N}\Bigr).
\]
Evaluating at $N = 365$ gives:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rl}
\toprule
people $n$ & $\Pr[\text{some shared birthday}]$ \\
\midrule
$10$ & $0.117$ \\
$20$ & $0.411$ \\
$23$ & $0.507$ \\
$30$ & $0.706$ \\
$50$ & $0.970$ \\
$70$ & $0.999$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

At $n = 23$ the probability crosses $1/2$. What grows fast is the number of unordered pairs, written $\binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$: among $23$ people there are $\binom{23}{2} = \frac{23 \cdot 22}{2} = 253$ pairs, each an opportunity for a match, and pairs grow quadratically with $n$.

\subsection{The clean bound}

To pull out the $n^2$ scaling algebraically, use $1 - x \le e^{-x}$, which holds for every real $x$ because $e^{-x}$ (where $e \approx 2.718$ is the base of the natural logarithm $\ln$, and $\exp(z) = e^z$) lies above its tangent line $1 - x$ at $x = 0$:
\begin{align*}
  \Pr[\text{all distinct}]
    &= \prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\Bigl(1 - \frac{k}{N}\Bigr) \\
    &\le \prod_{k=1}^{n-1} e^{-k/N} && \text{(since $1 - x \le e^{-x}$)} \\
    &= \exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} k\Bigr) && \text{(convert product to sum of exponents)} \\
    &= \exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{n(n-1)}{2N}\Bigr). && \Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} k = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}\Bigr)
\end{align*}
Subtracting from $1$ gives a lower bound on the collision probability:
\[
  \Pr[\text{collision}] \;\ge\; 1 - \exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{n(n-1)}{2N}\Bigr) \;\approx\; 1 - \exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{n^2}{2N}\Bigr).
\]
Setting $1 - \exp\bigl(-n^2/(2N)\bigr) = 1/2$ gives $\exp\bigl(-n^2/(2N)\bigr) = 1/2$. Taking natural logarithms ($\ln 2 \approx 0.693$) yields $n^2/(2N) = \ln 2$, or
\[
  \boxed{\,n \approx \sqrt{2\ln 2 \cdot N} \approx 1.177\sqrt{N}.\,}
\]
When $n = \sqrt{2N} \approx 1.414\sqrt{N}$, the collision probability reaches about $1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.632$. For $N = 365$, $1.177\sqrt{365} \approx 22.5$, matching the discrete crossover at $23$ in Figure~\ref{ch07:birthday-curve}. Collisions become likely after on the order of $\sqrt{N}$ draws rather than $N$.

\begin{whybox}
Why the square root governs symmetric output sizes. If outputs are drawn uniformly from an $m$-bit space of size $N = 2^m$, collisions become likely after roughly $\sqrt{2^m} = 2^{m/2}$ samples. An $m$-bit space provides only $m/2$ bits of collision resistance. That ceiling comes from counting pairs rather than any flaw in a particular algorithm, so hash digests are chosen to be \emph{twice} as wide as the target security level.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{Counting collisions with the indicator trick}

Indicator random variables reach the same $\sqrt{N}$ threshold directly. Throw $n$ independent uniform samples into $N$ bins. For each unordered pair $\set{i, j}$ of samples ($1 \le i < j \le n$), let $\mathbf{1}_{ij}$ be the indicator that samples $i$ and $j$ land in the same bin. Regardless of where sample $i$ lands, sample $j$ hits that bin with probability $1/N$, so $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{ij}] = 1/N$. Summing over all $\binom{n}{2}$ pairs by linearity of expectation -- even though the pair events $\mathbf{1}_{12}, \mathbf{1}_{23}, \mathbf{1}_{13}$ are not mutually independent -- gives
\[
  \mathbb{E}[\text{colliding pairs}] = \sum_{1 \le i < j \le n} \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{ij}] = \binom{n}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{N} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2N}.
\]
The expected number of colliding pairs crosses $1$ when $n(n-1) \approx 2N$, or $n \approx \sqrt{2N}$.

\subsection{What this dictates for hash functions}

A cryptographic hash function $\Hash : \set{0,1}^* \to \set{0,1}^n$ maps a bit string of any finite length (written $\set{0,1}^*$) to a fixed-length $n$-bit output called a \emph{digest}. It must be \emph{collision resistant}: no feasible attacker should find two distinct inputs $x \ne y$ with $\Hash(x) = \Hash(y)$. By the birthday bound with $N = 2^n$, a generic attacker who hashes $q$ random inputs finds a collision in roughly $q \approx 2^{n/2}$ evaluations:

\begin{itemize}
  \item A $128$-bit digest offers only $\approx 2^{64}$ generic collision resistance, a count reachable by large clusters. Treat $128$-bit hashes as obsolete for collision resistance.
  \item SHA-$256$ (from the Secure Hash Algorithm family) outputs $256$ bits, giving $\approx 2^{128}$ collision resistance, the standard modern target.
  \item For $256$-bit collision security, use a $512$-bit digest such as SHA-$512$.
\end{itemize}

Compare two distinct hash security goals (Figure~\ref{fig:ch07-preimage-vs-collision}). \emph{Preimage resistance} -- given a fixed target digest $y^*$, find any $x$ with $\Hash(x) = y^*$ -- takes $\approx 2^n$ hashes generically, because $q$ trial hashes give only $q$ chances to hit that single target $y^*$. \emph{Collision resistance} takes only $\approx 2^{n/2}$ hashes, because any match among all $\binom{q}{2} \approx q^2/2$ pairs of trial digests wins.

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Birthday attacks in practice.} Message Digest 5 (MD5) produces a $128$-bit digest ($2^{64}$ generic collision bound), and structural flaws lowered real collision attacks far further. In 2008 researchers used MD5 collisions to forge a rogue certificate-authority certificate that browsers trusted for any website. In 2012 the Flame malware used an MD5 collision to forge a Microsoft code-signing certificate (a digital credential Windows uses to verify software updates). SHA-1, with a $160$-bit digest ($2^{80}$ generic bound), fell in 2017 to the \emph{SHAttered} attack, which produced two distinct PDF files sharing one SHA-1 hash; in 2020 a \emph{chosen-prefix} SHA-1 collision (where the attacker picks two different file prefixes and computes suffixes that make the full files collide) forged identities in Pretty Good Privacy (PGP), an email and file-signing standard. In each case, a digest already halved in strength by the birthday bound was pushed below practical reach by cryptanalysis.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
The companion probability book proves the $1 - e^{-x}$ bound and analyzes the variance (average squared spread around the mean) and tail bounds (probabilities of straying far from the mean) of the collision count. The algorithms book pairs it with Pollard's \emph{rho} cycle-finding method, which walks a chain of hashes until it loops to find a collision in $O(2^{n/2})$ steps using almost no memory.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{implementbox}
A small birthday-collision experiment. We hash random inputs with SHA-256 truncated to $n = 24$ bits and count how many tries it takes to find a collision, comparing against $2^{n/2} = 2^{12} = 4096$. Truncating SHA-256 to $24$ bits is purely for a fast classroom test -- never truncate digests this way in production.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
import hashlib, os

def short_hash(data, nbits=24):
    full = hashlib.sha256(data).digest()
    val = int.from_bytes(full, "big")
    return val & ((1 << nbits) - 1)      # keep only the low nbits

def find_collision(nbits=24):
    seen = {}                            # digest -> input that produced it
    tries = 0
    while True:
        x = os.urandom(16)
        h = short_hash(x, nbits)
        tries += 1
        if h in seen and seen[h] != x:
            return tries, seen[h], x      # collision found
        seen[h] = x

n = 24
tries, a, b = find_collision(n)
print(f"collided after {tries} hashes; birthday estimate ~ {2**(n//2)}")
# Typical run: a few thousand hashes, right around 2**12 = 4096.
\end{lstlisting}
Average a dozen runs and the collision count clusters near $1.25 \times 2^{12} \approx 5130$, nowhere near $2^{24} \approx 1.68 \times 10^7$.
\end{implementbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
Birthday bounds set concrete limits in deployed protocols. AES in Galois/Counter Mode (GCM) uses a $96$-bit nonce; choosing nonces uniformly at random risks a repeat after roughly $2^{48}$ messages under one key, and a single repeated GCM nonce exposes the internal authentication key used to detect tampering. TLS 1.3 therefore derives GCM nonces from a packet counter rather than random draws. Older $64$-bit block ciphers -- the Data Encryption Standard (DES), Triple DES (3DES), and Blowfish, which encrypt $64$-bit ($8$-byte) blocks -- suffer the \emph{Sweet32} attack: in cipher block chaining (CBC) mode, ciphertext blocks collide after roughly $2^{32}$ blocks ($\approx 32\,\text{GB}$), leaking XOR relationships between plaintext blocks over long HTTPS or virtual private network (VPN) sessions. That $2^{32}$ ceiling is why $64$-bit block ciphers were retired from TLS.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Statistical distance and indistinguishability}

How close are two probability distributions on the same set? Modern security definitions rest on that question: a scheme is secure when the distribution an attacker observes in the real protocol cannot be told apart from an ideal, secret-free distribution.

\subsection{Definition and the guessing-game meaning}

\begin{definition}[Statistical distance]
\label{ch07:stat-distance}
Let $P$ and $Q$ be distributions over the same finite set $\Omega$, writing $P(\omega)$ for the probability $P$ assigns to outcome $\omega$ and $P(A) = \sum_{\omega \in A} P(\omega)$ for event $A \subseteq \Omega$. Their \emph{statistical distance} (also called \emph{total variation distance}) is
\[
  \Delta(P, Q) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{\omega \in \Omega} \abs{P(\omega) - Q(\omega)} = \max_{A \subseteq \Omega} \bigl( P(A) - Q(A) \bigr).
\]
\end{definition}

Why do the two expressions agree? Let $A^* = \set{\omega \in \Omega : P(\omega) > Q(\omega)}$ be the event collecting every outcome where $P$ assigns more probability than $Q$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ch07-stat-dist-bars}). Because both distributions sum to $1$, the excess probability $P(A^*) - Q(A^*)$ on $A^*$ equals the deficit $Q(\bar A^*) - P(\bar A^*)$ on the remaining outcomes $\bar A^*$. Summing $\abs{P(\omega) - Q(\omega)}$ across all $\omega \in \Omega$ counts that shifted probability mass twice -- once on $A^*$ and once on $\bar A^*$ -- so dividing by $2$ recovers $P(A^*) - Q(A^*) = \max_{A \subseteq \Omega}\bigl(P(A) - Q(A)\bigr)$.

The maximum form gives $\Delta(P, Q)$ its operational meaning: it is the largest advantage any test $A$ can achieve at telling $P$ from $Q$. When $\Delta(P, Q) = 0$, the two distributions are identical and no test beats a coin flip. When $\Delta(P, Q) = 1$, their \emph{supports} -- the sets of outcomes where $P(\omega) > 0$ and $Q(\omega) > 0$ -- do not overlap, so a single sample identifies the source with certainty.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Picture a distinguishing game (Figure~\ref{ch07:referee-game}). A referee flips a fair coin $b \getsr \set{0,1}$ out of sight: if $b = 0$, she draws $x \getsr P$; if $b = 1$, she draws $x \getsr Q$. You inspect $x$ and output a guess $\hat b$. Guessing $0$ when $x \in A^*$ and $1$ when $x \notin A^*$ (or vice versa) achieves the highest win probability any strategy can reach: $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\Delta(P, Q)$. Statistical distance is twice your best margin above $1/2$: if $\Delta(P, Q) = 0.02$, no distinguisher wins more than $51\%$ of the time.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{A worked distance}

\begin{example}[Two coins]
\label{ch07:two-coins}
Let $P$ be a fair coin, $P(\mathrm{H}) = P(\mathrm{T}) = \frac{1}{2}$, and $Q$ a biased coin, $Q(\mathrm{H}) = \frac{3}{4}$ and $Q(\mathrm{T}) = \frac{1}{4}$. Then
\begin{align*}
  \Delta(P, Q) &= \frac{1}{2}\Bigl( \Bigl\lvert \frac{1}{2} - \frac{3}{4} \Bigr\rvert + \Bigl\lvert \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4} \Bigr\rvert \Bigr) \\
               &= \frac{1}{2}\Bigl( \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4} \Bigr) = \frac{1}{4}.
\end{align*}
Compare with the maximum form: the optimal distinguisher guesses ``$Q$'' whenever it sees heads ($A = \set{\mathrm{H}}$). It outputs ``$Q$'' with probability $Q(\mathrm{H}) = \frac{3}{4}$ when $Q$ was used and with probability $P(\mathrm{H}) = \frac{1}{2}$ when $P$ was used, for an advantage of $\frac{3}{4} - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4} = \Delta(P, Q)$. In the game of Figure~\ref{ch07:referee-game}, its win probability is $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{4} = \frac{5}{8} = 0.625$, beating a blind guess by $12.5$ percentage points. Cryptographic constructions aim for distances like $2^{-128}$ rather than $1/4$.
\end{example}

\begin{trapbox}
Small distance per sample does not mean small distance forever. If one draw from $P$ sits at statistical distance $\delta$ from one draw from $Q$, then $m$ independent draws sit at distance \emph{at most} $m\delta$, and often close to $m\delta$. Suppose a \emph{pseudorandom generator} (PRG) -- a deterministic algorithm that stretches a short random seed into a longer string meant to look uniform -- had a one-block output at distance $2^{-40}$ from uniform. Across $m = 2^{30}$ (about a billion) independent uses, the total distance can grow toward $2^{30}\cdot 2^{-40} = 2^{-10} \approx 0.001$, a thousand times larger. Security proofs track the attacker's query count $m$ for this reason, and protocols enforce rekeying limits before $m\delta$ grows noticeable.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{From statistical to computational indistinguishability}

Statistical distance is \emph{information-theoretic}: it bounds every distinguisher, regardless of running time. Only a few cryptographic tools -- such as the one-time pad (Chapter~9) and \emph{secret sharing} (splitting a secret across shares so fewer than a threshold reveal zero information, Chapter~32) -- clear that bar. Practical ciphers with short keys use \emph{computational indistinguishability}: no efficient (polynomial-time) algorithm can tell the two distributions apart with more than a vanishing margin, even though an unbounded search could.

\begin{formalbox}
Two \emph{distribution ensembles} $\set{P_\lambda}$ and $\set{Q_\lambda}$ -- families of distributions indexed by a security parameter $\lambda$ (such as the key length in bits) -- are \emph{computationally indistinguishable}, written $P_\lambda \approx_c Q_\lambda$, if for every probabilistic polynomial-time ($\PPT$) distinguisher $D$ (a randomized algorithm whose running time is bounded by $\lambda^c$ for some constant $c$),
\[
  \bigl\lvert \Pr[D(P_\lambda) = 1] - \Pr[D(Q_\lambda) = 1] \bigr\rvert \le \negl(\lambda),
\]
where $\negl(\lambda)$ is \emph{negligible}: for every constant $c > 0$, $\negl(\lambda) < 1/\lambda^c$ once $\lambda$ is large enough. Statistical indistinguishability ($\Delta(P_\lambda, Q_\lambda) \le \negl(\lambda)$) is the stronger case where the bound holds against \emph{all} distinguishers, not only $\PPT$ ones. A PRG stretching a $\lambda$-bit seed to $2\lambda$ bits can cover at most $2^\lambda$ of the $2^{2\lambda}$ strings, so its statistical distance from uniform is at least $1 - 2^{-\lambda} \approx 1$; its security promise is instead computational indistinguishability, developed in Chapter~8.
\end{formalbox}

\begin{whybox}
Why indistinguishability is the working definition of secrecy. Making ciphertexts statistically independent of plaintexts forces keys as long as messages (Shannon's theorem, Chapter~9). Requiring instead that encryptions of $m_0$ be computationally indistinguishable from encryptions of $m_1$ lets short keys encrypt gigabytes: if no efficient attacker can tell which message was encrypted, the ciphertext leaks nothing usable. Indistinguishability under chosen-plaintext attack (IND-CPA, Chapter~1) and under chosen-ciphertext attack (IND-CCA, Chapter~16) are both instances of this distinguishing game against $\PPT$ adversaries.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{The data-processing inequality for distance}

Applying any function $f$ (deterministic or randomized, using independent randomness) cannot increase statistical distance: writing $f(P)$ for the distribution of $f(x)$ when $x \getsr P$,
\[
  \Delta\bigl(f(P), f(Q)\bigr) \le \Delta(P, Q).
\]
Feeding samples through $f$ can merge outcomes and hide differences, never amplify them. Combined with the \emph{triangle inequality},
\[
  \Delta(P, R) \le \Delta(P, Q) + \Delta(Q, R),
\]
this justifies \emph{hybrid arguments} -- security proofs that step from a real protocol $H_0$ to an ideal world $H_m$ by replacing one component at a time (Figure~\ref{ch07:fig-hybrid-chain}). If each single replacement changes the system's distribution by at most $\delta$, the real and ideal worlds sit within $m\delta$.

\section{Putting the pieces together}

Every chapter ahead uses these four measurements. For a stream cipher (which encrypts by XORing the plaintext with a key-derived bit sequence called a \emph{keystream}), security states that the keystream is computationally indistinguishable from uniform. For an $n$-bit hash function, the $2^{n/2}$ collision ceiling comes from the birthday bound. For a key generator, ``$256$ bits of entropy'' means min-entropy (Definition~\ref{ch07:min-entropy-def}), bounding one-shot guessing at $2^{-256}$. And for the one-time pad in Chapter~9, perfect secrecy reduces to $\Delta = 0$: every message induces the exact same uniform ciphertext distribution, so even an unbounded attacker learns zero bits. Because $\Delta = 0$ requires keys as long as messages, Chapter~8 develops computational hardness, $\PPT$ adversaries, and reductions for short-key cryptography.

\begin{recoverybox}
Check that you can answer four questions without notes: (1) Why does a uniform key maximize the attacker's uncertainty? (2) What does min-entropy catch that Shannon entropy misses? (3) Why does an $n$-bit hash provide $n/2$-bit collision resistance rather than $n$-bit? (4) What win probability can an attacker achieve in the distinguishing game when $\Delta(P, Q) = 0.01$?
\end{recoverybox}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Uniform key arithmetic]
\label{ch07:ex-uniform}
A system uses a $6$-bit key, drawn uniformly. (a) How many keys are there, and what is the probability Eve guesses the key on a single try? (b) What is the expected number of guesses for a brute-force attacker trying keys in random order without repeats? (c) Eve learns that the key is even (last bit $0$). After this leak, what is her single-guess success probability, and how many bits of the key's entropy has she gained?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A Bayesian leak]
\label{ch07:ex-bayes}
A $4$-bit key is uniform over $16$ values. A side channel reports the parity of the key (XOR of all four bits) and is correct with probability $0.75$, wrong with probability $0.25$. The probe says ``parity is $0$.'' (a) Using Bayes' rule, compute the posterior probability that the true parity is $0$. (b) Conditioned on the report, what is Eve's best single-guess success probability, and how does it compare with her $1/16$ before the leak? (c) A second, independent probe of the same accuracy also reports ``parity is $0$.'' Update again and give the new posterior. What does the trend say about repeated noisy measurements?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Three scores for one distribution]
\label{ch07:ex-guesswork}
For the distribution with probabilities $0.5$, $0.25$, $0.125$, $0.125$: (a) compute the Shannon entropy $H$, the min-entropy $H_\infty$, and the guessing entropy $G = \sum_i i\,p_i$ (outcomes sorted by decreasing probability). (b) Compute the same three for the uniform distribution on $4$ values. (c) Explain in two sentences which of the three numbers you would quote against an attacker who gets one guess, and which against an offline cracker trying all candidates in optimal order until recovery.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Entropy by hand]
\label{ch07:ex-entropy}
Compute the Shannon entropy $H(X)$ and the min-entropy $H_\infty(X)$ for each distribution. (a) $X$ uniform on $8$ values. (b) Probabilities $\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{4}$. (c) Probabilities $0.7, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1$. In which case is the gap $H - H_\infty$ largest, and explain in one sentence why that distribution is the weakest key source despite its Shannon entropy.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Min-entropy is what bounds guessing]
\label{ch07:ex-minent-bound}
A password is chosen so that \texttt{password123} has probability $0.05$ and the remaining probability is spread uniformly over $10^{6} - 1$ other strings. (a) Compute $H_\infty$. (b) Compute (approximately) $H$. (c) An attacker gets one guess. What is the best success probability, and which entropy predicted it? (d) Argue in two sentences why quoting the Shannon entropy here would overstate security.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The biased-bit key]
\label{ch07:ex-biased-key}
A flawed generator produces each key bit independently, with each bit equal to $1$ with probability $0.6$ rather than $0.5$. The key is $128$ bits. (a) What is the Shannon entropy per bit, and the total over $128$ bits? (b) What is the min-entropy per bit (the likeliest value of each bit is $1$), and the total across $128$ independent bits? (c) How many effective bits of security does this $128$-bit key have against a single-guess attacker, compared with a uniform $128$-bit key?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Birthday crossover]
\label{ch07:ex-birthday}
Using the approximation $\Pr[\text{collision}] \approx 1 - e^{-n^2/(2N)}$: (a) For $N = 365$, find the smallest integer $n$ making the collision probability at least $\frac{1}{2}$, and compare with the exact answer $23$. (b) For a $40$-bit hash ($N = 2^{40}$), about how many random inputs are needed before a collision is more likely than not? (c) State the general collision threshold for a $b$-bit hash ($N = 2^b$) in one line.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Sizing a hash]
\label{ch07:ex-sizing}
You are designing a system that must resist collision-finding by an attacker capable of $2^{100}$ hash evaluations. (a) What is the minimum digest length you should choose, and why? (b) Your colleague argues a $128$-bit digest suffices ``because $128 > 100$.'' Explain where that reasoning breaks. (c) The same system needs preimage resistance against the same $2^{100}$ attacker -- what digest length does preimage resistance alone require, and why is it different?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Counting collisions with indicators]
\label{ch07:ex-indicator}
Prove, using indicator random variables and linearity of expectation, that when $n$ items are thrown uniformly and independently into $N$ bins, the expected number of colliding (unordered) pairs is $\binom{n}{2}/N$. Find the sample count $n$ at which this expectation first reaches $1$ for $N = 2^{64}$, and connect your answer to the $2^{b/2}$ birthday rule for a $b$-bit digest ($b = 64$).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Statistical distance]
\label{ch07:ex-statdist}
Let $P$ be uniform on $\set{1,2,3,4}$ and let $Q$ assign probabilities $0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1$ to those four values. (a) Compute $\Delta(P, Q)$ from the sum form. (b) Find the optimal distinguishing event $A^*$ and its advantage, verifying that it equals $\Delta(P, Q)$. (c) What is the highest win probability achievable in the referee game of Figure~\ref{ch07:referee-game}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Distance cannot grow]
\label{ch07:ex-dpi}
Let $P$ and $Q$ be distributions on $\set{0,1}^{8}$ with $\Delta(P, Q) = 0.2$. Let $f$ map each byte to its parity (XOR of its eight bits), a single output bit. (a) State why $\Delta(f(P), f(Q)) \le 0.2$ without computing anything. (b) Give concrete distributions $P, Q$ where the inequality is strict, and another pair where equality holds. (c) Explain in one sentence why this inequality makes hybrid security proofs sound.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Why indistinguishability, not independence]
\label{ch07:ex-ind}
Shannon proved that perfect secrecy (ciphertext statistically independent of plaintext) requires a key at least as long as the message. (a) State why this makes perfect secrecy impractical for encrypting a $1\,\text{GB}$ file with a short key. (b) Explain how computational indistinguishability sidesteps that barrier while still protecting secrets, and state what restriction on the adversary makes the relaxation work. (c) In one sentence, relate IND-CPA security to the statistical distinguishing game of Figure~\ref{ch07:referee-game}.
\end{exercise}
