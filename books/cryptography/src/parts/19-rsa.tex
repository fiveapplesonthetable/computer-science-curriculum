\chapter{RSA}

In Chapter~18 Alice and Bob agreed on a shared secret across a public wire using Diffie--Hellman. That is an interactive key-\emph{agreement} protocol: both parties send a message, and the shared secret comes out of the exchange. RSA (named for Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir, and Leonard Adleman) removes the interaction. Alice publishes a short \emph{public key}, and anyone in the world can encrypt a message that only Alice can read, with no round trip and no prior contact. Bob can write to Alice the way you drop a letter through a mail slot: the slot is public, while the key to the box is not.

That asymmetry (public to lock, private to unlock) defines public-key encryption, and RSA was the first concrete scheme to achieve it for both encryption and digital signatures (publicly verifiable proof of who sent a message). Its arithmetic comes directly from Chapters~3 and~4: modular exponentiation, Euler's theorem, and the difficulty of factoring a large composite integer. How do you build a mathematical lock that anyone can close from public numbers, yet only the person who chose its prime factors can open?

\chapterline

\section{The setup: a one-way operation with a trapdoor}
\label{ch19:setup}

Extending the $(\Gen, \Enc, \Dec)$ template of symmetric encryption (Chapter~1) to the public-key setting gives three algorithms:

\begin{itemize}
  \item $\Gen$: key generation, which outputs a key pair $(pk, sk)$ consisting of a public key $pk$ that everyone may see and a secret (or private) key $sk$ that only the owner holds.
  \item $\Enc_{pk}(m)$: encryption, which lets anyone holding $pk$ turn a plaintext message $m$ into a ciphertext $c$.
  \item $\Dec_{sk}(c)$: decryption, which lets the holder of $sk$ recover $m$ from $c$.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{ch19:flow-diagram} shows the asymmetry we need: computing $\Enc_{pk}(m)$ must be easy for everyone, while inverting it to recover $m$ from $c$ must be hard for everyone \emph{except} the holder of $sk$. The mathematical object behind RSA is a \emph{trapdoor one-way permutation}: a bijection (one-to-one and onto mapping) from a finite set to itself that is fast to compute forward, infeasible to invert on a random output, yet fast to invert given one extra secret piece of information (the trapdoor; compare trapdoor one-way functions in Chapter~8).

\begin{intuitionbox}
Think of a padlock you can snap shut with a push, needing no key to lock it. Snapping it open again requires the physical key. Locking is encryption with the public key $pk$; opening is decryption with the private key $sk$. Diffie--Hellman gave us a one-way function (modular exponentiation) with no trapdoor, which is why Diffie--Hellman needs messages from both sides. RSA's contribution is the \emph{trapdoor}: choosing the modulus so that a secret piece of information makes inverting the exponentiation fast.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{historybox}
Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir, and Leonard Adleman published RSA at MIT in 1977, a year after Diffie and Hellman proposed public-key cryptography without giving a concrete encryption scheme. Shamir and Adleman spent months breaking Rivest's earlier drafts before Rivest hit on Euler's theorem modulo a two-prime product $n = pq$ after a Passover dinner. Publishing the paper sparked a long fight with the U.S.\ government over pre-publication censorship and export controls under the International Traffic in Arms Regulations (ITAR), which classified encryption software as a munition. In 1997 declassified archives revealed that Clifford Cocks at Britain's signals intelligence agency, Government Communications Headquarters (GCHQ), had found an equivalent scheme in 1973, where it stayed secret for over two decades.
\end{historybox}

\section{The number theory you need}
\label{ch19:numbertheory}

RSA rests on Euler's theorem (Chapter~4) and its prime-modulus special case, Fermat's little theorem.

For a positive integer $n$, Euler's totient $\varphi(n)$ counts the integers in $\set{1,2,\dots,n}$ that are coprime to $n$ (sharing no prime factor with $n$, so $\gcd(a,n)=1$). If $n=pq$ for distinct primes $p$ and $q$, then among $\set{1,2,\dots,pq}$ the integers \emph{not} coprime to $pq$ are $pq$ itself, the $q-1$ smaller multiples of $p$ ($p, 2p, \dots, (q-1)p$), and the $p-1$ smaller multiples of $q$ ($q, 2q, \dots, (p-1)q$). Because $p$ and $q$ are distinct primes, those two lists of smaller multiples do not overlap, giving
\begin{align*}
  \varphi(pq) &= pq - 1 - (q-1) - (p-1) \\
              &= pq - p - q + 1 \\
              &= (p-1)(q-1).
\end{align*}

\begin{theorem}[Euler]
\label{ch19:euler}
If $\gcd(a,n)=1$, then $a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod{n}$.
\end{theorem}

In group-theoretic terms (Chapter~5), the units modulo $n$ (the residues with multiplicative inverses), written $\Zns{n}$, form a group of size $\varphi(n)$ under multiplication modulo $n$. By Lagrange's theorem, the order of every element divides the size of the group, so raising any unit to the power $\varphi(n)$ returns the multiplicative identity $1$. Fermat's little theorem is the case $n=p$ prime, where $\varphi(p)=p-1$:
\[
  a^{p-1}\equiv 1 \pmod p \quad\text{whenever } p\nmid a \text{ ($p$ does not divide $a$)}.
\]

\begin{connectionbox}
Computing $a^e \bmod n$ uses square-and-multiply (Chapter~3 and the companion algorithms book): an exponent $e$ of $k$ bits takes at most $k$ squarings and $k$ multiplications, reducing modulo $n$ after each step. Exponentiating a $2048$-bit number therefore takes only a few thousand big-integer multiplications. RSA relies on the cost gap between directions: \emph{forward} exponentiation $m \mapsto m^e \bmod n$ is fast, whereas \emph{inverting} it (computing an $e$-th root modulo $n$ without the trapdoor) appears to require factoring $n$, for which no comparably fast classical algorithm is known.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Key generation}
\label{ch19:keygen}

To generate an RSA key pair:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Choose two large distinct primes $p$ and $q$ (in practice each around $1024$ bits or larger, so $n$ has at least $2048$ bits).
  \item Compute the \emph{modulus} $n = pq$ and the totient $\varphi(n) = (p-1)(q-1)$.
  \item Choose a public exponent $e$ satisfying $1 < e < \varphi(n)$ and $\gcd(e,\varphi(n))=1$. The standard choice is $e = 65537 = 2^{16} + 1$, whose binary form has only two $1$-bits, making square-and-multiply fast ($16$ squarings and one multiplication).
  \item Compute the private exponent $d = e^{-1} \bmod \varphi(n)$, the multiplicative inverse of $e$ modulo $\varphi(n)$, so that $ed \equiv 1 \pmod{\varphi(n)}$. This inverse exists because $\gcd(e,\varphi(n))=1$, and the extended Euclidean algorithm (Chapter~4) finds it in $O(\log \varphi(n))$ steps.
\end{enumerate}

The public key is $pk = (n, e)$ and the private key is $sk = (n, d)$. The primes $p$ and $q$ and the totient $\varphi(n)$ must be erased or guarded just like $d$, since anyone who learns $p$ and $q$ can immediately compute $\varphi(n) = (p-1)(q-1)$ and recover $d$.

\begin{focusbox}
The defining relation is $ed \equiv 1 \pmod{\varphi(n)}$: $e$ and $d$ are multiplicative inverses \emph{modulo $\varphi(n)$}, which makes raising to $e$ and raising to $d$ inverse operations \emph{modulo $n$}. Encryption raises to $e$, decryption raises to $d$, and because their product leaves remainder $1$ modulo $\varphi(n)$, applying one after the other returns the original message.
\end{focusbox}

\section{Encryption, decryption, and why it works}
\label{ch19:encdec}

A message is encoded as an integer $m$ with $0 \le m < n$. Encryption and decryption are each a single modular exponentiation:
\[
  c = \Enc_{pk}(m) = m^{e} \bmod n, \qquad
  m = \Dec_{sk}(c) = c^{d} \bmod n.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Correctness of RSA]
\label{ch19:correctness}
For every integer $m$ with $0\le m < n$, we have $(m^e)^d \equiv m \pmod n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why decryption undoes encryption.} We must show $m^{ed}\equiv m\pmod n$. Because $e > 1$, $d \ge 1$, and $ed \equiv 1\pmod{\varphi(n)}$, we can write $ed = 1 + k\varphi(n)$ for some integer $k \ge 1$. Therefore
\begin{align*}
  m^{ed} &= m^{1+k\varphi(n)} \\
         &= m\cdot\bigl(m^{\varphi(n)}\bigr)^{k}.
\end{align*}
If $\gcd(m,n)=1$, Theorem~\ref{ch19:euler} gives $m^{\varphi(n)}\equiv 1\pmod n$, so the parenthesized power is $1^k = 1$ and $m^{ed}\equiv m \pmod n$. That proves correctness for every $m$ coprime to $n$.

What if $\gcd(m,n) > 1$? If $m = 0$, then $0^{ed} = 0 \equiv 0 \pmod n$ because $ed \ge 1$. Otherwise $0 < m < pq$ shares a factor with $pq$, so $m$ is divisible by one of the primes, say $p \mid m$. Because $0 < m < pq$ and $p,q$ are distinct primes, $m$ cannot also be divisible by $q$ (else $pq \mid m$), so $\gcd(m,q) = 1$. We check modulo $p$ and modulo $q$ separately and combine them with the Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT, Chapter~4):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Modulo $q$:} Since $\gcd(m,q)=1$, Fermat's little theorem gives $m^{q-1}\equiv 1\pmod q$. Because $ed-1 = k(p-1)(q-1)$ is a multiple of $q-1$, raising both sides to $k(p-1)$ gives $m^{ed-1}\equiv 1\pmod q$, and multiplying by $m$ yields $m^{ed}\equiv m\pmod q$.
  \item \emph{Modulo $p$:} Since $p\mid m$, we have $m\equiv 0\pmod p$, and because $ed \ge 1$ we also have $m^{ed}\equiv 0\equiv m\pmod p$.
\end{itemize}
Since $m^{ed}\equiv m$ holds modulo both $p$ and $q$ and $\gcd(p,q)=1$, CRT guarantees $m^{ed}\equiv m\pmod{pq}$ for \emph{every} $m \in \set{0,1,\dots,n-1}$.
\end{whybox}

RSA decryption is therefore exact on the entire range $\set{0,1,\dots,n-1}$ (establishing that $m \mapsto m^e \bmod n$ is indeed a permutation of $\Zn{n}$). In practice, choosing an $m$ with $0 < m < n$ and $\gcd(m,n) > 1$ has probability $(p + q - 2)/(n - 1) \approx (p+q)/(pq)$ (around $2^{-1023}$ for $1024$-bit primes), and if such an $m$ were ever found, $\gcd(m,n)$ would immediately factor $n$.

\subsection{A miniature toy trace: \texorpdfstring{$p=5$, $q=11$, $n=55$}{p=5, q=11, n=55}}
\label{ch19:mini-toy}

Trace the arithmetic on numbers small enough to check by hand (summarized in Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-rsa-pipeline}, with the exponent cycle drawn in Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-exponent-cycle}):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Primes:} Choose $p = 5$ and $q = 11$.
  \item \textbf{Modulus and totient:} $n = 5 \cdot 11 = 55$, and $\varphi(n) = (5-1)(11-1) = 4 \cdot 10 = 40$.
  \item \textbf{Public exponent:} Pick $e = 3$, checking $\gcd(3, 40) = 1$.
  \item \textbf{Private exponent:} Solve $3d \equiv 1 \pmod{40}$. Since $3 \cdot 27 = 81 = 2 \cdot 40 + 1$, we get $d = 27$.
  \item \textbf{Keys:} Public key $pk = (55, 3)$, private key $sk = (55, 27)$.
  \item \textbf{Encrypt message $m = 9$:}
  \begin{align*}
    c &= m^e \bmod n \\
      &= 9^3 \bmod 55 \\
      &= 729 \bmod 55 \\
      &= 14 \quad (\text{since } 729 = 13 \cdot 55 + 14).
  \end{align*}
  \item \textbf{Decrypt ciphertext $c = 14$:} We compute $m = c^d \bmod n = 14^{27} \bmod 55$ by reducing modulo $5$ and modulo $11$:
  \begin{align*}
    14^{27} &\equiv (-1)^{27} \pmod 5 \\
            &= -1 \\
            &\equiv 4 \pmod 5, \\[1ex]
    14^{27} &\equiv 3^{27} \pmod{11} \\
            &= (3^{10})^2 \cdot 3^7 \\
            &\equiv 1^2 \cdot 2187 \pmod{11} &&\text{(by Fermat, $3^{10}\equiv 1 \pmod{11}$)} \\
            &\equiv 9 \pmod{11}.
  \end{align*}
  Among $\set{0, 1, \dots, 54}$, the integers congruent to $9 \pmod{11}$ are $9, 20, 31, 42, 53$; only $9 \equiv 4 \pmod 5$, so CRT gives $m = 9$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{A full worked example: \texorpdfstring{$p=37$, $q=43$}{p=37, q=43}}
\label{ch19:worked}

Trace the full scheme with two-digit primes, keeping every step checkable by hand.

\textbf{Key generation.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Choose $p = 37$ and $q = 43$.
  \item Compute $n = pq = 37\cdot 43 = 1591$ and $\varphi(n) = (p-1)(q-1) = 36\cdot 42 = 1512$.
  \item Choose $e = 11$. Check $\gcd(11,1512)=1$ with the Euclidean algorithm:
  \[
    1512 = 11\cdot 137 + 5, \qquad 11 = 5\cdot 2 + 1,
  \]
  so $\gcd(11,1512) = 1$, making $11$ a valid public exponent.
  \item Compute $d = 11^{-1}\bmod 1512$ by back-substituting those two divisions:
  \begin{align*}
    1 &= 11 - 2\cdot 5 \\
      &= 11 - 2\cdot(1512 - 137\cdot 11) \\
      &= 275\cdot 11 - 2\cdot 1512.
  \end{align*}
  Reducing modulo $1512$ gives $275\cdot 11 \equiv 1\pmod{1512}$, so $d = 275$.
\end{itemize}
Check: $11\cdot 275 = 3025 = 2\cdot 1512 + 1$, confirming $ed\equiv 1\pmod{\varphi(n)}$. The keys are $pk = (1591, 11)$ and $sk = (1591, 275)$.

\textbf{Encrypt.} Suppose Bob's message is $m = 42$. He computes $c = 42^{11}\bmod 1591$ by square-and-multiply, first squaring repeatedly modulo $1591$:
\begin{align*}
  42^1 &= 42, \\
  42^2 &= 1764 \\
       &= 1\cdot 1591 + 173 \\
       &\equiv 173 \pmod{1591}, \\[1ex]
  42^4 &\equiv 173^2 \pmod{1591} \\
       &= 29929 \\
       &= 18\cdot 1591 + 1291 \\
       &\equiv -300 \pmod{1591}, \\[1ex]
  42^8 &\equiv (-300)^2 \pmod{1591} \\
       &= 90000 \\
       &= 56\cdot 1591 + 904 \\
       &\equiv 904 \pmod{1591}.
\end{align*}
Because $11 = 8 + 2 + 1$ ($1011_2$ in binary), Bob multiplies the matching powers modulo $1591$:
\begin{align*}
  42^{11} &= 42^8\cdot 42^2\cdot 42^1 \\
          &\equiv 904\cdot 173\cdot 42 \pmod{1591}, \\[1ex]
  904\cdot 173 &= 156392 \\
               &= 98\cdot 1591 + 474 \\
               &\equiv 474 \pmod{1591}, \\[1ex]
  474\cdot 42 &= 19908 \\
              &= 12\cdot 1591 + 816 \\
              &\equiv 816 \pmod{1591}.
\end{align*}
Thus Bob sends ciphertext $c = 816$.

\textbf{Decrypt.} Alice computes $m = 816^{275}\bmod 1591$. In binary, $275 = 100010011_2$ ($256 + 16 + 2 + 1$), so square-and-multiply takes eight squarings and three extra multiplications modulo $1591$, yielding $816^{275}\equiv 42\pmod{1591}$, the original message.

\begin{walkbox}
Want to check decryption by hand without grinding through eight full-width squarings? Use CRT, just as in the correctness proof. Because Alice knows $p=37$ and $q=43$, she precomputes the reduced exponents (via Fermat's little theorem modulo $p$ and $q$)
\[
  d_p = d\bmod(p-1) = 275\bmod 36 = 23, \qquad
  d_q = d\bmod(q-1) = 275\bmod 42 = 23,
\]
and the per-prime ciphertext residues $c_p = 816\bmod 37 = 2$ and $c_q = 816\bmod 43 = 42 \equiv -1 \pmod{43}$. She then computes two smaller exponentiations:
\[
  m_p = c_p^{d_p}\bmod 37 = 2^{23}\bmod 37 = 5, \qquad
  m_q = c_q^{d_q}\bmod 43 = (-1)^{23}\bmod 43 = 42.
\]
For $2^{23} \bmod 37$, note that $2^5 = 32 \equiv -5 \pmod{37}$, so $2^{10} \equiv 25 \equiv -12 \pmod{37}$, $2^{20} \equiv 144 \equiv -4 \pmod{37}$, and $2^{23} = 2^{20}\cdot 8 \equiv -32 \equiv 5 \pmod{37}$.

Finally, she combines $m \equiv 5 \pmod{37}$ and $m \equiv 42 \pmod{43}$ via CRT. Writing $m = 42 + 43t$ and requiring $42 + 43t \equiv 5 \pmod{37}$ gives $5 + 6t \equiv 5 \pmod{37}$, so $6t \equiv 0 \pmod{37}$ and $t = 0$ (for $0 \le m < 1591$), recovering
\begin{align*}
  m &= 42 + 43\cdot 0 \\
    &= 42.
\end{align*}
Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-rsacrt} shows this \emph{RSA-CRT} pipeline. Production libraries decrypt this way for roughly a fourfold speedup: $p$ and $q$ each have half as many bits as $n$, so each modular multiplication takes $(1/2)^2 = 1/4$ the work and each exponent $d_p, d_q$ has half as many bits ($1/2$ as many squarings), making each branch cost $\approx 1/8$ of a full-modulus exponentiation and both branches together cost $2 \cdot (1/8) = 1/4$.
\end{walkbox}

\begin{implementbox}
Textbook RSA in a few lines of Python. \textbf{For learning only: never deploy this code.} It lacks padding, constant-time arithmetic, and cryptographic prime generation.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
from math import gcd

def egcd(a, b):                  # returns (g, x, y) with a*x + b*y = g
    if b == 0:
        return (a, 1, 0)
    g, x, y = egcd(b, a % b)
    return (g, y, x - (a // b) * y)

def inv_mod(a, m):
    g, x, _ = egcd(a % m, m)
    if g != 1:
        raise ValueError("no inverse")
    return x % m

def keygen(p, q, e=65537):
    n = p * q
    phi = (p - 1) * (q - 1)
    if gcd(e, phi) != 1:
        raise ValueError("bad e")
    d = inv_mod(e, phi)
    return (n, e), (n, d)        # public, private

def encrypt(pk, m):
    n, e = pk
    return pow(m, e, n)          # pow does square-and-multiply

def decrypt(sk, c):
    n, d = sk
    return pow(c, d, n)

# Reproduce the worked example:
pub, priv = keygen(37, 43, e=11)
print(pub, priv)                 # (1591, 11) (1591, 275)
c = encrypt(pub, 42)
print(c)                         # 816
print(decrypt(priv, c))          # 42
\end{lstlisting}
Encryption and decryption are one line each, and the missing padding is the vulnerability examined in Section~\ref{ch19:textbook-broken}.
\end{implementbox}

\section{The factoring assumption}
\label{ch19:factoring}

Why can't Eve, who sees $pk=(n,e)$ and ciphertext $c=m^e\bmod n$, recover $m$? She would need to compute an $e$-th root modulo $n$. The direct route is to compute $d = e^{-1} \bmod \varphi(n)$, which requires $\varphi(n)=(p-1)(q-1)$. Because $\varphi(n) = n - (p+q) + 1$, knowing both $n = pq$ and $\varphi(n)$ determines $p+q$ and $pq$, and therefore $p$ and $q$ as the two roots of $x^2 - (p+q)x + n = 0$. Computing $\varphi(n)$ is thus just as hard as factoring $n$.

\begin{definition}[RSA assumption]
\label{ch19:rsa-assumption}
Sample two random large $k$-bit primes $p$ and $q$, set $n=pq$, pick an exponent $e$ with $\gcd(e,\varphi(n))=1$, and sample a uniformly random target $y\in\Zns{n}$. The \emph{RSA problem} is to find $x \in \Zns{n}$ satisfying $x^e\equiv y\pmod n$. The RSA assumption states that no $\PPT$ (probabilistic polynomial-time, Chapter~8) algorithm solves this problem with non-negligible probability in $k$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Factoring assumption]
\label{ch19:factoring-assumption}
Given $n=pq$ for two random large $k$-bit primes $p$ and $q$, no $\PPT$ algorithm outputs $p$ or $q$ with non-negligible probability in $k$.
\end{definition}

Factoring breaks RSA: factoring $n$ yields $\varphi(n)$, then $d$, and hence every plaintext. Whether the converse holds (whether computing $e$-th roots modulo $n$ \emph{requires} factoring $n$) remains an open question; computing $e$-th roots could in principle be easier than factoring. The RSA assumption is therefore at least as strong an assumption as the factoring assumption. After decades of cryptanalysis, the fastest known classical algorithm for factoring general integers, the \emph{general number field sieve} (GNFS), runs in heuristic time roughly proportional to
\[
  \exp\!\Bigl( \bigl(\tfrac{64}{9}\bigr)^{1/3} (\ln n)^{1/3}(\ln\ln n)^{2/3} \Bigr)
\]
(ignoring lower-order factors in the exponent, where $\ln n$ is the natural logarithm of $n$, proportional to the bit-length of $n$). This running time is \emph{sub-exponential} (growing faster than any polynomial in $\ln n$, yet slower than any full exponential $e^{c \ln n}$), reaching roughly $2^{112}$ operations for a well-chosen $2048$-bit modulus (Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-gnfs-scaling}).

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{The RSA Factoring Challenge.} Between 1991 and 2007, RSA Laboratories offered cash prizes for factoring specific composite moduli (named by either their decimal-digit count or their bit-length). RSA-129 (a $129$-digit, $426$-bit number published in Martin Gardner's 1977 \emph{Scientific American} column, which Rivest originally estimated would take $40$ quadrillion years) fell in 1994 to a distributed internet effort. RSA-768 ($768$ bits) was factored in 2009 after roughly two thousand core-years of computation, and RSA-250 ($250$ decimal digits, $829$ bits) fell in 2020. No $1024$-bit or larger challenge modulus has been publicly factored, which is why $2048$ bits is today's minimum and $3072$ bits or more is recommended for long-lived keys.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Sub-exponential sieve complexity explains why RSA keys are so much longer than symmetric keys for comparable security: a $128$-bit AES key and a $3072$-bit RSA modulus require similar classical attack effort (around $2^{128}$ operations). The companion algorithms book derives the $\exp((\ln n)^{1/3}\cdots)$ exponent from the cost of collecting \emph{smooth} integers (numbers that factor completely into small primes) to build a \emph{congruence of squares} $x^2 \equiv y^2 \pmod n$, where $\gcd(x-y, n)$ splits $n$. On a large fault-tolerant quantum computer, Shor's algorithm (Chapter~35) factors integers in polynomial time $O((\log n)^3)$, breaking RSA completely and motivating post-quantum cryptography.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Why textbook RSA is broken}
\label{ch19:textbook-broken}

Everything so far defines \emph{textbook RSA}: encrypting the raw message integer directly as $c=m^e\bmod n$. Although decryption is exact and factoring protects the private key, textbook RSA fails as an encryption scheme because key secrecy does not guarantee message secrecy. Textbook RSA is \emph{deterministic}: the same $m$ always produces the same $c$. No deterministic public-key scheme can be semantically secure (hiding all partial information about the plaintext, Chapter~10) or satisfy indistinguishability under chosen-plaintext attack (IND-CPA, Chapter~13), because Eve holds $pk$ herself and can encrypt candidate plaintexts on her own to check for a match.

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Four ways textbook RSA leaks.}

\emph{Guess-and-check.} Suppose Bob sends one of two known messages, ``BUY'' or ``SELL''. Holding $pk$, Eve computes $\Enc_{pk}(\text{BUY})$ and $\Enc_{pk}(\text{SELL})$ herself and compares them to the intercepted $c$, reading the plaintext without touching $sk$. Any small or predictable message set (a four-digit PIN, a yes/no vote, a short password) falls to a dictionary of forward encryptions.


\emph{Small-message / small-$e$ attack.} If $e=3$ and $m$ is small enough that $m^3 < n$, then $c = m^3 \bmod n = m^3$ never wraps around the modulus. Eve takes the ordinary integer cube root $\sqrt[3]{c}$ over $\Z$ to recover $m$ directly, with no factoring. Short plaintexts (such as an unpadded $128$-bit key) fail whenever $m^e < n$ (Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-small-e}).

\emph{H\r{a}stad's broadcast attack.} Suppose Bob sends the \emph{same} message $m$ to three recipients who all use $e=3$ with distinct moduli $n_1,n_2,n_3$. Eve collects $c_i = m^3\bmod n_i$ for $i=1,2,3$. If any two moduli share a prime factor, $\gcd(n_i, n_j)$ factors both immediately; otherwise $n_1, n_2, n_3$ are pairwise coprime and CRT reconstructs $m^3 \bmod n_1 n_2 n_3$. Because $0 \le m < n_i$ for each $i$, we have $m^3 < n_1 n_2 n_3$, so that residue equals $m^3$ over $\Z$, and an integer cube root recovers $m$.

\emph{Malleability.} Textbook RSA is \emph{multiplicatively homomorphic} (multiplying ciphertexts encrypts the product of their plaintexts):
\begin{align*}
  \Enc_{pk}(m_1)\cdot\Enc_{pk}(m_2) &\equiv m_1^e \cdot m_2^e \pmod n \\
                                    &= (m_1 m_2)^e \\
                                    &\equiv \Enc_{pk}(m_1 m_2) \pmod n.
\end{align*}
This makes ciphertexts \emph{malleable} (Chapter~10): from $c = m^e \bmod n$, Eve can compute $c' = c\cdot 2^e\bmod n \equiv (2m)^e \pmod n$ without knowing $m$. In a chosen-ciphertext setting (where a server will decrypt ciphertexts other than the target $c$), Eve submits $c'$, receives $m' = 2m \bmod n$, and multiplies by $2^{-1} \bmod n$ to recover $m$ (Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-textbook-attacks}).
\end{attackbox}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Never encrypt a raw message with $m^e\bmod n$.} Determinism alone breaks confidentiality regardless of key size. Before exponentiating, a \emph{padding scheme} must inject fresh randomness and verifiable redundancy into $m$ so that (a) encrypting the same plaintext twice produces independent-looking ciphertexts, and (b) tampered ciphertexts fail validation upon decryption.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Padding done right: OAEP}
\label{ch19:oaep}

The standard fix for RSA encryption is \emph{Optimal Asymmetric Encryption Padding} (OAEP), designed by Mihir Bellare and Phillip Rogaway in 1994. Before exponentiating, OAEP passes the message through a randomized, reversible network built from two hash functions modeled as random oracles (idealized hashes whose outputs behave like independent uniform bitstrings for each distinct input, Chapter~14). The resulting integer fills the width of the modulus and scrambles completely if any bit is altered.

Suppose $n$ has $k$ bits, and choose bit-lengths $n_0$ and $n_1$ with $n_1 + n_0 = k - 1$ (leaving a leading $0$-bit so the padded integer is strictly less than $n$). Fix two mask-generating hash functions $G : \set{0,1}^{n_0} \to \set{0,1}^{n_1}$ (expanding the seed length to the message-block length) and $H : \set{0,1}^{n_1} \to \set{0,1}^{n_0}$ (compressing back to the seed length). To encrypt a message $M$, first append fixed zero padding (and the hash of an optional context label, usually empty) to form an $n_1$-bit block $M'$, sample a fresh uniform $n_0$-bit seed $r$, and compute a two-round Feistel-like mixing:
\[
  X = M' \xor G(r), \qquad Y = r \xor H(X).
\]
Form the $(k-1)$-bit integer $m = 0 \concat X\concat Y$ (satisfying $0 \le m < n$) and output $c = m^e\bmod n$. To decrypt, compute $m = c^d \bmod n$, check that the leading bit is $0$, parse $X$ and $Y$, invert the two XOR steps in reverse order via
\[
  r = Y\xor H(X), \qquad M' = X\xor G(r),
\]
and verify that the fixed zero-padding bytes in $M'$ are intact; if any check fails, reject the ciphertext without revealing which check failed. Figure~\ref{ch19:oaep-diagram} traces the mixing.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why OAEP closes the leaks.} Sampling a fresh seed $r$ makes encryption probabilistic: encrypting the same $M$ twice gives unrelated ciphertexts, stopping guess-and-check and H\r{a}stad broadcast attacks. Because $G(r)$ and $H(X)$ diffuse across all $n_1 + n_0$ bits, $m$ spans the width of the modulus, ruling out unreduced cube roots. Checking the fixed zero padding upon decryption defeats malleability: modifying $c$ produces a scrambled $m$ whose unmasked $M'$ fails the zero-padding check with overwhelming probability. In the random-oracle model, RSA-OAEP is provably secure under adaptive chosen-ciphertext attack (\emph{IND-CCA2}: an adversary cannot tell which of two plaintexts was encrypted even when given a decryption oracle for arbitrary other ciphertexts both before and after seeing the challenge ciphertext; compare IND-CCA in Chapter~16) under the RSA assumption.
\end{whybox}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Bleichenbacher's attack on PKCS\#1 v1.5.} The earlier RSA standard, Public-Key Cryptography Standards \#1 version 1.5 (PKCS\#1 v1.5), formatted a $k$-byte block as $\mathtt{0x00} \concat \mathtt{0x02} \concat \text{random nonzero bytes} \concat \mathtt{0x00} \concat M$, without OAEP's two-way hash mixing. In 1998 Daniel Bleichenbacher showed that if a server reveals even a single bit indicating whether a decrypted ciphertext begins with $\mathtt{0x00\,0x02}$ (a padding oracle, Chapter~13), an attacker can recover any target plaintext $m$ in roughly a million chosen-ciphertext queries. Multiplying the target ciphertext $c$ by $s^e \bmod n$ asks the server whether $s \cdot m \bmod n$ falls inside the narrow valid prefix band $[2B, 3B-1]$ (where $B = 2^{8(k-2)}$), slicing the range of possible values for $m$ smaller on every ``valid'' answer (Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-bleichenbacher}). In 2017 the ROBOT attack (Return Of Bleichenbacher's Oracle Threat) showed this flaw was still exploitable in major TLS servers that retained legacy v1.5 key transport. Padding and indistinguishable error handling are part of the cryptographic boundary, not formatting details.
\end{attackbox}

\section{Implementation mistakes}
\label{ch19:mistakes}

Even with OAEP padding, mistakes in choosing RSA parameters have broken deployed systems.

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Small public exponent $e$ on unpadded messages.} As Section~\ref{ch19:textbook-broken} showed, $e=3$ on an unpadded or deterministically padded $m$ invites integer cube-root and H\r{a}stad broadcast attacks. Standards settled on $e = 65537 = 2^{16}+1$: it avoids low-exponent attacks on legacy formats while keeping encryption fast because its binary expansion has only two $1$-bits. (With full-width randomized OAEP, $e=3$ is also safe; the hazard is combining small $e$ with missing or rigid padding.)
\end{attackbox}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Small private exponent $d$.} Choosing a small $d$ is a tempting way to speed up decryption, since $c^d \bmod n$ then requires fewer squarings. In 1990 Michael Wiener showed that whenever $d < \tfrac{1}{3} n^{1/4}$, an attacker can recover $d$ from $(n,e)$ in polynomial time using \emph{continued fractions} (tight rational approximations generated by repeatedly taking reciprocals of remainders): because $ed - k\varphi(n) = 1$ and $\varphi(n) \approx n$, the secret fraction $k/d$ sits close enough to the public ratio $e/n$ to appear among its continued-fraction convergents. Dan Boneh and Glenn Durfee later extended the attack to $d < n^{0.292}$. Never choose a small $d$; accelerate decryption with RSA-CRT instead.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Shared modulus.} Suppose an organization issues several users distinct exponent pairs $(e_i, d_i)$ over the \emph{same} modulus $n$. This fails in two independent ways. First, every user holds a pair $(e_i, d_i)$ with $e_i d_i - 1 = k_i \varphi(n)$, and \emph{any} nonzero multiple of $\varphi(n)$ lets its holder factor $n$ in expected polynomial time: write $e_i d_i - 1 = 2^s t$ with $t$ odd, sample a random base $g \in \Zns{n}$, and square $g^t, g^{2t}, \dots \bmod n$ until finding a nontrivial square root $x \not\equiv \pm 1 \pmod n$ of $1$, so that $\gcd(x-1, n)$ reveals $p$ or $q$ (Example~\ref{ch08:ex-rabin}). User~A can then compute every other user's private key. Second, if the same unpadded message $m$ is encrypted under two coprime public exponents $e_1$ and $e_2$ on the same $n$, an eavesdropper holding $c_1 = m^{e_1} \bmod n$ and $c_2 = m^{e_2} \bmod n$ uses the extended Euclidean algorithm to find integers $a, b$ with $a e_1 + b e_2 = 1$ and computes
\begin{align*}
  c_1^{a} c_2^{b} &\equiv m^{a e_1 + b e_2} \pmod n \\
                  &= m^1 \\
                  &= m.
\end{align*}
(evaluating a negative exponent via the modular inverse modulo $n$). Never share an RSA modulus across keys.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Low-entropy primes (the GCD harvest).} If two distinct RSA moduli share a prime factor, say $n_1 = p q_1$ and $n_2 = p q_2$ with $q_1 \ne q_2$, then $\gcd(n_1,n_2) = p$ factors both moduli in microseconds using Euclid's algorithm, bypassing GNFS entirely. In 2012 two independent research groups (Arjen Lenstra et al.\ and Nadia Heninger et al.) collected millions of public TLS and SSH keys from the internet and computed their pairwise GCDs, factoring hundreds of thousands of keys. Embedded devices such as routers and firewalls had generated keys at first boot before their operating-system entropy pools (Chapter~17) had gathered enough timing noise, causing unrelated devices to pick the same primes. Always generate primes from a properly seeded CSPRNG.

For a concrete trace (Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-shared-prime-gcd}), suppose two poorly seeded devices publish $n_1 = 10403$ and $n_2 = 10807$. Factoring either number alone takes prime search up to $\floor{\sqrt{n_i}} \approx 103$, whereas one Euclidean GCD gives $\gcd(10403,10807) = 101$, splitting $n_1 = 101\cdot 103$ and $n_2 = 101\cdot 107$ in a single division each. Across a collection of $N$ public moduli, a \emph{batch-GCD} algorithm finds all colliding pairs in $O(N\log^2 N)$ operations rather than $\binom{N}{2}$ separate GCDs by building a \emph{product tree}: a binary tree that multiplies all $N$ moduli upward to their product $P = n_1 n_2 \cdots n_N$ and then reduces $P$ downward modulo each $n_i^2$ to extract $\gcd(n_i, P/n_i)$ for every key at once.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{trapbox}
Three further implementation requirements apply to every RSA deployment:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Distance between $p$ and $q$.} The primes $p$ and $q$ must have the same bit-length without lying too close together. If $b = \abs{p-q}/2$ is small, then $a = (p+q)/2 = \sqrt{n + b^2}$ sits slightly above $\sqrt{n}$, so \emph{Fermat's factorization method} tests integers $a = \ceil{\sqrt{n}}, \ceil{\sqrt{n}}+1, \dots$ until $a^2 - n = b^2$ is a perfect square and factors
  \begin{align*}
    n &= a^2 - b^2 \\
      &= (a - b)(a + b) \\
      &= q \cdot p.
  \end{align*}
  \item \textbf{Unstructured primes.} Primes must be sampled uniformly rather than built from a restricted algebraic template; in 2017 the \emph{ROCA} flaw (Return of Coppersmith's Attack) factored millions of Infineon smart-card keys whose primes came from a narrow formula that leaked enough structure to solve for $p$ and $q$.
  \item \textbf{Constant-time decryption.} Modular exponentiation with $d$ (or $d_p, d_q$) must execute in \emph{constant time}, meaning its instruction count, branches, and memory addresses do not depend on the secret bits of $d$; otherwise timing or CPU-cache measurements can recover $d$ bit by bit (Chapter~37).
\end{itemize}
\end{trapbox}

\section{The other half: RSA signatures}
\label{ch19:signatures}

The same trapdoor permutation also builds digital signatures when the roles of $e$ and $d$ are swapped. To \emph{sign} a message, the holder of the secret key $sk = (n,d)$ raises to $d$, producing a signature $\sigma$; to \emph{verify}, anyone holding $pk = (n,e)$ raises $\sigma$ to $e$. Encryption is ``public to lock, private to unlock''; signing is ``private to stamp, public to check.''

In textbook RSA signatures, a message integer $m \in \set{0,1,\dots,n-1}$ is signed and verified by
\[
  \sigma = \Sign_{sk}(m) = m^{d}\bmod n, \qquad
  \Vrfy_{pk}(m,\sigma) : \ \text{accept if and only if } \sigma^{e}\equiv m \pmod n.
\]
Correctness follows from the identity $(m^d)^e = m^{de} = m^{ed}\equiv m\pmod n$ of Theorem~\ref{ch19:correctness}, since multiplication in the exponent is commutative.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why this authenticates.} Only the holder of $sk$ knows $d$, so only she can directly compute an $e$-th root $\sigma$ of $m$ modulo $n$. A forger who knows only $pk=(n,e)$ and wants a valid signature on a target $m$ must solve $\sigma^e \equiv m \pmod n$, which is the RSA problem of Definition~\ref{ch19:rsa-assumption}. Anyone can run verification because $e$ and $n$ are public.
\end{whybox}

Like textbook RSA encryption, raw textbook RSA signing is insecure without hashing and padding.

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Forgeries against raw RSA signatures.}

\emph{Existential forgery.} Pick any integer $\sigma \in \Zns{n}$ and compute $m = \sigma^e\bmod n$. The pair $(m,\sigma)$ passes verification $\sigma^e \equiv m \pmod n$ automatically. Although the attacker does not choose $m$ in advance, in protocols where messages are raw keys, nonces, or structured numbers, a valid signature on \emph{some} message (an existential forgery, Chapter~15) can already cause harm.

\emph{Multiplicative forgery.} Given valid signatures $\sigma_1 = m_1^d \bmod n$ and $\sigma_2 = m_2^d \bmod n$, their product
\begin{align*}
  \sigma_1\sigma_2 &\equiv m_1^d m_2^d \pmod n \\
                   &= (m_1 m_2)^d.
\end{align*}
is a valid signature on $m_1 m_2 \bmod n$, letting an attacker combine signatures on benign messages into a signature on their product.

\emph{Blinding.} Suppose the signer refuses to sign a specific target $m$. The attacker samples a random unit $r \in \Zns{n}$ (so $\gcd(r,n)=1$ and $r^{-1} \bmod n$ exists), asks the signer to sign the disguised value $m' = r^e m\bmod n$ (which is uniformly distributed in $\Zns{n}$ when $m \in \Zns{n}$), receives $\sigma' = (m')^d \bmod n$, and unblinds by multiplying by $r^{-1} \bmod n$:
\begin{align*}
  \sigma' \cdot r^{-1} &\equiv (r^e m)^d \cdot r^{-1} \pmod n \\
                       &\equiv r^{ed} m^d r^{-1} \pmod n \\
                       &\equiv r \cdot m^d \cdot r^{-1} \pmod n \\
                       &= m^d,
\end{align*}
recovering a valid signature $\sigma = m^d \bmod n$ on the forbidden message $m$.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{trapbox}
Never sign a raw message integer $m$. Modern RSA signing uses \textbf{RSA-PSS} (Probabilistic Signature Scheme, Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-rsa-signatures}), which hashes the message together with a fresh random salt (a short random bitstring) and formats a full-width representative $\mu < n$ before computing $\sigma = \mu^d \bmod n$. Hashing destroys the multiplicative relation $(m_1 m_2)^d = m_1^d m_2^d$ because $\Hash(M_1 \cdot M_2)$ bears no algebraic relation to $\Hash(M_1)$ and $\Hash(M_2)$; the salt makes signatures randomized; and the structured padding causes random $\sigma^e \bmod n$ values to fail verification. In the random-oracle model, forging an RSA-PSS signature is provably nearly as hard as solving the RSA problem. The older deterministic PKCS\#1 v1.5 signature format remains common in legacy certificates and avoids the padding-oracle failure of v1.5 encryption, though RSA-PSS is the modern standard. Chapter~22 develops digital signatures in detail.
\end{trapbox}

\section{RSA in the real world}
\label{ch19:realworld}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Where RSA is used today.} For decades TLS used RSA in two roles: the server's certificate held an RSA public key, and the client encrypted a random session-key seed (called the \emph{pre-master secret}) under that key. Because static RSA key transport provides no forward secrecy (Chapter~18) and repeatedly suffered Bleichenbacher and ROBOT oracle flaws, TLS~1.3 removed RSA key exchange completely in favor of ephemeral Diffie--Hellman. RSA remains central for \emph{authentication}: TLS~1.3 servers sign handshakes with RSA-PSS, and a large share of certificates in the web's public-key infrastructure (PKI, the system of certificate authorities binding domain names to public keys, Chapter~24) carry RSA keys. SSH host and user keys often use RSA with SHA-2 signatures, as do Pretty Good Privacy (PGP) and its open-source implementation GNU Privacy Guard (GnuPG), software code-signing systems, document signatures, and hardware smart cards. In modern deployments RSA serves primarily for signatures and certificates, while elliptic-curve Diffie--Hellman (ECDH, Chapter~21) handles key exchange and AES-GCM (Chapter~16) encrypts bulk data.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{focusbox}
Practical rule for deployed systems: RSA encryption means \emph{RSA-OAEP}, and RSA signing means \emph{RSA-PSS} (Chapter~22). Use a modulus of at least $2048$ bits ($3072$ bits or more for keys intended to remain valid past 2030), public exponent $e = 65537$, and primes generated by a vetted library seeded from the operating system's CSPRNG. Treat raw $m^e\bmod n$, PKCS\#1 v1.5 encryption, or custom prime generation as broken.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
RSA builds its trapdoor from the hardness of factoring $n = pq$: knowing the factors gives $\varphi(n)$ and lets you invert exponents. In Chapter~18 we met a second hard problem, computing discrete logarithms in a cyclic group. Chapter~20 turns that discrete logarithm problem into a full public-key encryption scheme: ElGamal.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch19:exercises}

\begin{exercise}
With $p=11$, $q=23$, compute $n$ and $\varphi(n)$. Show that $e=3$ is a valid public exponent, and find the corresponding private exponent $d$ using the extended Euclidean algorithm.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Using your key from the previous exercise, encrypt the message $m=7$ by computing $7^{3}\bmod n$ with square-and-multiply, then decrypt the result and confirm you recover $7$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Prove that for RSA, $\Enc_{pk}(m_1)\cdot\Enc_{pk}(m_2)\equiv \Enc_{pk}(m_1 m_2)\pmod n$. Explain in one sentence why this homomorphic property makes textbook RSA malleable, and why OAEP destroys it.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
(Common modulus.) Two users share modulus $n=1591$ with public exponents $e_1=11$ and $e_2=5$ (check that each is coprime to $\varphi(1591)=1512$, so both are valid exponents), and the same message $m$ is sent to both, yielding $c_1$ and $c_2$. Show $\gcd(e_1,e_2)=1$, find integers $a,b$ with $a e_1+b e_2=1$, and write the formula that recovers $m$ from $c_1,c_2$. (One of $a,b$ is negative; how do you handle a negative exponent modulo $n$? Hint: replace $c_1^{a}$ by $(c_1^{-1})^{\abs{a}}$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
(H\r{a}stad in miniature.) A message $m=10$ is sent with $e=3$ to three recipients with moduli $n_1=21$, $n_2=143$, $n_3=323$ (each a product of two small distinct primes, and the three are pairwise coprime, as CRT requires). Compute the three ciphertexts $c_i = m^3\bmod n_i$, CRT-combine them to recover $m^3$ modulo $n_1 n_2 n_3$, and take the integer cube root to get $m$. Verify $m^3 < n_1 n_2 n_3$. (You should find $c_1=13$, $c_2=142$, $c_3=31$, and the combined value $1000$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Suppose you are told $n=1591$ and $\varphi(n)=1512$. Recover $p$ and $q$ without trial division, by setting up and solving the quadratic whose roots are $p$ and $q$. (Hint: you know $p+q = n-\varphi(n)+1$ and $pq=n$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Two RSA moduli $n_1 = 5459$ and $n_2 = 5777$ were generated by a device with a broken random source and happen to share a prime. Compute $\gcd(n_1,n_2)$ to find the shared prime, then factor both moduli completely. Explain why this attack is exponentially cheaper than factoring either modulus from scratch.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Explain precisely why a deterministic public-key encryption scheme can never satisfy IND-CPA security. Frame your answer as a winning strategy for the IND-CPA adversary against textbook RSA.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
(Why $\gcd(m,n)>1$ is rare in practice.) Show that if Bob ever encrypts a message $m$ with $0 < m < n$ and $\gcd(m,n)=p$, then anyone who sees $m$ can factor $n$. Estimate the probability that a uniformly random $m\in\set{1,\dots,n-1}$ has $\gcd(m,n)>1$ for a $2048$-bit modulus, and argue that it is negligible.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
(Wiener's setup.) Look up the statement of Wiener's attack and explain, in your own words, why choosing a small private exponent $d$ to speed up decryption is dangerous, and why using RSA-CRT achieves the same speedup safely. You need not reproduce the continued-fraction machinery; argue at the level of which quantities leak.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
(RSA-CRT by hand.) Using $pk=(1591,11)$, $sk=(1591,275)$ from the worked example, decrypt the ciphertext $c=816$ \emph{via CRT}: reduce $c$ modulo $37$ and $43$, reduce $d$ modulo $36$ and $42$, perform the two small exponentiations, and CRT-combine to recover $m$. Check that you get $42$, and count how many modular multiplications you used versus the full $816^{275}\bmod 1591$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
(Signature forgery.) For the public key $pk=(1591,11)$, produce an existential forgery: pick any $\sigma$ you like, compute $m=\sigma^{11}\bmod 1591$, and confirm that $(m,\sigma)$ passes textbook verification. Then, given that $\sigma_1$ signs $m_1$ and $\sigma_2$ signs $m_2$, write the signature on $m_1 m_2 \bmod 1591$ in terms of $\sigma_1,\sigma_2$. Explain in one sentence how hashing the message before signing kills both forgeries.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
(Blinding.) A signer will stamp anything that ``looks random'' but refuses to sign the specific value $m^\star$. Describe precisely the messages an attacker submits, and the post-processing on the returned signatures, to obtain a valid signature on $m^\star$ anyway. Which property of textbook RSA makes this possible, and why does RSA-PSS prevent it?
\end{exercise}
