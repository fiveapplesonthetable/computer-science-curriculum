\chapter{Authenticated Encryption}

Up to now we have built two kinds of machine and kept them in separate rooms. One kind hides a message: stream ciphers, block ciphers, and the modes that turn a block cipher into something that encrypts arbitrary data. The other kind detects tampering: hash functions, and the keyed message authentication codes (MACs) built on top of them. Encryption protects \emph{confidentiality}; a MAC protects \emph{integrity} and \emph{data-origin authentication}. The natural next question is the one a working engineer faces: I have a channel, I have a key, and I want to send a message that nobody can read and nobody can alter undetected. Which machine do I use?

The answer is ``both, combined in the right order -- or you risk getting neither.'' Combining encryption and authentication looks routine -- encrypt, compute a MAC, and send both -- yet two of the three natural ways to wire the two building blocks together fail in general, and those two were the popular choices for years, costing deployed protocols a decade of practical breaks. Modern cryptography resolves this with a single primitive, \emph{authenticated encryption with associated data} (AEAD), which hides the secret payload, authenticates both that payload and any unencrypted header fields (\emph{associated data}, such as packet addresses or sequence numbers), and exposes one interface that leaves no separate parts to miswire.

\section{Why Confidentiality Without Integrity Is a Trap}
\label{ch16:why-both}

Beginners carry a stubborn intuition: if Eve cannot \emph{read} my ciphertext, surely she cannot \emph{change} it into anything meaningful either. The ciphertext looks like random noise; how could an attacker who cannot decode it nudge it toward a chosen plaintext? The intuition is wrong, and the reason lies in the structure of the fast, simple ciphers we have already built.

Recall the one-time pad and every stream cipher built in its image (Chapter~10). Encryption is $c = m \xor k$, where $m$ is the plaintext, $k$ is the keystream, and $c$ is the ciphertext. This is confidential when $k$ is used once. Look at what an active attacker who never learns $k$ can still do (Figure~\ref{ch16:malleability-fig}). Suppose Eve knows -- or guesses -- that the plaintext at some position has a particular value, or wants to flip specific bits. She picks a bitmask $\Delta$ of the same length (with $1$s where she wants bits flipped) and replaces $c$ on the wire with $c' = c \xor \Delta$. Bob decrypts:
\[
m' = c' \xor k = (m \xor k \xor \Delta) \xor k = m \xor \Delta.
\]
The keystream cancels as it did for the legitimate plaintext, and Bob recovers $m \xor \Delta$ -- the original message with Eve's chosen bits flipped. Eve altered the plaintext in a controlled way without knowing a single bit of the key or the message. This property is called \emph{malleability}, and it is fatal whenever integrity matters.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Encryption scrambles the \emph{content} of a message; it says nothing about whether the message you received is the one that was sent. Picture a sealed envelope written in a code only Bob can read. Confidentiality means Eve cannot read what is inside. Nothing about the cipher stops an active attacker from opening the envelope, scribbling over it blindly, and resealing -- or swapping it for a different envelope altogether. Hiding the contents does nothing to detect changes to them. You need a second mechanism whose job is to reject the message when a bit changes.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Flipping a transfer amount under encryption.} A toy banking gateway sends instructions as fixed-format ASCII encrypted with a stream cipher: \texttt{PAY USER 0001 AMOUNT 0000100} (a $\$100$ transfer). Suppose the attacker knows the message format (by Kerckhoffs's principle, everything except the key is public) and knows that byte $21$ (counting from $0$) holds the leading digit of the seven-digit amount field. She does not know the keystream and cannot read the ciphertext, yet she can apply Figure~\ref{ch16:malleability-fig} with a mask $\Delta$ aimed at that one byte. In ASCII, the digit \texttt{0} is hexadecimal \texttt{0x30} and \texttt{9} is \texttt{0x39}; their XOR difference is $\texttt{0x30} \xor \texttt{0x39} = \texttt{0x09}$. She XORs ciphertext byte $21$ with \texttt{0x09}. When Bob decrypts, that plaintext byte flips from \texttt{0} to \texttt{9}: the instruction now reads \texttt{AMOUNT 9000100} ($\$9{,}000{,}100$). She has multiplied the transfer more than ninety-thousand-fold without breaking the encryption. She broke \emph{integrity}, which the cipher never promised to protect.
\end{attackbox}

This is not a quirk of stream ciphers. Cipher block chaining (CBC) mode (Chapter~13) has its own malleability: flipping a bit in ciphertext block $C_{i}$ flips the corresponding bit in the next plaintext block $P_{i+1}$ (while scrambling block $P_i$ into random-looking bytes), because CBC decryption XORs $C_i$ into the decrypted block that yields $P_{i+1}$. Counter (CTR) mode is as malleable as any stream cipher, because it \emph{is} a stream cipher. The lesson generalizes: \textbf{encryption schemes that achieve confidentiality say nothing about integrity, and most of them are actively malleable.} If you want Bob to reject a tampered ciphertext, you must add a mechanism that checks integrity. That mechanism is a MAC, and combining it safely with encryption is the subject of authenticated encryption.

\begin{trapbox}
``It's encrypted, so it can't be forged'' is one of the most expensive false beliefs in applied cryptography. Wired Equivalent Privacy (WEP), the original Wi-Fi standard, encrypted packets with RC4 and checked integrity with an unkeyed cyclic redundancy check (CRC32) -- an error-detecting code that is \emph{linear} over XOR, meaning flipping bits by a mask $\Delta$ changes the checksum by a known, key-independent offset $\operatorname{CRC}(\Delta)$ (up to a fixed constant). Because both RC4's keystream encryption and CRC32 distribute across XOR, an attacker could flip chosen ciphertext bits \emph{and} XOR the matching adjustment into the encrypted checksum, forging valid packets without knowing the key. Chapter~26 analyzes WEP's failure in full; the lesson here is that an unkeyed checksum is not a MAC, and encryption is not integrity.
\end{trapbox}

\section{What ``Secure'' Means Here: AE and AEAD}
\label{ch16:definitions}

To combine encryption and authentication we first need to say precisely what we want the combination to achieve. We already have a confidentiality goal from earlier chapters -- indistinguishability under chosen-plaintext attack (IND-CPA), the property that ciphertexts leak nothing about plaintexts even to an adversary who can get things encrypted. Integrity gets its own goal.

\begin{definition}[Integrity of ciphertexts, INT-CTXT]
\label{ch16:int-ctxt}
A symmetric encryption scheme with algorithms $(\Gen,\Enc,\Dec)$ -- key generation, encryption, and decryption -- has \emph{ciphertext integrity} (\emph{integrity of ciphertexts}, INT-CTXT) if no probabilistic polynomial-time ($\PPT$) adversary, allowed to query an encryption oracle $\Enc_k(\cdot)$ on chosen plaintexts under $k \gets \Gen(1^n)$, can produce a \emph{new} ciphertext (one never returned by $\Enc_k$) that $\Dec_k$ accepts rather than rejecting. Formally, the adversary wins if it outputs $c^{*}$ with $\Dec_k(c^{*}) \neq \bot$ where $c^{*}$ was never output by $\Enc_k$; the scheme is INT-CTXT secure if the winning probability is a negligible function $\negl(n)$ of the key length $n$.
\end{definition}

The $\bot$ symbol (``bottom'') is the decryption routine's way of saying ``reject -- this is not a valid ciphertext.'' Under INT-CTXT, the \emph{only} ciphertexts Bob accepts are ones Alice produced. An attacker can copy or drop them, yet cannot manufacture a fresh ciphertext that decrypts to anything other than $\bot$.

\begin{definition}[Authenticated encryption]
\label{ch16:ae-def}
A scheme is an \emph{authenticated encryption} (AE) scheme if it is both IND-CPA secure (confidentiality) and INT-CTXT secure (integrity).
\end{definition}

Why is this pairing the right target? Because together they imply security against \emph{indistinguishability under chosen-ciphertext attack} (IND-CCA), where the adversary may submit any ciphertexts other than the challenge ciphertext $c^*$ to a decryption oracle $\Dec_k(\cdot)$ and observe the decrypted results.

\begin{theorem}[AE implies CCA security]
\label{ch16:ae-implies-cca}
If a scheme is IND-CPA secure and INT-CTXT secure, then it is IND-CCA secure.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
In a chosen-ciphertext attack the adversary's extra power beyond IND-CPA is the decryption oracle $\Dec_k(\cdot)$. As Figure~\ref{fig:ch16-cca-neutralized} shows, INT-CTXT strips that oracle of any new information. Every query $c \neq c^*$ the adversary submits to $\Dec_k$ falls into one of two cases: either $c$ was returned by an earlier encryption query $\Enc_k(m_i)$ (so the adversary already knows $m_i$), or $c$ is a fresh ciphertext, which INT-CTXT forces $\Dec_k$ to reject with $\bot$ except with negligible probability. Because both answers are predictable without the secret key, the decryption oracle teaches the adversary nothing, reducing IND-CCA back to IND-CPA. Authenticated encryption therefore protects a channel against an attacker who can both read and \emph{inject} packets, as on any real network.
\end{whybox}

Real network messages also carry unencrypted metadata. A network packet has a header -- routing addresses, sequence numbers, version tags, packet types -- that must travel in the clear (\emph{cleartext}, meaning unencrypted so routers can read it) yet must still be \emph{authenticated} so an active attacker cannot alter it undetected. A Transport Layer Security (TLS) record -- a framed unit of application data -- carries a content type and a length; a packet carries a sequence number bound to the payload so it cannot be replayed or reordered. We need a scheme that encrypts the secret payload while authenticating both that payload \emph{and} additional cleartext data under a single tag.

\begin{definition}[AEAD]
\label{ch16:aead-def}
An \emph{authenticated encryption with associated data} (AEAD) scheme is a pair of algorithms
\[
\Enc_k(N, A, M) \to (C, T), \qquad \Dec_k(N, A, C, T) \to M \text{ or } \bot,
\]
where $k$ is the secret key, $N$ is a \emph{nonce} (a number used once per key), $A$ is the \emph{associated data} (authenticated, not encrypted), $M$ is the plaintext (authenticated and encrypted), $C$ is the encrypted payload, and $T$ is the authentication tag (libraries often return $C \concat T$ as a single byte string). Decryption returns $M$ only if $(N, A, C, T)$ matches a valid encryption output under $k$; if any bit of $N$, $A$, $C$, or $T$ was altered, it returns $\bot$. Security requires IND-CPA confidentiality of $M$ and INT-CTXT integrity covering $(N, A, C, T)$ jointly.
\end{definition}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Think of AEAD as sealing a shipping crate. The plaintext $M$ is the cargo, sealed inside where no one can see it. The associated data $A$ is the shipping label glued to the outside: everyone can read it (the courier needs the address), and the seal is engineered so that peeling off or editing the label breaks the seal. The nonce $N$ is the unique tracking number stamped on this specific crate so two crates can never be confused or one replayed as another. A single operation with one key and one tag gives all three guarantees: the cargo is hidden, the label cannot be altered, and the crate is unique.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{focusbox}
The nonce is not a key and not a secret. It can be a counter, it can travel in cleartext, and an attacker is allowed to see it. The single rule -- absolute for the schemes in this chapter -- is that a given key must \textbf{never} encrypt two different messages under the \textbf{same nonce}. ``Number used once'' means once per key, forever. For Galois/Counter Mode (GCM, Section~\ref{ch16:gcm}), security does not degrade gradually when this rule is broken: a single repeated nonce exposes the authentication key (Section~\ref{ch16:gcm-nonce-reuse}).
\end{focusbox}

\section{Three Ways to Glue, Two of Them Broken}
\label{ch16:composition}

Before examining concrete AEAD designs, consider the black-box question (\emph{generic composition}, treating the cipher and MAC as abstract primitives without relying on their internal algebra): given an IND-CPA encryption scheme and a secure MAC \emph{with independent keys} $k_e$ and $k_m$, how should Alice combine them on a plaintext $m$? Writing $c$ for ciphertext, $t$ for the MAC tag, and $\concat$ for bit-string concatenation, Figure~\ref{ch16:composition-fig} shows the three natural orderings.

\begin{description}
  \item[Encrypt-and-MAC (E\&M).] Compute $c = \Enc_{k_e}(m)$ and $t = \MAC_{k_m}(m)$, send $(c, t)$. The tag is computed over the \emph{plaintext}.
  \item[MAC-then-Encrypt (MtE).] Compute $t = \MAC_{k_m}(m)$, then $c = \Enc_{k_e}(m \concat t)$, send $c$. The tag is hidden inside the ciphertext.
  \item[Encrypt-then-MAC (EtM).] Compute $c = \Enc_{k_e}(m)$, then $t = \MAC_{k_m}(c)$, send $(c, t)$. The tag is computed over the \emph{ciphertext}.
\end{description}

\textbf{Encrypt-then-MAC is the one that always works.} If $\Enc$ is IND-CPA secure and $\MAC$ is a strongly unforgeable MAC, then EtM is an authenticated encryption scheme for any underlying cipher and MAC that meet their individual definitions. (\emph{Strongly} unforgeable strengthens Chapter~15's EUF-CMA rule so an attacker cannot even find a second valid tag $t' \ne t$ for an already-seen ciphertext $c$; otherwise $(c, t')$ would count as a fresh valid pair. Deterministic MACs such as hash-based MAC (HMAC), which assign a unique valid tag to each input, satisfy this automatically.) Because the MAC covers $c$, Bob checks whether $\MAC_{k_m}(c) = t$ \emph{before} decrypting anything (bottom row of Figure~\ref{fig:ch16-verify-vs-decrypt}). By unforgeability, only ciphertexts Alice sent pass the check, so INT-CTXT holds directly: any forged ciphertext fails verification and returns $\bot$ without reaching $\Dec_{k_e}$. Confidentiality holds because $t$ is computed from $c$ alone, which IND-CPA guarantees leaks nothing about $m$.

\textbf{Encrypt-and-MAC can leak plaintext.} The tag $t = \MAC_{k_m}(m)$ travels in cleartext and is a deterministic function of the plaintext. A standard MAC makes no promise to hide its input -- HMAC produces the identical tag whenever the plaintext repeats. If Alice sends the same plaintext twice, the two tags match, and an eavesdropper learns that the two messages are equal without breaking $\Enc_{k_e}$, violating IND-CPA. E\&M can also fail INT-CTXT when $c$ can be mauled into a distinct ciphertext $c'$ that still decrypts to the same $m$.

\textbf{MAC-then-Encrypt is secure only for \emph{some} ciphers, and the exceptions failed badly in practice.} Because the tag is encrypted inside $c$, Bob must \emph{decrypt first} to uncover $t$, and only then verify it (top row of Figure~\ref{fig:ch16-verify-vs-decrypt}). Processing attacker-controlled ciphertext before authenticating it is the structural flaw. If the decryption or unpadding step leaks any hint about \emph{why} a ciphertext was rejected -- a distinct error alert or a small response-time difference between bad padding and a bad MAC -- the attacker gains a decryption oracle. That leak drove the padding-oracle family of attacks (Serge Vaudenay's 2002 attack, Lucky Thirteen, and POODLE), which repeatedly broke MtE with CBC mode in Secure Sockets Layer (SSL) and TLS.

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{The padding oracle, in one paragraph.} TLS up through version 1.2 supported MAC-then-Encrypt with CBC-mode AES. To decrypt, Bob removes CBC padding and \emph{then} checks the MAC. If the padding is malformed he can reject before computing the MAC -- and if that rejection is observably different (a distinct alert code, or faster because HMAC was skipped), the attacker learns whether any submitted ciphertext decrypted to valid padding. As Chapter~13 showed, a valid-padding oracle suffices to decrypt an entire CBC block byte by byte without the key: the attacker flips bytes in the preceding ciphertext block, watches when the padding check passes, and recovers one byte of plaintext per $256$ trials on average. Lucky Thirteen (2013) revived this attack through a small HMAC \emph{timing} difference even after TLS implementations merged the error alerts. Encrypt-then-MAC eliminates that surface: a forged ciphertext fails the MAC check first and never reaches decryption.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Do not separate encryption and authentication when an AEAD is available.} Even Encrypt-then-MAC requires managing two independent keys, two primitives, constant-time tag comparison (comparing every byte in fixed time rather than stopping at the first mismatch, Chapter~15), and strict verify-before-decrypt ordering -- where a single slip reintroduces an oracle or a key-reuse bug. Modern libraries instead expose a single AEAD call -- such as the AES-GCM and ChaCha20-Poly1305 schemes built in the next sections -- that wires the composition internally and returns either verified plaintext or $\bot$. Do not assemble EtM by hand unless a protocol specification requires it and a cryptographer reviews the design.
\end{trapbox}

Mihir Bellare and Chanathip Namprempre settled the generic-composition question in 2000, alongside Hugo Krawczyk's analysis the same year (``The order of encryption and authentication for protecting communications''). Their result -- that Encrypt-then-MAC is the generically sound choice -- appeared just as early internet protocols were locking in all three options: SSL/TLS chose MAC-then-Encrypt, Internet Protocol Security (IPsec) chose Encrypt-then-MAC, and Secure Shell (SSH) chose Encrypt-and-MAC. IPsec, the one that picked Encrypt-then-MAC, was the one spared a decade of padding-oracle vulnerabilities.

\section{AES-GCM: Counter Mode Meets a Polynomial Hash}
\label{ch16:gcm}

AES in Galois/Counter Mode (AES-GCM) secures most TLS connections and serves as the default AEAD in IPsec, SSH, and disk encryption. Both of its stages parallelize -- separate blocks can be processed simultaneously without waiting on prior blocks -- and modern CPUs accelerate both halves in hardware through \texttt{AES-NI} (dedicated AES round instructions) and \texttt{PCLMULQDQ} (carry-less polynomial multiplication). Figure~\ref{ch16:gcm-fig} shows its two stages: CTR mode for confidentiality, feeding into a keyed polynomial hash called GHASH for integrity, all derived from a single AES key $k$.

\subsection{The confidentiality half: CTR mode}
\label{ch16:gcm-ctr}

Counter mode turns AES into a stream cipher. Given a 96-bit nonce $N$, GCM builds the initial 128-bit counter block $J_0 = N \concat 0^{31}1$ ($N$ followed by $31$ zero bits and a $1$, i.e., the 32-bit big-endian integer $1$). Plaintext blocks $P_1, P_2, \dots$ (each 128 bits, with the final block truncated if needed) are encrypted using counters $J_0 + 1, J_0 + 2, \dots$, incrementing the low 32 bits modulo $2^{32}$:
\[
C_i = P_i \xor \operatorname{AES}_k(J_0 + i).
\]
Counter block $J_0$ itself is held back to mask the authentication tag rather than encrypt plaintext. CTR mode is IND-CPA secure so long as no counter block repeats under $k$, which is why $N$ must never repeat: reusing $N$ repeats $J_0$ and every $J_0 + i$, reusing the entire keystream.

\subsection{The integrity half: GHASH over \texorpdfstring{$\mathbb{F}_{2^{128}}$}{F\_\{2\textasciicircum 128\}}}
\label{ch16:gcm-ghash}

GHASH belongs to a \emph{universal hash family} -- a keyed collection of fast algebraic functions (not unkeyed cryptographic hashes on their own) designed so any two distinct messages have only a negligible probability of colliding when the key is secret and random. Every 128-bit block is viewed as an element of the finite field $\F_{2^{128}}$ of polynomials over $\F_2$ modulo the irreducible polynomial $x^{128} + x^7 + x^2 + x + 1$ (Chapter~6). Field addition is bitwise XOR; field multiplication is carry-less polynomial multiplication (combining partial products with XOR instead of integer carries) reduced modulo that polynomial.

Let $H = \operatorname{AES}_k(0^{128})$ be the \emph{hash subkey}, computed by applying AES to the block of $128$ zero bits. GHASH pads $A$ and $C$ separately with zero bits to 128-bit boundaries, appends a final 128-bit block $\langle\text{len}\rangle = \operatorname{len}(A) \concat \operatorname{len}(C)$ storing their 64-bit bit-lengths, and splits the resulting stream into $m$ blocks $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_m$. It then evaluates the polynomial
\begin{align*}
\operatorname{GHASH}_H(X_1,\dots,X_m)
  &= X_1 H^{m} \xor X_2 H^{m-1} \xor \cdots \xor X_{m-1} H^{2} \xor X_m H \\
  &= \bigl(\cdots\bigl((X_1 H \xor X_2)H \xor X_3\bigr)H \cdots \xor X_m\bigr)H
\end{align*}
in $\F_{2^{128}}$. The nested second line is \emph{Horner's rule}: factoring out $H$ step by step lets an implementation stream through the data one block at a time -- XOR the next block $X_i$ into the running 128-bit accumulator and multiply by $H$ -- without buffering the message or precomputing powers of $H$. Writing $\operatorname{GHASH}_H(A, C)$ for this evaluation over the formatted blocks of $A$, $C$, and $\langle\text{len}\rangle$, the authentication tag is the hash masked by the reserved keystream block at $J_0$:
\[
T = \operatorname{GHASH}_H(A, C) \xor \operatorname{AES}_k(J_0).
\]

\begin{whybox}
Why does evaluating a polynomial at a secret point detect tampering? GHASH is \emph{$\eps$-almost-universal}: for any two distinct formatted inputs, the probability over a uniform hash subkey $H \in \F_{2^{128}}$ that they yield the same output is at most $m / 2^{128}$ for $m$-block inputs. Suppose an attacker alters the formatted blocks from $X = (X_1,\dots,X_m)$ to $X' = (X_1',\dots,X_m') \neq X$ (including the length block keeps differently sized messages distinct). Because subtraction and addition in $\F_{2^{128}}$ are both XOR, the two GHASH values differ by
\[
(X_1 \xor X_1')H^m \xor (X_2 \xor X_2')H^{m-1} \xor \cdots \xor (X_m \xor X_m')H,
\]
a nonzero polynomial in $H$ of degree at most $m$. Over any field, a nonzero degree-$m$ polynomial has at most $m$ roots, so at most $m$ of the $2^{128}$ values of $H$ make the difference vanish. Because $H = \operatorname{AES}_k(0^{128})$ is pseudorandom and hidden from the attacker, a blind modification hits a root with probability at most $m / 2^{128}$ (plus the negligible PRP distinguishing gap of AES). Finally, XORing with $\operatorname{AES}_k(J_0)$ encrypts the GHASH output with a fresh per-nonce mask so observed tags leak nothing about $H$.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{A worked GHASH in a toy field}
\label{ch16:gcm-toy}

The real field $\F_{2^{128}}$ is too large to trace by hand, so we shrink to $\F_{2^4}$, the field of 4-bit nibbles modulo the irreducible polynomial $x^4 + x + 1$. Write elements in hexadecimal as $\texttt{0}$--$\texttt{f}$, where each nibble represents a polynomial over $\F_2$: for example, $\texttt{b} = 1011_2 = x^3 + x + 1$. Addition is bitwise XOR; multiplication is carry-less polynomial multiplication followed by reduction using $x^4 = x + 1$ (since $x^4 + x + 1 = 0$ in $\F_2[x]/(x^4+x+1)$).

Take hash subkey $H = \texttt{2} = x$ and two blocks $X_1 = \texttt{3} = x + 1$ and $X_2 = \texttt{1} = 1$, so we want
\[
\operatorname{GHASH}_H(X_1, X_2) = X_1 H^2 \xor X_2 H = \bigl((X_1 H) \xor X_2\bigr)H.
\]
With $H = x$ and $H^2 = x^2 = \texttt{4}$, evaluate the two terms and combine them:
\begin{align*}
X_1 H^2 &= \texttt{3} \cdot \texttt{4} = (x+1)x^2 = x^3 + x^2 = \texttt{c} && \text{(degree $3 < 4$, no reduction)}, \\
X_2 H   &= \texttt{1} \cdot \texttt{2} = 1 \cdot x = x = \texttt{2}, \\
\operatorname{GHASH}_x(\texttt{3},\texttt{1}) &= \texttt{c} \xor \texttt{2} = 1100_2 \xor 0010_2 = 1110_2 = \texttt{e}.
\end{align*}

To see how tampering shifts the hash (Figure~\ref{fig:ch16-ghash-trace}), suppose an attacker flips $X_2$ from $\texttt{1}$ to $\texttt{1}\xor\texttt{1}=\texttt{0}$, a difference of $\Delta = \texttt{1}$ in the final block. The hash changes by $\Delta \cdot H = \texttt{1}\cdot\texttt{2} = \texttt{2}$, moving from $\texttt{e}$ to $\texttt{e}\xor\texttt{2}=\texttt{c}$. Leaving the tag unchanged requires $\Delta \cdot H = 0$, hence $H = 0$, whereas $H = x \neq 0$. Forcing any chosen tag difference requires knowing $H$, which stays hidden behind AES. Over $\F_{2^{128}}$, the same root bound caps a blind collision at $m / 2^{128}$.

\begin{labbox}
\textbf{Reduction practice.} In $\F_{2^4}$ with $x^4 = x+1$, compute powers of $H=x$ starting at $H^4$:
\begin{align*}
H^4 &= x^4 = x + 1 = \texttt{3}, \\
H^5 &= x \cdot \texttt{3} = x^2 + x = \texttt{6}, \\
H^6 &= x \cdot \texttt{6} = x^3 + x^2 = \texttt{c}, \\
H^7 &= x \cdot \texttt{c} = x^4 + x^3 = (x+1) + x^3 = x^3 + x + 1 = \texttt{b}.
\end{align*}
The powers of $x$ cycle through all $15$ nonzero elements -- $x$ generates the multiplicative group $\F_{2^4}^{*}$ of order $15$. Verify $H^{15} = \texttt{1}$ by extending the table. GHASH relies on this same field arithmetic to evaluate its polynomial.
\end{labbox}

\begin{implementbox}
The listing below implements GHASH and a GCM-style tag over $\F_{2^4}$ so you can run the algebra directly. A 4-bit field gives \emph{zero} security and the loop is not constant-time; use this code only for study.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
# Toy GHASH in GF(2^4), modulus x^4 + x + 1.  LEARNING ONLY -- not secure.
def gf_mul(a, b):
    """Carryless multiply of two nibbles, reduced mod x^4 + x + 1."""
    p = 0
    for _ in range(4):
        if b & 1:
            p ^= a
        b >>= 1
        carry = a & 0x8          # bit x^3; shifting it makes x^4
        a = (a << 1) & 0xF
        if carry:
            a ^= 0b0011          # x^4 = x + 1  ->  XOR with 0011
    return p

def ghash(H, blocks):
    """Horner evaluation: (((X1*H) ^ X2)*H ^ X3)*H ... all in GF(2^4)."""
    y = 0
    for X in blocks:
        y = gf_mul(y ^ X, H)
    return y

def gcm_tag(H, mask, A_blocks, C_blocks, lenblock):
    # Authenticate associated data, ciphertext, and a length block, then mask.
    data = A_blocks + C_blocks + [lenblock]
    return ghash(H, data) ^ mask

H = 0x2                                # hash key = x  (= AES_k(0) in the real scheme)
print(hex(ghash(H, [0x3, 0x1])))       # -> 0xe, matching the hand computation

# Flipping the last block changes the tag predictably by Delta*H:
print(hex(ghash(H, [0x3, 0x0])))       # -> 0xc  ( = 0xe XOR (0x1 * H) )
\end{lstlisting}
The Horner form \texttt{y = (y \^{} X)*H} is the standard streaming way to evaluate the GHASH polynomial one block at a time; expand it for two blocks and you recover $X_1 H^2 \xor X_2 H$ exactly.
\end{implementbox}

\section{The Nonce-Reuse Catastrophe in GCM}
\label{ch16:gcm-nonce-reuse}

GCM has one weak point, and it is the most important thing to know when deploying it. Reuse a nonce -- encrypt two different messages with the same key and the same $N$ -- and you lose the confidentiality of \emph{both} messages; worse, you leak the hash key $H$, which lets the attacker forge \emph{arbitrary} messages from then on. One repeated nonce can destroy the integrity of every message under that key.

\subsection{Why a repeat is so much worse than for a plain stream cipher}
\label{ch16:gcm-why-worse}

You already know half of the damage. CTR mode is a stream cipher; reusing the nonce reuses the keystream blocks $\operatorname{AES}_k(J_0+i)$. If $C = P \xor S$ and $C' = P' \xor S$ with the same keystream $S$, then $C \xor C' = P \xor P'$ -- the XOR of two plaintexts, with the keystream cancelled, the classic two-time-pad break that leaks the messages. This is bad, but no worse than any other stream-cipher misuse.

The second half is specific to GCM (Figure~\ref{ch16:fig-nonce-catastrophe}): the attacker recovers the \emph{hash subkey} $H$. The two tags are
\[
T = \operatorname{GHASH}_H(A, C) \xor \operatorname{AES}_k(J_0), \qquad
T' = \operatorname{GHASH}_H(A', C') \xor \operatorname{AES}_k(J_0),
\]
and because the nonce repeated, $J_0$ repeated, so the masking block $L = \operatorname{AES}_k(J_0)$ is \emph{identical} in both. XOR the tags and $L$ cancels:
\[
T \xor T' = \operatorname{GHASH}_H(A, C) \xor \operatorname{GHASH}_H(A', C').
\]
The right-hand side is a polynomial in the unknown $H$ whose coefficients are the known block-by-block XOR differences of the two formatted messages, and its value equals the known XOR $T \xor T'$. Bringing $T \xor T'$ to the other side gives a polynomial equation $\Phi(H) = 0$ over $\F_{2^{128}}$, whose roots can be found in milliseconds using standard finite-field polynomial-factoring algorithms. A degree-$m$ polynomial has at most $m$ roots, giving a short list of candidate values for $H$; a third message under the same nonce or one forgery trial pins down $H$ uniquely.

Once $H$ is known, any single valid message $(A, C, T)$ observed under \emph{any} nonce $N^*$ reveals that nonce's mask $L^* = T \xor \operatorname{GHASH}_H(A, C)$. Because GHASH is linear over XOR in its block inputs, the attacker can then forge a valid tag for any chosen $(A^*, C^*)$ under $N^*$:
\[
T^* = T \xor \operatorname{GHASH}_H(A, C) \xor \operatorname{GHASH}_H(A^*, C^*).
\]
Integrity is lost across the lifetime of the key $k$, not only for the two messages that shared a nonce.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Recovering $H$ from a reused nonce, in the toy field.} Work in $\F_{2^4}$ modulo $x^4+x+1$ with unknown subkey $H$. Suppose two one-block ciphertexts are sent under the same nonce (so the mask $L = \operatorname{AES}_k(J_0)$ repeats), with no associated data and identical length block $\ell$. Their tags are
\[
T = (C_1 H^2 \xor \ell H) \xor L, \qquad T' = (C_1' H^2 \xor \ell H) \xor L.
\]
XORing cancels both $L$ and $\ell H$, leaving $T \xor T' = (C_1 \xor C_1')\,H^2$. Suppose the attacker observes $C_1 = \texttt{5}$, $C_1' = \texttt{6}$, and $T \xor T' = \texttt{c}$. Then $\texttt{5} \xor \texttt{6} = \texttt{3} = x + 1$, giving $\texttt{3}\,H^2 = \texttt{c}$. Multiply both sides by $\texttt{3}^{-1} = \texttt{e} = x^3 + x^2 + x$ (using $x^4 = x+1$, so $x^5 = x^2+x$ and $x^6 = x^3+x^2$):
\begin{align*}
\texttt{3} \cdot \texttt{e} &= (x+1)(x^3 + x^2 + x) = x^4 + x = (x+1) + x = 1 = \texttt{1} \quad\text{($\texttt{e} = \texttt{3}^{-1}$)}, \\
H^2 &= \texttt{c} \cdot \texttt{e} = (x^3 + x^2)(x^3 + x^2 + x) = x^6 + x^3 = (x^3 + x^2) + x^3 = x^2 = \texttt{4}, \\
H   &= x = \texttt{2} \quad\text{(unique square root in $\F_{2^4}$)}.
\end{align*}
(In characteristic~$2$, $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + b^2$ makes squaring a bijection on $\F_{2^4}$, so every element has a unique square root.) Two ciphertexts and two tags exposed $H$. Over $\F_{2^{128}}$ the degree and field size grow, while the algebra is identical.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The 96-bit nonce is small, and random nonces collide.} Because GCM nonces are 96 bits, choosing them \emph{uniformly at random} per message under one key runs into the birthday bound (Figure~\ref{fig:ch16-nonce-birthday}): after $q$ messages, the probability of at least one repeated nonce is approximately $q^2 / 2^{97}$, reaching $39\%$ around $q = 2^{48}$ -- and \emph{one} collision exposes $H$. For random 96-bit nonces, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) therefore caps usage at $2^{32}$ messages per key, keeping collision probability below $2^{-32}$. Whenever state allows, use a deterministic \textbf{counter} nonce (message sequence number, never repeating) instead of random draws, rotating keys periodically (for instance every $2^{32}$ messages). In stateless or distributed systems where no shared counter exists, replace plain GCM with a nonce-misuse-resistant mode such as AES-GCM-SIV (\emph{Synthetic Initialization Vector}), where an accidental repeat reveals only whether two plaintexts were identical and never leaks $H$.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
The $2^{48}$-message threshold in Figure~\ref{fig:ch16-nonce-birthday} is the birthday bound from Chapter~7 and the probability companion: drawing uniformly from $N = 2^{96}$ values produces a first repeat after roughly $\sqrt{N} = 2^{48}$ draws. The same $\sqrt{N}$ law governs hash collisions (Chapter~14) and meet-in-the-middle attacks on double encryption (Chapter~11). Here it sets a hard ceiling on random-nonce GCM and explains why ``pick a random 96-bit nonce'' is not a substitute for guaranteeing uniqueness.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{historybox}
David McGrew and John Viega designed GCM in 2004, building on the authenticated-encryption definitions of Bellare, Phillip Rogaway, and Krawczyk around 2000, and NIST standardized it in Special Publication 800-38D (2007). GCM meets its stated security bound whenever nonces are unique, yet real deployments have repeatedly broken that assumption. A 2016 internet-wide scan found hundreds of HTTP-over-TLS (HTTPS) servers reusing GCM nonces due to faulty random-number generators, rolled-back virtual-machine (VM) snapshots, or buggy counter code -- allowing active attackers to decrypt and forge sessions on each affected server. Subsequent designs, including the Competition for Authenticated Encryption: Security, Applicability, and Robustness (CAESAR, 2014--2019), AES-GCM-SIV (RFC~8452), and the internal sequence-number nonces of TLS~1.3, treat nonce uniqueness as something the protocol or library must enforce rather than leave to callers.
\end{historybox}

\section{ChaCha20-Poly1305: The Software-Friendly AEAD}
\label{ch16:chacha-poly}

AES-GCM is fastest when the CPU provides AES hardware instructions. On processors without them -- older mobile chips, microcontrollers, or any platform where table-based software AES is slow and leaks secret bits through CPU cache-timing differences (because secret bytes index into memory tables whose cache hits and misses take measurably different time) -- you want an AEAD built from operations that run fast and in constant time in plain software. That cipher is ChaCha20-Poly1305, designed by Daniel J.\ Bernstein and standardized for TLS in RFC~8439. Google deployed it across Android and Chrome because it runs fast and resists timing side channels without special instructions.

Figure~\ref{ch16:chacha-fig} shows its structure, which parallels GCM: a stream cipher for confidentiality paired with a one-time universal-hash MAC over the ciphertext.

\textbf{ChaCha20} (Chapter~10) is a stream cipher built on a 512-bit state (sixteen 32-bit words holding fixed constants, a 256-bit key, a 32-bit block counter, and a 96-bit nonce) mixed by twenty rounds of Add--Rotate--XOR (ARX) word operations. Because it uses no secret-indexed lookup tables, every CPU register operation takes the same number of cycles regardless of the data. Adding the initial state back to the mixed state produces a 64-byte keystream block, which is XORed onto the plaintext as in CTR mode.

\textbf{Poly1305} is the MAC, a polynomial-evaluation hash like GHASH that works over the prime field $\F_p$ with $p = 2^{130} - 5$ rather than a binary field. The authenticated byte string $D$ (padded associated data $A$, padded ciphertext $C$, and their two 64-bit byte-lengths) is split into 16-byte chunks, and each chunk is read as a little-endian integer with a $1$ bit appended just above its top byte ($+2^{128}$ for a full 16-byte chunk) to form nonzero field elements $c_1, \dots, c_q$ -- preventing trailing zero bytes from vanishing as leading zero polynomial coefficients. Given a secret 128-bit evaluation point $r$ and a secret 128-bit additive mask $s$ (the \emph{blinder}), the tag is
\[
\operatorname{Poly1305}_{r,s}(D) = \Bigl( \bigl( c_1 r^{q} + c_2 r^{q-1} + \cdots + c_q r \bigr) \bmod p \Bigr) + s \pmod{2^{128}}.
\]
As in GHASH, the message chunks act as coefficients of a polynomial evaluated at a secret point, using prime-field integer arithmetic suited to constant-time software, followed by addition of $s$ modulo $2^{128}$. The pair $(r, s)$ is \emph{derived freshly for each packet} from ChaCha20 itself: running ChaCha20 with block counter $0$ on $(k, N)$ yields $64$ pseudorandom bytes, whose first $32$ bytes supply $(r, s)$ (with $22$ fixed bits of $r$ cleared to zero -- \emph{clamped} -- so intermediate limb products cannot overflow in 32- and 64-bit integer registers, leaving $106$ secret bits in $r$), while counters $1, 2, \dots$ generate the keystream that encrypts $M$. Because $(r, s)$ is used for one nonce only and Poly1305 authenticates $A$ and $C$ rather than $M$, the construction is pure Encrypt-then-MAC.

\begin{whybox}
Poly1305 follows the same polynomial root argument as GHASH. Modifying a $q$-chunk input changes the polynomial mod $2^{130}-5$ by a nonzero polynomial of degree at most $q$ in $r$. Accounting for the $2^{106}$ clamped choices of $r$ and the final reduction from $\F_{2^{130}-5}$ down to $128$ bits, Daniel Bernstein proved that any single blind forgery attempt succeeds with probability at most $8q / 2^{106} = q / 2^{103}$ (below $2^{-83}$ even for a $16\,\text{MB}$ message). Adding the one-time blinder $s \bmod 2^{128}$ hides the polynomial output so valid tags leak nothing about $r$. Confidentiality comes from ChaCha20's IND-CPA security, and because Poly1305 authenticates the ciphertext before decryption, Theorem~\ref{ch16:ae-implies-cca} gives full IND-CCA security.
\end{whybox}

\begin{labbox}
\textbf{A worked Poly1305-style tag in a toy prime field.} Shrink the modulus to $p = 17$ and treat each chunk as a single nonzero residue modulo $17$ (using $\bmod\,17$ for the final blinder addition as well, where full Poly1305 reduces modulo $2^{130}-5$ and then adds $s \bmod 2^{128}$). Take secret evaluation point $r = 5$ and blinder $s = 9$, and authenticate two chunks $c_1 = 3$ and $c_2 = 7$:
\begin{align*}
c_1 r^2 + c_2 r &= 3 \cdot 25 + 7 \cdot 5 = 75 + 35 = 110 \equiv 110 - 6 \cdot 17 = 8 \pmod{17}, \\
T &= (8 + 9) \bmod 17 = 0.
\end{align*}
Now test tampering: shift $c_2$ from $7$ to $7 + \Delta$. The polynomial shifts by $\Delta \cdot r \equiv 5\Delta \pmod{17}$, which is nonzero for every $\Delta \not\equiv 0 \pmod{17}$ because $17$ is prime (no zero divisors) and $r \not\equiv 0$. Without $r$ an attacker cannot predict that shift, and $s$ masks the polynomial value so observed tags hide $r$. Over $\F_{2^{130}-5}$, the same root bound caps a blind $q$-chunk forgery at $8q / 2^{106}$.
\end{labbox}

\begin{focusbox}
ChaCha20-Poly1305 is \emph{not} nonce-misuse resistant: reusing a nonce repeats the ChaCha20 keystream (leaking $M \xor M'$) \emph{and} reuses the one-time pair $(r,s)$, letting an attacker solve for $r$ and forge messages under that repeated nonce. The blast radius is narrower than GCM's, however. GCM reuses a single subkey $H = \operatorname{AES}_k(0^{128})$ across every nonce, so one nonce collision compromises integrity for all past and future nonces under $k$. ChaCha20-Poly1305 derives an independent $(r,s)$ per nonce, confining forgery to the specific nonce that repeated. Even so, the rule remains unchanged: never repeat a nonce under a key. ChaCha20-Poly1305's advantage over GCM is \emph{software safety}: it runs fast and in constant time without dedicated CPU instructions, leaving fewer ways to introduce a timing leak.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{implementbox}
In production software, use an AEAD by calling a vetted library rather than writing your own. The listing below shows the interface in the Python Cryptographic Authority's \texttt{cryptography} package (\texttt{pyca/cryptography}). The library enforces verify-before-decrypt: any tampered ciphertext raises an exception instead of returning unauthenticated bytes.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
import os
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.ciphers.aead import ChaCha20Poly1305, AESGCM

key   = ChaCha20Poly1305.generate_key()      # 256-bit key
aead  = ChaCha20Poly1305(key)

nonce = os.urandom(12)                        # 96-bit nonce: unique per key
ad    = b"v1|type=transfer|hdr"              # associated data: authenticated, not hidden
msg   = b"PAY USER 0001 AMOUNT 0000100"

ct = aead.encrypt(nonce, msg, ad)            # ciphertext WITH appended 16-byte tag
pt = aead.decrypt(nonce, ct, ad)             # returns msg, or raises InvalidTag
assert pt == msg

# Integrity in action: flip byte 21 (the stream-cipher attack from Section 16.1)
# and decryption raises an exception instead of returning altered plaintext.
tampered = bytearray(ct); tampered[21] ^= 0x09
try:
    aead.decrypt(nonce, bytes(tampered), ad)
    print("BUG: forgery accepted")
except Exception:
    print("rejected forged ciphertext")      # <-- this branch runs

# AES-GCM is a drop-in with the same interface and the same nonce rule:
gkey = AESGCM.generate_key(bit_length=256)
AESGCM(gkey).encrypt(os.urandom(12), msg, ad)
\end{lstlisting}
The bit-flipping attack from Section~\ref{ch16:why-both} is the line \texttt{tampered[21] \^{}= 0x09}; under AEAD, \texttt{decrypt} detects the tag mismatch and raises an exception before returning a single byte of plaintext.
\end{implementbox}

\section{Choosing and Using AEAD in the Real World}
\label{ch16:real-world}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Where AEAD lives.} TLS~1.3 (2018) deleted every non-AEAD cipher suite: its only record-protection algorithms are AES-GCM, ChaCha20-Poly1305, and AES in Counter with CBC-MAC mode (AES-CCM), retiring the MAC-then-Encrypt CBC suites altogether. Each TLS~1.3 record places its cleartext header (content type, protocol version, and length) into the associated data $A$ and derives its 96-bit nonce from a per-connection record counter so nonces cannot collide. The same primitives protect nearly every modern protocol: WPA2 and WPA3 Wi-Fi use AES-CCM and AES-GCM (replacing WEP's linear CRC32); SSH offers \texttt{aes128-gcm@openssh.com} and \texttt{chacha20-poly1305@openssh.com}; IPsec uses AES-GCM; Signal and the WireGuard VPN use ChaCha20-Poly1305; and QUIC (the encrypted transport protocol underlying HTTP/3) protects every packet with an AEAD keyed to the packet number.
\end{applicationbox}

A short decision guide for the practitioner:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Default to an AEAD, never raw encryption.} If your library offers \texttt{encrypt(key, nonce, plaintext, associated\_data)}, use it. Manually composing a cipher and a MAC invites subtle ordering, padding, and key-separation bugs.
  \item \textbf{AES-GCM} when AES hardware is present (nearly all modern servers, desktops, and recent phones via \texttt{AES-NI} or ARM Cryptography Extensions). It is the fastest choice on hardware-accelerated CPUs and the most widely supported.
  \item \textbf{ChaCha20-Poly1305} when AES hardware is unavailable or you need guaranteed constant-time software -- embedded chips, portable libraries, or platforms where cache-timing leaks are a concern.
  \item \textbf{Nonces: enforce uniqueness by construction.} Prefer a monotonic counter over random draws. Where shared counters are impractical across distributed workers, use a nonce-misuse-resistant AEAD (such as AES-GCM-SIV) or an extended-nonce scheme (such as XChaCha20-Poly1305, which uses a 192-bit nonce) rather than risking the $2^{48}$ birthday bound.
  \item \textbf{Put every unencrypted context field into the associated data} -- protocol versions, sequence numbers, sender and recipient identifiers, packet types. Any cleartext field whose alteration would change how the recipient interprets the message belongs in $A$.
  \item \textbf{Never act on plaintext before the tag verifies.} Vetted AEAD libraries enforce this in memory by returning $\bot$ or raising an exception. Streaming a huge file requires breaking it into authenticated chunks with sequence-numbered nonces and an end-of-stream marker -- never processing unverified partial output.
\end{itemize}

\begin{aestheticbox}
Early cryptographic protocols treated confidentiality and integrity as separate add-ons, wired ciphers and MACs together ad hoc, and paid with a decade of padding oracles and forged wireless frames. The lasting fix came from a tighter \emph{abstraction} rather than a stronger block cipher: bundle encryption, authentication, nonce handling, and header binding behind one call whose contract rejects tampered inputs before decryption. Designing interfaces so the default call path is also the safe path is a central engineering lesson of modern cryptography.
\end{aestheticbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Every security guarantee in authenticated encryption rests on an unstated premise: that keys are chosen uniformly at random and nonces never repeat. If those numbers come from a broken pseudorandom generator or a low-entropy seed, GCM collapses regardless of how strong AES is. Chapter~17 explores where cryptographic keys and unpredictable nonces come from: physical entropy and cryptographically secure pseudorandom number generators (CSPRNGs).
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{exercise}
A message is encrypted with a stream cipher as $c = m \xor k$. You do not know $k$ or $m$, but you know $m$ is ASCII and that byte 5 of the plaintext is the digit \texttt{1} (\texttt{0x31}), and you want Bob to decrypt it as \texttt{8} (\texttt{0x38}). \end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
State the three generic compositions of an encryption scheme and a MAC. For each of Encrypt-and-MAC and MAC-then-Encrypt, give a concrete reason it can fail to be a secure authenticated-encryption scheme (one confidentiality failure, one integrity/oracle failure). Then prove informally that Encrypt-then-MAC achieves ciphertext integrity, assuming the MAC is strongly unforgeable.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Prove Theorem~\ref{ch16:ae-implies-cca} in detail: show that IND-CPA plus INT-CTXT implies IND-CCA. Structure the argument as a reduction -- assume a CCA adversary with non-negligible advantage and build either an INT-CTXT forger or an IND-CPA distinguisher. Where exactly does the decryption oracle become useless?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Work in $\F_{2^4}$ with modulus $x^4 + x + 1$ (so $x^4 = x+1$). Let $H = \texttt{3}$. Compute $\operatorname{GHASH}_H$ of the two-block input $(X_1, X_2) = (\texttt{6}, \texttt{a})$, i.e. evaluate $X_1 H^2 \xor X_2 H$, showing the reduction step.
