\chapter{Message Authentication Codes and HMAC}

In Chapter 14, hash functions gave us a tamper-evident fingerprint for any file, but an unkeyed hash cannot prove \emph{who} computed it: anyone can alter a message and hash the new version. Up to now we have focused on keeping Eve from \emph{reading} a message, yet confidentiality is only half of what security requires in practice.

Picture Alice wiring money. She sends her bank the message
\[
\texttt{"Pay Bob \$100"}.
\]
Suppose Eve cannot read it -- it is encrypted under a strong cipher. Has Eve been defeated? Not at all. If Eve can \emph{change} the ciphertext as it flies past, she may be able to turn it into an encryption of \texttt{"Pay Eve \$9999"}, or replay last week's \texttt{"Pay Eve \$100"} ten thousand times. Encryption said nothing about whether the message arrived intact, or whether Alice sent it. That second guarantee -- the message arrived unchanged and came from the claimed sender -- is \emph{integrity} and \emph{authenticity}. The tool that delivers it is the \emph{message authentication code}, or MAC.

How can two parties who share a secret key attach a short, unforgeable tag to a message of any length, and how should that tag be combined with encryption?

\chapterline

\section{Confidentiality Is Not Integrity}
\label{ch15:conf-vs-integrity}

Three distinct security goals often get conflated:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Confidentiality} hides the \emph{content}. After seeing the ciphertext, Eve learns nothing useful about the plaintext. This is what encryption gives you.
  \item \textbf{Integrity} guarantees the message was not \emph{altered} in transit. If even one bit flips, the receiver notices and rejects.
  \item \textbf{Authenticity} guarantees the message came from who it claims to come from -- someone holding the secret key.
\end{itemize}

In the symmetric-key world, integrity and authenticity come bundled together: a MAC delivers both. If a tag verifies under the shared key, the receiver knows simultaneously that the bits are unchanged and that whoever produced them held the key. (In the public-key world these split apart again into digital signatures, Chapter~22.)

\begin{intuitionbox}
A MAC is a \emph{tamper-evident seal} for digital data. Think of a wax seal on a letter, or the foil under a bottle cap. Anyone can see the message; only the holder of the secret signet ring can stamp a valid seal; and a stamp made for one letter cannot be peeled off and stuck onto another. The MAC is that seal, computed with a key only Alice and Bob share. Eve can rewrite the letter all she likes, but she cannot forge the stamp, so Bob throws her version away.
\end{intuitionbox}

\emph{Encryption alone provides no integrity.} A ciphertext may look like random noise to an eavesdropper, yet an active attacker on the wire can still modify it in predictable ways.

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Malleability of stream ciphers.} A stream cipher (Chapter~10) encrypts by bitwise XOR: $c = m \xor k$, where $k$ is the pseudorandom keystream. Suppose Alice encrypts the 11-byte ASCII message \texttt{"Pay Eve \$10"} (indexing bytes $1$ through $11$, so $m_i, k_i, c_i$ denote the $i$-th plaintext, keystream, and ciphertext bytes). Eve cannot read $k$, yet she \emph{knows the format} of these wire-transfer messages and wants to raise the amount.

The last two plaintext bytes are $m_{10} = \texttt{'1'}$ (\texttt{0x31}) and $m_{11} = \texttt{'0'}$ (\texttt{0x30}). Eve wants \texttt{'9'} (\texttt{0x39}) in both positions. Without touching the key, she XORs into the last two ciphertext bytes the XOR difference between the old digits and the new ones:
\[
  \Delta = \texttt{0x31} \xor \texttt{0x39} = \texttt{0x08}, \qquad
  \Delta' = \texttt{0x30} \xor \texttt{0x39} = \texttt{0x09}.
\]
She replaces $c_{10}$ with $c_{10} \xor \texttt{0x08}$ and $c_{11}$ with $c_{11} \xor \texttt{0x09}$. When Bob decrypts byte~$10$:
\begin{align*}
  (c_{10} \xor \texttt{0x08}) \xor k_{10}
    &= (m_{10} \xor k_{10} \xor \texttt{0x08}) \xor k_{10} && \text{(since $c_{10} = m_{10} \xor k_{10}$)} \\
    &= m_{10} \xor \texttt{0x08} && \text{($k_{10} \xor k_{10} = 0$ cancels)} \\
    &= \texttt{'1'} \xor \texttt{0x08} = \texttt{'9'}.
\end{align*}
Byte~$11$ likewise decrypts to $\texttt{'0'} \xor \texttt{0x09} = \texttt{'9'}$. The keystream cancels regardless of its value: Eve never learned a single keystream byte and never decrypted anything, yet raised the payment from \$10 to \$99. Confidentiality held, while integrity failed completely.
\end{attackbox}

\section{What a MAC Is, Formally}
\label{ch15:mac-syntax}

\begin{definition}[Message authentication code]
\label{ch15:def-mac}
A message authentication code (MAC) is a triple of algorithms $(\Gen,\MAC,\Vrfy)$ for key generation, tag computation, and verification:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\Gen(1^n)$ takes the security parameter $n$ (written in unary as $1^n$, typically the key length in bits) and outputs a secret key $k$, usually sampled uniformly at random as $k\getsr\{0,1\}^n$.
  \item $\MAC_k(m)$ takes the key $k$ and a message $m\in\{0,1\}^*$ (a bit string of any finite length) and outputs a fixed-length \emph{tag} $t$. We write $t \gets \MAC_k(m)$.
  \item $\Vrfy_k(m,t)$ takes $k$, a message $m$, and a candidate tag $t$, and outputs $1$ (accept) or $0$ (reject).
\end{itemize}
Correctness requires that for every key $k$ output by $\Gen(1^n)$ and every message $m \in \{0,1\}^*$,
\[
  \Vrfy_k\bigl(m,\MAC_k(m)\bigr) = 1.
\]
\end{definition}

When $\MAC$ is deterministic -- as every construction in this chapter is -- verification recomputes $t' = \MAC_k(m)$ and accepts if and only if $t' = t$. (Section~\ref{ch15:constant-time} shows why naive byte-by-byte comparison leaks the tag through timing.)

Alice and Bob share $k$ ahead of time (Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-mac-flow}). To send $m$, Alice transmits $(m, t)$ with $t=\MAC_k(m)$. Bob runs $\Vrfy_k(m,t)$; if it returns $1$ he accepts the message, and otherwise he discards it. The tag travels \emph{in the clear} alongside the message -- a MAC is not encryption and hides nothing. Its job is to be unforgeable.

\begin{focusbox}
A MAC tag is short -- 128 or 256 bits is typical -- no matter how long the message is. That is the same compression you saw in hash functions (Chapter~14): a fingerprint. The difference is the key. A hash $\Hash(m)$ is public; anyone can compute it, so anyone can compute a matching hash for a modified message. A MAC $\MAC_k(m)$ folds in a secret key $k$, so only key-holders can produce valid tags. A MAC is a \emph{keyed} fingerprint.
\end{focusbox}

\section{What ``Secure'' Means: Unforgeability}
\label{ch15:unforgeability}

To test whether a MAC is secure, we specify both what the attacker may query and what counts as a successful forgery.

The standard definition is \textbf{existential unforgeability under an adaptive chosen-message attack}, abbreviated EUF-CMA. Each part of the name has a specific meaning:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Chosen-message attack:} the adversary may ask for tags on any messages she likes. We model this by giving her a \emph{MAC oracle} -- a black box that, on input $m$, returns $\MAC_k(m)$ -- without revealing $k$. ``Adaptive'' means she picks each query after seeing previous answers.
  \item \emph{Existential forgery:} she wins by producing \emph{any} new valid message--tag pair $(m^*,t^*)$ -- ``existential'' because it can be any message she manages to forge, not a predetermined target. The single restriction is that $m^*$ must be a message she never queried.
\end{itemize}

\begin{formalbox}
\textbf{The forgery game $\mathsf{MacForge}_{\mathcal{A},\Pi}(n)$.} For a MAC scheme $\Pi=(\Gen,\MAC,\Vrfy)$ and adversary $\mathcal{A}$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Run $k\gets\Gen(1^n)$.
  \item $\mathcal{A}$ is given oracle access to $\MAC_k(\cdot)$. Let $Q$ be the set of messages she queries.
  \item $\mathcal{A}$ outputs a candidate pair $(m^*,t^*)$.
  \item The game outputs $1$ (adversary wins) if and only if $\Vrfy_k(m^*,t^*)=1$ \emph{and} $m^*\notin Q$.
\end{enumerate}
$\Pi$ is \textbf{EUF-CMA secure} if for every probabilistic polynomial-time ($\PPT$) adversary $\mathcal{A}$,
\[
  \Pr\!\Bigl[\mathsf{MacForge}_{\mathcal{A},\Pi}(n)=1\Bigr] \;\le\; \negl(n),
\]
where $\negl(n)$ is a negligible function of the security parameter $n$.
\end{formalbox}

Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-euf-cma} summarizes the interaction: the adversary queries the MAC oracle adaptively on chosen messages $m_1, \dots, m_q$, and wins if she outputs a valid tag $t^*$ on any fresh message $m^* \notin \{m_1,\dots,m_q\}$.

Why such a strong definition? Because real attackers have these capabilities.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why adaptive chosen-message?} In practice the attacker often \emph{can} get the system to MAC messages of her choosing. A login server returns a MAC'd session cookie for any username she registers; a payment terminal MACs whatever transaction she types in. If a MAC were secure only against attackers who passively eavesdrop, it would fail the moment the attacker could nudge the system into signing things. We assume the worst: she gets tags on everything she asks for.

\textbf{Why existential (any new message)?} A MAC's job is to make \emph{every} forgery hard, not only forgeries of ``important-looking'' messages. If an attacker could forge a tag for even one nonsense message she never queried, the scheme is broken -- because that nonsense might be a control command, a length field, or a building block for a bigger attack. Demanding that no new valid pair exists is the cleanest, strongest bar, and it is the one cryptographers hold MACs to.
\end{whybox}

\begin{trapbox}
EUF-CMA does \emph{not} promise protection against \textbf{replay}. If Alice sends $(m,t)$ and Eve records it, Eve can resend the same pair later; the tag still verifies because $m$ is unchanged. The MAC did its job -- the message is authentic and intact -- it is merely \emph{old}. Defeating replay is a separate, protocol-level concern: include a counter, a timestamp, or a nonce \emph{inside} the MAC'd message, so each authenticated message is unique. TLS, IPsec, and Signal all do this. Never assume ``it has a MAC'' means ``it cannot be replayed.''
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{A pseudorandom function is already a MAC}
\label{ch15:prf-is-mac}

Recall from Section~\ref{ch11:what} that a \emph{pseudorandom function} (PRF) is the function cousin of a pseudorandom permutation: a deterministic keyed function $F_k$ mapping fixed-length inputs to $\ell$-bit outputs such that no $\PPT$ adversary, even after querying $F_k(\cdot)$ on chosen inputs, can distinguish it from a truly random function $F$ (one that assigns an independent, uniformly random $\ell$-bit output to each distinct input). Any secure PRF is already a secure MAC on its input domain:
\[
  \MAC_k(m) = F_k(m), \qquad
  \Vrfy_k(m,t) =
  \begin{cases}
    1 & \text{if } F_k(m) = t, \\
    0 & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
\]

\begin{whybox}
Suppose $F$ were a \emph{truly} random function with $\ell$-bit outputs. The adversary queries it on messages $m_1,\dots,m_q$ and learns those outputs. Now she must guess $t^* = F(m^*)$ for a \emph{fresh} $m^*$. Because a truly random function's value on an unqueried input is uniformly random in $\{0,1\}^\ell$ and independent of every prior output, her best guess is blind: she wins with probability $2^{-\ell}$, which for $\ell = 128$ is $2^{-128}$.

A real PRF $F_k$ is computationally indistinguishable from $F$, so an efficient adversary's forgery probability against $F_k$ can exceed $2^{-\ell}$ by at most $\negl(n)$ -- otherwise running the forger and checking its output would distinguish $F_k$ from $F$. Hence \emph{any secure PRF is a secure fixed-length MAC.} AES, viewed as a PRF, is a MAC for single $128$-bit blocks. Extending this guarantee to messages of \emph{arbitrary} length is the problem that CBC-MAC and HMAC solve.
\end{whybox}

\section{CBC-MAC: A MAC From a Block Cipher}
\label{ch15:cbc-mac}

Suppose we have a block cipher $E_k : \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}^n$ (such as AES with block size $n = 128$ bits) that behaves like a PRF on single blocks. How do we authenticate a multi-block message? A \emph{Cipher Block Chaining Message Authentication Code} (CBC-MAC) chains the blocks through $E_k$ much like CBC-mode encryption (Chapter~13), except that it starts from a fixed all-zero block, discards every intermediate output, and keeps only the final block (Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-cbcmac}). Because that final block depends on every input block, it serves as a keyed fingerprint of the entire message.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Raw CBC-MAC.} Let $E_k$ have block width $n$ bits, and split an $\ell$-block message into $n$-bit blocks $m_1,m_2,\dots,m_\ell$. Set $t_0 = 0^n$ (the block of $n$ zero bits -- unlike CBC encryption, there is \emph{no random initialization vector (IV)}). For $i=1,\dots,\ell$:
\[
  t_i = E_k\bigl(t_{i-1}\xor m_i\bigr).
\]
Output the tag $t = t_\ell$.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{theorem}[Security of raw CBC-MAC, informal]
\label{ch15:cbcmac-thm}
If $E_k$ is a secure PRF and we restrict ourselves to messages of a single \emph{fixed} length $\ell$ blocks, then raw CBC-MAC is an EUF-CMA-secure MAC.
\end{theorem}

The ``fixed length'' condition is essential. If messages may vary in length, raw CBC-MAC can be forged.

\subsection{Working it by hand with a 4-bit toy cipher}
\label{ch15:cbcmac-toy}

Let us trace CBC-MAC on numbers small enough to compute by hand. Take a block size of $n = 4$ bits, so blocks are nibbles (4-bit integers $0$ through $15$). For a stand-in permutation $E_k$, use
\[
  E(x) = (7x + 3) \bmod 16,
\]
which is a bijection on $\{0,\dots,15\}$ because $\gcd(7,16)=1$. (A real block cipher is nonlinear rather than affine -- i.e., never of the form $ax + b \bmod 16$ -- so this map is strictly to trace the chaining.) Authenticate the two-block message $m_1 = 5$, $m_2 = 9$.

\begin{walkbox}
Start from $t_0 = 0$:
\begin{align*}
  t_0 \xor m_1 &= 0 \xor 5 = 5, \\
  t_1 &= E(5) = (7 \cdot 5 + 3) \bmod 16 = 38 \bmod 16 = 6, \\
  t_1 \xor m_2 &= 0110_2 \xor 1001_2 = 1111_2 = 15, \\
  t_2 &= E(15) = (7 \cdot 15 + 3) \bmod 16 = 108 \bmod 16 = 12.
\end{align*}
The resulting tag is $t = t_2 = 12$.
\end{walkbox}

Now flip a single bit of the message by changing $m_1$ from $5$ ($0101_2$) to $4$ ($0100_2$), keeping $m_2 = 9$ unchanged:
\begin{align*}
  t_0 \xor m_1 &= 0 \xor 4 = 4,
    & t_1 &= E(4) = 31 \bmod 16 = 15, \\
  t_1 \xor m_2 &= 1111_2 \xor 1001_2 = 0110_2 = 6,
    & t_2 &= E(6) = 45 \bmod 16 = 13.
\end{align*}
The tag changed from $12$ ($1100_2$) to $13$ ($1101_2$); with a real block cipher such as AES, the avalanche effect flips roughly half of all $128$ tag bits unpredictably.

\subsection{The length-extension forgery on raw CBC-MAC}
\label{ch15:cbcmac-attack}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Forging variable-length CBC-MAC.} Suppose the system uses raw CBC-MAC on messages of \emph{any} block length, starting from $0^n$. The attacker queries the oracle on a single-block message $m$ and receives
\[
  t = E_k(0^n \xor m) = E_k(m).
\]
She then outputs the same $t$ as a candidate tag for the unqueried \emph{two-block} message $M = m \concat (m \xor t)$, where $\concat$ denotes concatenation. Running CBC-MAC on $M$ confirms the forgery:
\begin{align*}
  t_1 &= E_k(0^n \xor m) = E_k(m) = t, \\
  t_2 &= E_k\bigl(t_1 \xor (m \xor t)\bigr) = E_k(t \xor m \xor t) = E_k(m) = t.
\end{align*}
Because $t \xor t = 0^n$, the XOR at block~$2$ cancels $t_1$ and feeds $m$ into $E_k$ a second time, producing $t_2 = t$. She has forged a valid tag on a fresh message, breaking EUF-CMA security for variable-length inputs.

\subsection{Fixing CBC-MAC}
\label{ch15:cbcmac-fix}

Two standard repairs break the splicing trick by preventing a valid tag on a short message from masquerading as an internal chaining value of a longer message (Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-cbcmac-fixes}).

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Prepend the length.} Encode the block count $\ell$ as an $n$-bit block $\langle\ell\rangle$, and run raw CBC-MAC over $\langle\ell\rangle \concat m_1 \concat \cdots \concat m_\ell$. Because messages of different lengths begin with different initial blocks $\langle\ell\rangle \ne \langle\ell'\rangle$, their chaining states diverge before $m_1$ is ever processed. (Appending $\langle\ell\rangle$ at the \emph{end} fails: an attacker can still splice earlier blocks before reaching the final length block.)
  \item \textbf{Encrypt the final block under a second key.} In \emph{encrypted MAC} (EMAC, also called encrypted CBC-MAC or ECBC-MAC), compute raw CBC-MAC under key $k_1$ to obtain $t_\ell$, then encrypt $t_\ell$ under an independent key $k_2$ and output $t = E_{k_2}(t_\ell)$. That outer encryption hides the raw chaining state $t_\ell$ so the attacker cannot cancel it in a subsequent XOR. NIST's standardized \emph{Cipher-based MAC} (CMAC, NIST SP 800-38B) refines this approach by deriving subkeys from a single block-cipher key and handling partial final blocks without extra length blocks.
\end{itemize}

\section{Why You Cannot Just MAC With a Hash}
\label{ch15:length-extension}

Hash functions (Chapter~14) already compress arbitrary messages to short digests and resist collisions. A tempting shortcut is to build a MAC by hashing the secret key concatenated with the message:
\[
  \MAC_k(m) \stackrel{?}{=} \Hash(k \concat m).
\]
This candidate is called the \emph{secret-prefix} MAC, and for every Merkle--Damg\aa{}rd hash deployed in practice -- MD5, SHA-1, SHA-256, SHA-512 -- it is \textbf{broken}. The reason is \emph{length extension}.

\begin{intuitionbox}
A Merkle--Damg\aa{}rd hash processes a message block by block, updating an internal chaining state. \textbf{The digest it outputs at the end is that raw final chaining state.} Anyone who sees $\Hash(x)$ knows the exact state where the compression chain stopped after consuming $x$ (padded to a block boundary), and can resume the chain from that state to compute $\Hash(x\concat\texttt{pad}\concat y)$ for any chosen suffix $y$ \emph{without knowing $x$}. The public digest acts as a continuation token.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Length-extension forgery on secret-prefix MAC.} Suppose a server authenticates messages with $t = \Hash(k\concat m)$ using SHA-256. An attacker observes one valid pair $(m, t)$, such as
\[
  m = \texttt{"amount=100\&to=alice"},
\]
and knows (or tries the few plausible values of) the key's byte-length $\abs{k}$.

Before hashing, SHA-256 appends deterministic padding $\texttt{pad}_1$ (a $1$ bit, zero bits, and the total bit-length of $k \concat m$) to reach a $64$-byte block boundary. Thus the tag $t$ equals the internal chaining state immediately after absorbing
\[
  k \concat m \concat \texttt{pad}_1,
\]
where $\texttt{pad}_1$ depends only on $\abs{k} + \abs{m}$, not on the contents of $k$. The attacker \emph{initializes her own SHA-256 state to $t$}, sets the byte counter to $\abs{k} + \abs{m} + \abs{\texttt{pad}_1}$, and hashes any suffix $y$ she chooses, such as
\[
  y = \texttt{"\&to=eve\&admin=true"}.
\]
The resulting output $t'$ is the exact SHA-256 digest of $k \concat m' \concat \texttt{pad}_2$ for the extended message
\[
  m' = m \concat \texttt{pad}_1 \concat y,
\]
so $\Vrfy_k(m', t') = 1$ even though the attacker never knew $k$.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{trapbox}
Length extension has broken deployed web APIs. The 2009 Flickr API signature vulnerability and a wave of similar web-authentication bugs stemmed directly from authenticating requests with $\Hash(\text{secret}\concat\text{message})$ under MD5, SHA-1, or SHA-256. Whenever homegrown authentication prepends a secret to data and hashes once with a Merkle--Damg\aa{}rd hash, it is forgeable. Use HMAC.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{The length extension, worked on a toy hash}
\label{ch15:lenext-toy}

Tracing the internal state on small numbers makes the mechanism concrete (Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-lenext}). Define a toy Merkle--Damg\aa{}rd hash whose chaining state is an integer modulo $251$ and whose compression function $C$ absorbs one byte $x_i \in \{0,\dots,255\}$ at a time:
\[
  s_0 = 0, \qquad s_i = C(s_{i-1}, x_i) = (31\,s_{i-1} + x_i) \bmod 251.
\]
After absorbing the padded input, the hash outputs its final state directly as the digest. To keep the arithmetic short, model Merkle--Damg\aa{}rd padding by appending a single byte $P = 128$ (\texttt{0x80}). Writing $[b_1, b_2, \dots]$ for a sequence of byte values, let the secret key be $k = [9]$ (so $\abs{k} = 1$ byte, unknown to the attacker) and let the message be $m = [2, 5]$.

\begin{walkbox}
\textbf{The legitimate tag.} The server computes $t=\Hash(k\concat m)$ over the padded byte stream $[9,2,5,P]$ with $P=128$:
\begin{align*}
  s_1 &= (31\cdot 0 + 9)\bmod 251 = 9, \\
  s_2 &= (31\cdot 9 + 2)\bmod 251 = 281\bmod 251 = 30, \\
  s_3 &= (31\cdot 30 + 5)\bmod 251 = 935\bmod 251 = 935 - 3\cdot 251 = 182, \\
  s_4 &= (31\cdot 182 + 128)\bmod 251 = 5770\bmod 251 = 5770 - 22\cdot 251 = 248.
\end{align*}
The server publishes $(m,t)=([2,5],\,248)$, and the attacker knows $\abs{k}=1$.
\end{walkbox}

\begin{walkbox}
\textbf{The forgery, without $k$.} The attacker chooses a suffix byte $y=[7]$, sets her hash state directly to the public tag $s_4 = t = 248$, and feeds in $y = 7$ followed by a fresh closing pad $P = 128$:
\begin{align*}
  s' &= (31\cdot 248 + 7)\bmod 251 = 7695\bmod 251 = 7695 - 30\cdot 251 = 165, \\
  t' &= (31\cdot 165 + 128)\bmod 251 = 5243\bmod 251 = 5243 - 20\cdot 251 = 223.
\end{align*}
She outputs $t'=223$ as the forged tag for the four-byte message
\[
  m' = m \concat [P] \concat y = [2, 5, 128, 7].
\]
\end{walkbox}

\begin{walkbox}
\textbf{Does the server agree?} Holding $k=[9]$, the server verifies $(m', t')$ by hashing $k \concat m' \concat [P] = [9,2,5,128,7,128]$. The first four bytes reproduce $s_1 = 9$, $s_2 = 30$, $s_3 = 182$, and $s_4 = 248$ -- the exact state from which the attacker resumed. The final two bytes give
\[
  s_5 = (31\cdot 248 + 7)\bmod 251 = 165, \qquad
  s_6 = (31\cdot 165 + 128)\bmod 251 = 223.
\]
The server's recomputed tag $s_6 = 223$ matches $t'$, so verification accepts even though the attacker never knew $k$.
\end{walkbox}

\subsection{Does secret-suffix help? Partly, and not enough}

Appending the key at the end -- $\Hash(m\concat k)$ -- blocks length extension because the attacker cannot append bytes after the unknown final key $k$. However, its security now rests entirely on the collision resistance of $\Hash$. If an attacker finds any two messages $m_1\ne m_2$ with $\Hash(m_1)=\Hash(m_2)$ (assuming they share a block boundary, or by padding them to one) -- work that the birthday bound (Chapters~7 and~14) cuts to $2^{\ell/2}$ for an $\ell$-bit hash and that is practical today for MD5 and SHA-1 -- then feeding the same suffix $k$ after identical chaining states yields $\Hash(m_1\concat k)=\Hash(m_2\concat k)$. Querying the tag on $m_1$ immediately forges the tag on $m_2$. A good hash-based MAC should remain unforgeable even if off-line collisions in the unkeyed hash are found.

\section{HMAC: The Right Way to MAC With a Hash}
\label{ch15:hmac}

The \emph{Hash-based Message Authentication Code} (HMAC) wraps an unkeyed hash function in a two-pass nested call that stops length extension and reduces security to assumptions on the hash's compression function alone. Designed by Mihir Bellare, Ran Canetti, and Hugo Krawczyk in 1996 and standardized as RFC~2104 and Federal Information Processing Standard (FIPS)~198, HMAC authenticates traffic across TLS, IPsec, SSH, and Signal.

\begin{historybox}
Before HMAC, protocol designers improvised ad hoc ``hash the key with the message'' recipes such as $\Hash(k \concat m)$ or $\Hash(m \concat k)$, leaving systems open to length extension or collision forgeries. Bellare, Canetti, and Krawczyk solved two problems at once: they gave engineers a fixed recipe built on top of any unmodified hash library, and they proved it is an EUF-CMA-secure MAC whenever the hash's compression function behaves like a PRF when keyed through its chaining state -- a much weaker requirement than collision resistance of the full hash. When Xiaoyun Wang broke MD5 collision resistance in 2004, many feared HMAC-MD5 would fall too; Bellare proved in 2006 that HMAC's proof needs no collision resistance from the unkeyed hash, explaining why HMAC-MD5 and HMAC-SHA-1 remained unforgeable long after collisions in MD5 and SHA-1 became practical.
\end{historybox}

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The HMAC construction.} Let $\Hash$ be a hash function with compression block size $B$ bytes (for SHA-256, $B=64$) and digest size $L$ bytes (for SHA-256, $L=32$). Define two fixed $B$-byte pad constants:
\[
  \texttt{ipad} = \texttt{0x36}\ \text{repeated $B$ times},\qquad
  \texttt{opad} = \texttt{0x5c}\ \text{repeated $B$ times}.
\]
Normalize the secret key $k$ to a $B$-byte block $k_0$: if $\abs{k} > B$ bytes, replace $k$ with $\Hash(k)$; then append zero bytes on the right until $k_0$ has length $B$. Define
\[
  \mathrm{HMAC}_k(m) \;=\; \Hash\!\Bigl(\,(k_0\xor\texttt{opad})\;\concat\;\Hash\bigl((k_0\xor\texttt{ipad})\concat m\bigr)\Bigr).
\]
\end{focusbox}

Read the formula from the inside out (Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-hmac}). First, the \emph{inner} hash computes $h_{\mathrm{in}} = \Hash\bigl((k_0\xor\texttt{ipad})\concat m\bigr)$, hashing a $B$-byte keyed block followed by $m$. Second, the \emph{outer} hash computes $\Hash\bigl((k_0\xor\texttt{opad})\concat h_{\mathrm{in}}\bigr)$, hashing a second $B$-byte keyed block followed by the $L$-byte inner digest.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why the nesting defeats length extension.} Extending the message $m$ would mean resuming the \emph{inner} hash $\Hash\bigl((k_0\xor\texttt{ipad})\concat m\bigr)$ from its output $h_{\mathrm{in}}$, which the attacker never sees. She sees only the \emph{outer} tag $\Hash\bigl((k_0\xor\texttt{opad})\concat h_{\mathrm{in}}\bigr)$. Even if she applies Merkle--Damg\aa{}rd length extension to that outer tag, she obtains the hash of $(k_0\xor\texttt{opad})\concat h_{\mathrm{in}}\concat\texttt{pad}\concat z$ -- an outer input longer than $B+L$ bytes. Verification in $\mathrm{HMAC}_k(m')$, however, \emph{always} feeds the outer hash an input of fixed length $B+L$ bytes (one $B$-byte key block plus one $L$-byte inner digest), so an extended outer hash never matches $\mathrm{HMAC}_k(m')$ for any message $m'$.

\textbf{Why the security proof needs so little.} Bellare, Canetti, and Krawczyk prove HMAC is EUF-CMA secure provided the hash's \emph{compression function} behaves like a PRF when keyed via its chaining input, and provided an attacker who does not know $k_0$ cannot find two distinct queried messages $m \ne m'$ that yield the same keyed inner digest $\Hash\bigl((k_0\xor\texttt{ipad})\concat m\bigr)$. Both conditions hold even when an attacker can find collisions in the \emph{unkeyed} hash $\Hash(\cdot)$, because choosing colliding inputs without knowing the secret starting block $k_0\xor\texttt{ipad}$ does not force a collision after that secret first block is processed.

\textbf{Why two different pads?} Because $\texttt{ipad} = \texttt{0x36} = 00110110_2$ and $\texttt{opad} = \texttt{0x5c} = 01011100_2$ differ in four of their eight bits, $k_0\xor\texttt{ipad}$ and $k_0\xor\texttt{opad}$ act as two distinct subkeys derived from a single secret $k$, keying the inner and outer compression calls independently without requiring a double-length key.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{A tiny HMAC by hand}
\label{ch15:hmac-toy}

Real HMAC operates on $64$-byte blocks, too wide to trace by hand. To see the sequence of operations on single bytes, set the block size to $B=1$ byte and the digest size to $L=1$ byte, and replace $\Hash$ with a toy polynomial hash over a byte list $x = [x_0, x_1, \dots, x_{r-1}]$ modulo the prime $251$:
\[
  h(x) = \Biggl(\sum_{i=0}^{r-1} x_i \cdot 31^{i}\Biggr) \bmod 251.
\]
Take $\texttt{ipad}=\texttt{0x36}=54$, $\texttt{opad}=\texttt{0x5c}=92$, secret key $k = 10$ (already $B=1$ byte, so $k_0=10$), and one-byte message $m = [7]$.

\begin{walkbox}
\textbf{1. Inner key and inner digest.} XOR $k_0$ with $\texttt{ipad}$:
\[
  k_0 \xor \texttt{ipad} = 10 \xor 54 = 00001010_2 \xor 00110110_2 = 00111100_2 = 60.
\]
Evaluate $h$ on the concatenated inner byte list $[60, 7]$:
\begin{align*}
  h_{\mathrm{in}}
    &= (60\cdot 31^{0} + 7\cdot 31^{1}) \bmod 251 \\
    &= (60 + 217) \bmod 251 = 277 \bmod 251 = 26.
\end{align*}

\textbf{2. Outer key and final tag.} XOR $k_0$ with $\texttt{opad}$:
\[
  k_0 \xor \texttt{opad} = 10 \xor 92 = 00001010_2 \xor 01011100_2 = 01010110_2 = 86.
\]
Evaluate $h$ on the concatenated outer byte list $[86, 26]$:
\begin{align*}
  \mathrm{HMAC}_{10}([7])
    &= (86\cdot 31^{0} + 26\cdot 31^{1}) \bmod 251 \\
    &= (86 + 806) \bmod 251 = 892 - 3\cdot 251 = 139.
\end{align*}
\end{walkbox}

Thus $\mathrm{HMAC}_{10}([7]) = 139$ under the toy hash. Although the polynomial $h$ has no cryptographic strength, the data flow matches full HMAC: derive $k_0 \xor \texttt{ipad}$, hash with $m$, derive $k_0 \xor \texttt{opad}$, and hash again over the inner digest. Changing either $k$ or $m$ alters both stages, and without $k$ an attacker can compute neither stage.

\begin{implementbox}
Building HMAC-SHA-256 directly on Python's \texttt{hashlib} takes ten lines of code and shows every block operation explicitly. \textbf{Learning only -- in production call \texttt{hmac.new(key, msg, hashlib.sha256)} from the standard library, which is audited and paired with constant-time verification.}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
import hashlib

def hmac_sha256(key: bytes, msg: bytes) -> bytes:
    B = 64                      # SHA-256 block size in bytes
    if len(key) > B:            # long keys get hashed down first
        key = hashlib.sha256(key).digest()
    k0 = key + b"\x00" * (B - len(key))   # right-pad to B bytes

    ipad = bytes(b ^ 0x36 for b in k0)
    opad = bytes(b ^ 0x5c for b in k0)

    inner = hashlib.sha256(ipad + msg).digest()
    return hashlib.sha256(opad + inner).digest()

# Sanity check against the standard library
import hmac
key, msg = b"my secret key", b"transfer 100 to bob"
mine = hmac_sha256(key, msg)
ref  = hmac.new(key, msg, hashlib.sha256).digest()
assert mine == ref, "mismatch"
print(mine.hex())
\end{lstlisting}
No explicit length field appears in the code: HMAC's outer hash, rather than a length prefix, blocks length extension.
\end{implementbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
The forgery probability for a PRF-based MAC with an $\ell$-bit tag bottoms out at $2^{-\ell}$ (Section~\ref{ch15:prf-is-mac}), the probability of guessing one uniformly random $\ell$-bit string -- an application of uniform independence from the probability companion. By contrast, collision attacks against secret-suffix MACs $\Hash(m \concat k)$ obey the \emph{birthday bound}: an $\ell$-bit hash begins colliding after roughly $2^{\ell/2}$ evaluations, capping collision-based security at $2^{\ell/2}$ instead of $2^{\ell}$. Because HMAC does not rely on unkeyed collision resistance, its tag-guessing bound remains $2^{-\ell}$. Computationally, HMAC adds only two extra $B$-byte block compressions (one for $k_0 \xor \texttt{ipad}$ and one for $k_0 \xor \texttt{opad}$, which can even be precomputed per key) beyond hashing the message itself.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Combining Encryption and Authentication}
\label{ch15:combining}

Most real traffic needs \emph{both} confidentiality and integrity. Given a CPA-secure encryption scheme $(\Enc, \Dec)$ under key $k_E$ and an EUF-CMA-secure MAC under an \emph{independent} key $k_M$, there are three natural ways to combine them on the sender side (Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-compose}). Two of the three have caused major vulnerabilities in deployed protocols.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Encrypt-and-MAC (E\&M):} compute $c = \Enc_{k_E}(m)$ and $t = \MAC_{k_M}(m)$ in parallel, and send $(c,t)$. The tag authenticates the \emph{plaintext} and travels in the clear alongside $c$.
  \item \textbf{MAC-then-Encrypt (MtE):} compute $t = \MAC_{k_M}(m)$, then encrypt message and tag together as $c = \Enc_{k_E}(m\concat t)$, and send $c$.
  \item \textbf{Encrypt-then-MAC (EtM):} compute $c = \Enc_{k_E}(m)$, then authenticate the \emph{ciphertext} via $t = \MAC_{k_M}(c)$, and send $(c,t)$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Encrypt-then-MAC is the composition to use.} It is the only one of the three that is provably secure for \emph{every} CPA-secure cipher and \emph{every} EUF-CMA-secure MAC with no extra assumptions. The other two can be proved safe only for restricted classes of ciphers or MACs, and have failed repeatedly in deployed protocols.
\end{focusbox}

\subsection{Why Encrypt-then-MAC wins}

\begin{whybox}
With EtM, the receiver \textbf{verifies the MAC on the ciphertext \emph{before decrypting anything}} (Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-mte-vs-etm-rx}, bottom). If $\Vrfy_{k_M}(c,t) = 0$, he drops the packet and never invokes $\Dec_{k_E}$. Two guarantees follow immediately:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Integrity of ciphertexts.} An attacker cannot produce any new pair $(c,t)$ that passes verification without forging the MAC. Every ciphertext that reaches $\Dec_{k_E}$ is therefore one the legitimate sender produced.
  \item \emph{No decryption oracle.} Because modified ciphertexts die at the MAC gate, an active attacker learns nothing about how $\Dec_{k_E}$ would react to malformed inputs -- no padding errors, no framing errors, and no secret-dependent decryption timing.
\end{enumerate}
Formally, composing any CPA-secure cipher with any EUF-CMA-secure MAC via EtM (under independent keys) yields an \emph{authenticated encryption} scheme that resists chosen-ciphertext attacks (CCA, Chapter~1).
\end{whybox}

\subsection{Why the other orders are dangerous}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{MAC-then-Encrypt and the padding oracle.} Under MtE, the receiver must \emph{decrypt first} to recover $m\concat t$ before he can check $t$ (Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-mte-vs-etm-rx}, top). When $\Enc_{k_E}$ is CBC mode, decryption checks and strips PKCS\#7 padding (Chapter~13) \emph{before} the MAC is ever inspected.

Recall from Chapter~13 that a \emph{padding oracle} is any observable difference -- a distinct alert code, a connection drop, or a microsecond timing gap -- between valid and invalid decrypted padding. Because MtE runs decryption and padding checks on unauthenticated ciphertexts, an active attacker can submit modified ciphertexts, observe one bit of padding validity per query, and recover the entire plaintext without knowing $k_E$ in roughly $128$ queries per byte.

\textbf{TLS~1.0--1.2 used MAC-then-Encrypt with CBC}, triggering a decade of padding-oracle breaks: Serge Vaudenay's 2002 error-code oracle, followed by Lucky~13 (AlFardan and Paterson, 2013), which exploited subtle HMAC compression-count timing differences after TLS hid the explicit padding error. Each attack recovered secrets such as HTTP session cookies. Under EtM, every modified ciphertext is rejected at the MAC check before CBC decryption or padding removal ever runs.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Encrypt-and-MAC leaks through the tag.} E\&M computes $t=\MAC_{k_M}(m)$ directly on the \emph{plaintext} and sends $t$ in the clear. EUF-CMA requires a MAC to be \emph{unforgeable}, not to hide $m$. Every deterministic MAC -- including HMAC and CMAC -- maps equal plaintexts to equal tags: if Alice sends \texttt{"YES"} today and \texttt{"YES"} tomorrow, the ciphertexts differ (thanks to fresh IVs or nonces), yet the tags match, telling an eavesdropper that the two plaintexts were identical. That breaks IND-CPA confidentiality outright. (\textbf{SSH} originally used a variant of E\&M and added per-packet sequence numbers inside the MAC input plus AEAD modes to close the leak.)
\end{attackbox}

\begin{trapbox}
Two rules govern every composition of encryption and authentication:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Use independent keys} $k_E\ne k_M$. Sharing one key across $\Enc$ and $\MAC$ can let algebraic interactions break both (for instance, CBC encryption and raw CBC-MAC under the same key cancel each other). Derive separate keys $k_E, k_M$ from a master secret using a \emph{key derivation function} (KDF) -- a function that deterministically expands one secret into multiple independent-looking subkeys, such as the HMAC-based Key Derivation Function (HKDF, RFC~5869).
  \item \textbf{Use Encrypt-then-MAC}, verifying the tag in constant time \emph{before} decrypting. Never pass unauthenticated bytes to decryption or parsing code.
\end{enumerate}
Better yet, avoid manual composition altogether: use a standardized \emph{authenticated encryption with associated data} (AEAD) cipher such as AES-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305 (Chapter~16), which integrates encryption and ciphertext authentication behind a single key and interface.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
Modern standards reflect these lessons. \textbf{TLS~1.3} (2018) removed CBC-mode MtE cipher suites completely and permits only AEAD ciphers (AES-GCM, ChaCha20-Poly1305, AES-CCM). In \textbf{IPsec}, the Encapsulating Security Payload (ESP -- the protocol layer that encrypts and authenticates IP packets) uses Encrypt-then-MAC with HMAC-SHA-256 or AEAD. \textbf{Signal} encrypts messages with AES-CBC and authenticates the resulting ciphertext via Encrypt-then-MAC with HMAC-SHA-256 under keys derived via HKDF. Even \textbf{TLS~1.2} received an Encrypt-then-MAC extension (RFC~7366, 2014) to shut down Lucky~13 on legacy CBC suites.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Verifying Tags in Constant Time}
\label{ch15:constant-time}

Verification compares the received tag $t$ against the recomputed tag $t'$. Standard byte-string equality in almost every programming language (such as Python's \texttt{==} or C's \texttt{memcmp}) uses a \emph{short-circuit comparison}, halting at the \emph{first} index where two bytes differ:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
def insecure_verify(t_received, t_computed):
    return t_received == t_computed   # stops at the first differing byte
\end{lstlisting}
That early exit creates a \emph{timing side channel} (Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-timing}, Panel~A). A tag guess that fails at byte~$0$ returns slightly sooner than one that matches bytes~$0$ through~$2$ and fails at byte~$3$. Over repeated queries, an attacker who times the server's rejections can recover a valid tag one byte at a time (Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-timing-spike}).

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Timing a tag, byte by byte.} To forge a tag on a chosen message $m$, the attacker submits $(m, g)$ for a candidate tag $g$ and records how long the server takes to reject.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Set $g = [\texttt{0x00}, \dots, \texttt{0x00}]$ and sweep the first byte $g_0$ through all $256$ values $\texttt{0x00}$--$\texttt{0xFF}$. For the $255$ wrong values, comparison stops at index~$0$; for the unique byte $g_0 = t'_0$, comparison advances to index~$1$ before failing and takes slightly longer. Averaging multiple measurements per candidate filters out network jitter and identifies $t'_0$.
  \item Fix $g_0 = t'_0$ and sweep $g_1$ through all $256$ values to find the candidate that advances to index~$2$.
  \item Repeat across all $L$ bytes of the tag. Recovering a $32$-byte HMAC-SHA-256 tag takes roughly $32 \times 256 = 8{,}192$ timed guesses (multiplied by the samples needed to average out noise) instead of the $2^{256}$ operations required for blind guessing.
\end{enumerate}
A secure MAC is broken here by the \texttt{==} operator rather than any weakness in HMAC. Vulnerabilities of this exact form were found in Google's KeyCzar library (2009) and numerous web-framework signature verifiers.
\end{attackbox}

The defense is a \emph{constant-time comparison} (Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-timing}, Panel~B): examine every byte unconditionally so execution time depends only on the public tag length $L$, never on where a mismatch occurs. For each index $i$, compute the bytewise XOR $a_i \xor b_i$ (which is $0$ when $a_i = b_i$ and nonzero otherwise) and fold it into an accumulator with bitwise OR (\texttt{|}), testing whether the accumulator equals $0$ once after the loop finishes.

\begin{implementbox}
\textbf{Learning only -- in production call \texttt{hmac.compare\_digest} (Python) or \texttt{CRYPTO\_memcmp} (OpenSSL), which are written in C so compiler or interpreter optimizations do not reintroduce data-dependent branches.}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
def constant_time_equal(a: bytes, b: bytes) -> bool:
    # Assumes a and b have the same, non-secret length.
    if len(a) != len(b):
        return False
    acc = 0
    for x, y in zip(a, b):
        acc |= x ^ y          # any differing bit sets a 1 in acc
    return acc == 0           # single test after scanning every byte

# Standard library call:
import hmac
assert hmac.compare_digest(b"abc", b"abc")
\end{lstlisting}
The loop runs the same number of iterations and executes the same bitwise operations whether the tags match or differ at byte~$0$ or byte~$31$, leaking no information about matching prefixes.
\end{implementbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Timing leaks are an example of \emph{side-channel attacks} (Chapter~37), where secrets leak not through mathematical weaknesses in the primitive, but through execution time or hardware power draw. Keeping control flow and memory access independent of secret values recurs throughout cryptographic engineering: constant-time modular exponentiation for RSA (Chapter~3), bitsliced or hardware-instruction AES without secret-indexed table lookups (Chapter~12), and constant-time scalar multiplication in elliptic-curve cryptography (Chapter~21). In the probability companion's terms, averaging $N$ repeated timing measurements per candidate shrinks random network noise by $1/\sqrt{N}$ until even a nanosecond single-byte step stands above the noise floor.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Putting It Together}
\label{ch15:summary}

A MAC is the symmetric-key tool for integrity and authenticity: a short, keyed tag $\MAC_k(m)$ sent alongside the message so any holder of $k$ can detect tampering. Security means EUF-CMA -- even an attacker who obtains valid tags on adaptively chosen messages cannot forge a valid tag on any unqueried message except with negligible probability. Any secure PRF already meets this bar on fixed-length inputs; practical MACs extend that guarantee to arbitrary lengths.

Two designs handle variable-length messages. CBC-MAC chains a block cipher across message blocks, and once repaired against variable-length splicing -- by prepending the length or encrypting the final block under an independent key, as in CMAC -- yields a secure block-cipher MAC. HMAC nests an ordinary hash function twice,
\[
  \mathrm{HMAC}_k(m) = \Hash\!\Bigl((k_0\xor\texttt{opad}) \concat \Hash\bigl((k_0\xor\texttt{ipad})\concat m\bigr)\Bigr),
\]
hiding the inner chaining state so Merkle--Damg\aa{}rd length extension cannot resume it, while requiring PRF security only from the hash's keyed compression function rather than unkeyed collision resistance. When pairing encryption with a MAC, use independent keys derived via a KDF and apply \emph{Encrypt-then-MAC} -- never MAC-then-Encrypt (which exposes decryption padding oracles such as Lucky~13) or Encrypt-and-MAC (which leaks plaintext equality through deterministic tags). Finally, verify every tag with a constant-time comparison so timing probes cannot read off the valid tag byte by byte. Chapter~16 packages encryption and ciphertext authentication into single AEAD primitives so protocols do not have to assemble the pieces by hand.

\section*{Exercises}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Malleability, concretely]
Alice encrypts the 8-byte ASCII message \texttt{"send \$05"} with a stream cipher, producing ciphertext $c$. Eve knows the format and wants the bank to read \texttt{"send \$98"}. The last two plaintext bytes are \texttt{'0'} (\texttt{0x30}) and \texttt{'5'} (\texttt{0x35}); she wants \texttt{'9'} (\texttt{0x39}) and \texttt{'8'} (\texttt{0x38}). Compute the two XOR masks Eve must apply to the last two ciphertext bytes, and explain in one sentence why she needs no knowledge of the keystream.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Trace CBC-MAC]
Using the toy cipher $E(x)=(7x+3)\bmod 16$ from Section~\ref{ch15:cbcmac-toy} and start value $t_0=0$, compute the raw CBC-MAC tag of the three-block message $m_1=2,\;m_2=11,\;m_3=4$. Show $t_1,t_2,t_3$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Break variable-length CBC-MAC]
You can query a raw CBC-MAC oracle (fixed $0$ start, the cipher from Section~\ref{ch15:cbcmac-toy}, no length binding). Query the one-block message $m=6$ to get its tag $t$. Using the splicing attack of Section~\ref{ch15:cbcmac-attack}, write down a two-block message you have never queried whose tag is also $t$, and verify by tracing CBC-MAC on it.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Length extension on paper]
Explain, in terms of Merkle--Damg\aa{}rd internal state, why an attacker who knows $\Hash(k\concat m)$ and the byte-length $\abs{k}$ can compute a valid secret-prefix tag for a longer message but \emph{cannot} do so against HMAC. Identify the structural feature of HMAC that blocks her.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Toy HMAC]
With the toy hash $h(x)=\bigl(\sum_{i=0}^{r-1} x_i\cdot 31^{i}\bigr)\bmod 251$, block size $B=1$, $\texttt{ipad}=54$, and $\texttt{opad}=92$ from Section~\ref{ch15:hmac-toy}, compute $\mathrm{HMAC}_k(m)$ for key $k=20$ and one-byte message $m=[3]$. Show the inner key, the inner digest, the outer key, and the final tag.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Why two pads?]
Suppose someone defines a ``simplified HMAC'' that uses the \emph{same} pad for inner and outer: $\Hash\bigl((k_0\xor\texttt{p})\concat\Hash((k_0\xor\texttt{p})\concat m)\bigr)$. Argue why having the inner and outer hashes keyed by the \emph{same} value is undesirable, and what property of $\texttt{ipad}\ne\texttt{opad}$ the standard relies on.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Order matters]
For each of MAC-then-Encrypt, Encrypt-and-MAC, and Encrypt-then-MAC, state in one sentence the concrete failure mode discussed in this chapter (padding oracle / tag leakage / none), and name the real protocol associated with each lesson.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Replay is not forgery]
Eve records a valid pair $(m,t)$ from a session and resends it an hour later; the receiver accepts it again. Explain why this does \emph{not} contradict EUF-CMA security, and describe a concrete change to what gets MAC'd that defeats the replay.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Timing attack budget]
A server uses a non-constant-time \texttt{==} to compare $16$-byte HMAC tags. Estimate the number of timed queries an attacker needs to recover a full valid tag for a chosen message via the byte-by-byte attack of Section~\ref{ch15:constant-time}, and compare it to the $2^{128}$ cost of a blind forgery. By what factor does the timing leak reduce the work?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Constant-time proof obligation]
The \texttt{constant\_time\_equal} routine in Section~\ref{ch15:constant-time} early-returns \texttt{False} when the two inputs have different lengths. Explain why this length check does \emph{not} leak secret information in the HMAC verification setting (hint: who controls the tag length, and is it secret?), and give one situation where leaking a length \emph{would} be dangerous.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[PRF implies MAC]
Let $F_k$ be a secure PRF with $128$-bit outputs, used directly as a fixed-length MAC $\MAC_k(m)=F_k(m)$. An adversary makes $q$ oracle queries on distinct messages, then outputs a forgery $(m^*,t^*)$ with $m^*$ fresh. Bound her success probability, and explain why it does not grow with $q$ (beyond the PRF's own distinguishing advantage).
\end{exercise}
