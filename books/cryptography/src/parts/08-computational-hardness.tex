\chapter{Computational Hardness and One-Way Functions}

In Chapter~7, we used probability and statistical distance to measure uncertainty. If we demand that a ciphertext leak zero information even to an adversary with unlimited time and computers, Shannon's theorem (which we prove next chapter) dictates an impossible price: the secret key must be as long as everything you will ever send.

Modern cryptography escapes this trap by changing the question. Instead of asking ``can the adversary \emph{ever} recover the message?'' we ask ``can the adversary recover the message \emph{using a realistic amount of computation}?'' In exchange for bounding the adversary's computation, we get short keys, public keys, and digital signatures. That trade rests on one bet: that certain problems are \emph{easy to pose and hard to solve}. Multiplying two large primes is fast; recovering them from the product seems to require effort that grows wildly with their size. The rest of this chapter makes ``too expensive to break'' mathematically precise and shows how to turn a hard math problem into a security proof.

\section{The asymmetry we are buying}
\label{ch08:asymmetry}

Picture the usual cast. Alice wants to send Bob something secret; Eve listens to everything that crosses the wire. In the unconditional world Eve has unlimited computing power. In the computational world Eve is the best-funded adversary you can imagine (a nation-state with warehouses of hardware), yet still bound by the same laws of computation as everyone else. Our schemes are allowed to be breakable in principle, as long as breaking them would take Eve longer than she or the universe has.

For that to be a sensible bargain we need a job that is cheap for Alice and Bob and ruinously expensive for Eve. The cleanest example is a padlock. Snapping a padlock shut takes a second and no key. Opening it without the key takes a locksmith, a drill, and time. The lock is \emph{asymmetric}: the forward direction (lock it) is easy, the backward direction (open it without the key) is hard. Cryptography relies on mathematical versions of this padlock.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Three concrete asymmetries:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Multiplication vs.\ factoring.} Given $p=61$ and $q=53$, computing $N = pq = 3233$ is instant. Given only $N=3233$, finding the factors takes searching. For a $2048$-bit $N$ it is, as far as anyone knows, hopeless.
\item \textbf{Exponentiation vs.\ discrete log.} Working modulo the prime $p=23$, computing $5^{6} \bmod 23 = 8$ takes a handful of multiplications. Going backwards -- given $5^{x}\equiv 8 \pmod{23}$, find $x$ -- means searching for the exponent. With a $256$-bit modulus and the right group, the backward search is the discrete logarithm problem, and it is believed hard.
\item \textbf{Hashing vs.\ inverting a hash.} A good hash $\Hash$ turns ``hello'' into a fixed $256$-bit digest in microseconds. Given the digest alone, producing \emph{any} input that hashes to it requires trying astronomically many candidates.
\end{itemize}
\end{intuitionbox}

Everything depends on what we mean by ``hard.'' A child can fail to factor $3233$; that does not make factoring hard in any useful sense. We need a definition of hardness that (a) talks about all input sizes at once rather than one number, (b) measures effort against the size of the input, and (c) survives the fact that a lucky adversary might break one instance now and then.

\begin{historybox}
The idea that security could rest on computational difficulty rather than perfect secrecy grew out of work in the 1970s. Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman at Stanford, building on independent work by Ralph Merkle, framed it openly in their 1976 paper ``New Directions in Cryptography'': they proposed building secrecy on functions that are easy to compute and infeasible to invert, and coined the modern notion of a \emph{trapdoor} one-way function. Within a year Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir, and Leonard Adleman at MIT turned that abstract wish into RSA, an actual trapdoor built from factoring. Despite attempts by intelligence agencies to restrict academic publication, this shift from ``unbreakable'' to ``infeasible to break in practice'' let cryptography leave the domain of military one-time pads and become an open science. Still, the foundational question remains open: proving that one-way functions exist would prove $\mathrm{P} \ne \mathrm{NP}$, the conjecture that problems whose solutions are fast to \emph{check} ($\mathrm{NP}$, nondeterministic polynomial time) are not all fast to \emph{find} ($\mathrm{P}$, polynomial time).
\end{historybox}

\section{Measuring effort: input size and asymptotics}
\label{ch08:size}

When we say factoring ``gets hard for large numbers,'' large compared to what? The yardstick is not the numerical value $N$ but the number of bits it takes to \emph{write down} $N$, which is $\floor{\log_2 N} + 1 \approx \log_2 N$. Writing $n$ for this bit-length, a $2048$-bit modulus ($n = 2048$) is a number with roughly $617$ decimal digits; trying to divide it by every integer up to $\sqrt{N} \approx 2^{n/2}$ would mean about $2^{1024}$ trial divisions. The \emph{input} is $2048$ bits (fits in a short text message), yet the naive attack is exponential in that input length. We measure an algorithm's running time as a function $T(n)$ of the input length $n$ in bits, and we track how $T(n)$ scales as $n$ grows.

\begin{definition}[Polynomial and exponential time]
\label{ch08:def-polytime}
An algorithm runs in \emph{polynomial time} if there is a constant $c > 0$ and a threshold $n_0$ such that on every input of bit-length $n \ge n_0$ it halts within $T(n) \le n^{c}$ basic steps. Using the big-$O$ upper-bound notation of Chapter~1 (formally, $O(g(n))$ means ``at most a constant multiple of $g(n)$ for all large $n$''), we write $T(n) = n^{O(1)}$. An algorithm runs in \emph{exponential time} if its running time grows at least like $2^{\alpha n^{\delta}}$ for constants $\alpha, \delta > 0$ and all large $n$. Using the matching Big-$\Omega$ lower-bound notation (where $\Omega(g(n))$ means ``at least a positive constant multiple of $g(n)$ for all large $n$,'' distinct from Chapter~7's sample space $\Omega$), we write $T(n) = 2^{\Omega(n^{\delta})}$; such a function eventually outpaces every polynomial.
\end{definition}

Polynomial time is our stand-in for ``feasible.'' It is an idealization: an algorithm taking $n^{50}$ steps is polynomial yet useless in practice, while one taking $1.000001^{n}$ is exponential yet fast for moderate $n$. What buys this idealization its place is how polynomials combine. Any fixed cutoff (``feasible means under $2^{80}$ steps'') breaks as soon as you repeat a feasible step enough times, whereas polynomials are \emph{closed} under addition, multiplication, and composition: adding, multiplying, or plugging one polynomial into another produces another polynomial. Build a procedure out of polynomially many polynomial-time pieces and the whole procedure stays polynomial-time. Security proofs rely on that guarantee at every step.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Why bits and not the number itself? Because the difficulty of a problem should be compared against how much it costs to \emph{state} the problem. Writing down a $2048$-bit number costs $2048$ bits; an attack that costs $2^{1024}$ steps is exponentially more expensive than the question. If we measured against the numerical value $N$ instead, trial division up to $\sqrt{N} = N^{1/2}$ would look ``polynomial in $N$'' and make factoring look easy.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
The companion algorithms book develops this scaling in detail, and Figure~\ref{ch08:fig-complexity-growth} plots the three regimes side by side. Multiplication of $n$-bit numbers is polynomial (schoolbook multiplication takes $O(n^2)$ bit operations, and faster methods do better), and modular exponentiation $a^b \bmod N$ on $n$-bit inputs takes $O(n)$ modular multiplications via repeated squaring -- $O(n^3)$ bit operations in all -- rather than $b$ multiplications. Meanwhile, the fastest known classical factoring algorithm, the \emph{general number field sieve} (GNFS), runs in time roughly $\exp\!\bigl(c\,n^{1/3}(\ln n)^{2/3}\bigr)$ (where $\exp(z) = e^{z}$). That growth is \emph{sub-exponential}: faster than every polynomial $n^{d}$, yet slower than any true exponential $2^{\alpha n}$. Those cost curves are the empirical evidence behind the key sizes used throughout cryptography.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{The adversary: probabilistic polynomial time}
\label{ch08:ppt}

We do not model Eve as one fixed program, because a security guarantee should hold against every efficient strategy at once. Instead we model her as an arbitrary algorithm endowed with two capabilities beyond a deterministic program (whose steps are fixed by the input alone).

First, randomness. Real attackers flip coins: they sample random inputs, guess random candidates, and run randomized number-theory algorithms. Forbidding randomness would understate Eve's power and let us claim false security, so the adversary may toss fair coins for free.

Second, polynomial time. An adversary given exponential time can brute-force any secret key. Security that is achievable with short keys means security against \emph{efficient} adversaries.

\begin{definition}[$\PPT$ adversary]
\label{ch08:def-ppt}
A \emph{probabilistic polynomial-time} ($\PPT$) algorithm is a randomized algorithm whose running time is bounded by a polynomial in the bit-length of its input, on every input and every sequence of internal coin tosses. A $\PPT$ \emph{adversary} is such an algorithm whose goal is to break a scheme: recover a key, distinguish two ciphertexts, forge a signature, or invert a function.
\end{definition}

Because Eve flips coins (and the challenger picks random keys), the outcome of an attack is a random variable. ``Eve breaks the scheme'' is an \emph{event with a probability}, not a yes/no fact. We therefore need a precise definition of a probability too small to matter.

\begin{focusbox}
Every cryptographic scheme takes a \emph{security parameter}, written $n$ (or $\lambda$ or $k$ in other texts). Think of $n$ as a dial: turn it up and keys get longer, legitimate work grows polynomially in $n$, and -- if the design is sound -- the adversary's success probability shrinks faster than any inverse polynomial in $n$. An RSA modulus of $n$ bits or a symmetric key of $n$ bits makes the parameter concrete. Every quantity below -- running time, encryption cost, attacker advantage -- is a function of $n$.
\end{focusbox}

\section{Negligible vs.\ noticeable}
\label{ch08:negligible}

Suppose an attack against a $256$-bit key succeeds with probability $2^{-128}$. Even running that attack a trillion times per second since the Big Bang, the expected number of successes is far below one. A probability that small is zero for both practice and theory, provided it shrinks rapidly as we turn up the security parameter $n$.

\begin{definition}[Negligible function]
\label{ch08:def-negl}
A function $\negl : \N \to \R_{\ge 0}$ (mapping positive integers $n \in \N$ to non-negative real numbers) is \emph{negligible} if for every positive constant $c$ there is a threshold $n_0$ such that
\[
\negl(n) < \frac{1}{n^{c}} \qquad \text{for all } n \ge n_0.
\]
Equivalently, $\negl(n)$ eventually stays below the reciprocal of \emph{every} positive polynomial.
\end{definition}

Concretely, $2^{-n}$, $2^{-\sqrt{n}}$, and $n^{-\log_2 n}$ are all negligible: each one eventually drops below $1/n^{1000}$, $1/n^{10^6}$, and any other $1/n^{c}$. By contrast, $1/n^{3}$, $1/(1000\,n^2)$, and $1/2^{\log_2 n} = 1/n$ are \emph{not} negligible -- they decay only polynomially. A function that is not negligible (meaning there is a fixed $c > 0$ for which $f(n) \ge 1/n^c$ for infinitely many $n$) is called \emph{noticeable} (or \emph{non-negligible}).

\begin{example}[Negligible or not?]
\label{ch08:ex-negl}
Test each candidate against Definition~\ref{ch08:def-negl}:
\begin{itemize}
\item $f(n) = 2^{-n}$: at $n=80$ this is about $8.3 \times 10^{-25}$, and for any $c$ it stays below $1/n^c$ once $n$ is large enough. \textbf{Negligible.}
\item $f(n) = 2^{-n/2}$: halving the exponent still beats every inverse polynomial. \textbf{Negligible.} (In practice, ``$128$-bit security'' means the best known attack requires roughly $2^{128}$ operations, which is why defending against a $2^{n/2}$ birthday attack requires $n = 256$.)
\item $f(n) = 1/n^{100}$: equals the inverse polynomial $1/n^{100}$, so it never drops below $1/n^{101}$ for $n \ge 2$. \textbf{Noticeable.}
\item $f(n) = n^{-\log_2 n}$: taking base-$2$ logarithms gives $\log_2 f(n) = -(\log_2 n)^2$, which drops below $-c\log_2 n = \log_2(1/n^c)$ once $\log_2 n > c$. \textbf{Negligible.}
\item $f(n) = 2^{-n} + 1/n^{5}$: the $1/n^5$ term keeps $f(n) > 1/n^5$ for all $n$. \textbf{Noticeable.}
\end{itemize}
\end{example}

Watching the crossover with actual numbers shows why the word ``eventually'' in Definition~\ref{ch08:def-negl} matters. For very small $n$, the negligible function $2^{-n}$ is \emph{larger} than the polynomial $1/n^{3}$, and only crosses beneath it near $n = 10$:

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Negligible $\times$ polynomial is negligible.} If $\negl(n)$ is negligible and $p(n)$ is any positive polynomial, then $p(n)\cdot\negl(n)$ is negligible. \emph{Proof.} Fix any constant $c > 0$; we want $p(n)\,\negl(n) < 1/n^{c}$ for all large $n$. Because $p$ is a polynomial, there is an integer $d$ with $p(n) \le n^{d}$ for all sufficiently large $n$. Applying Definition~\ref{ch08:def-negl} with exponent $c + d$ gives $\negl(n) < 1/n^{c+d}$ for all large $n$, so
\[
p(n)\cdot\negl(n) \;\le\; n^{d}\cdot \frac{1}{n^{c+d}} \;=\; \frac{1}{n^{c}}. \qquad \square
\]

Security proofs use this closure rule constantly via the union bound from Chapter~1, $\Pr[E_1 \cup \dots \cup E_k] \le \sum_{i=1}^{k}\Pr[E_i]$. A $\PPT$ adversary runs for $p(n)$ steps; if each step hits a ``bad'' lucky guess with probability at most $\negl(n)$, the union bound caps the total failure probability across the whole run at $p(n)\cdot\negl(n)$, which remains negligible. Repeating an attack polynomially many times cannot amplify a negligible chance into a noticeable one.

\medskip
\textbf{Negligible $+$ negligible is negligible.} If $\negl_1$ and $\negl_2$ are negligible, each eventually drops below $\tfrac{1}{2}n^{-(c+1)} < \tfrac{1}{2}n^{-c}$, so their sum stays below $n^{-c}$. Chaining a finite (or polynomial) number of negligible failure bounds in a proof still leaves a negligible total.
\end{whybox}

A fixed numeric cutoff like ``probability below $2^{-80}$'' lacks this closure: run a $2^{-80}$ attack $2^{40}$ times and the union bound climbs to $2^{-40}$. Defining security asymptotically in $n$ keeps ``too small to matter'' preserved across polynomial repetition and composition.

\begin{trapbox}
Negligibility is an \emph{asymptotic} property of a function of $n$, not a property of a single number. Asking ``is $2^{-100}$ negligible?'' is a category error because $2^{-100}$ is one constant, not a function of $n$. Theory asks whether the success-probability curve $\eps(n)$ beats every $1/n^c$ as $n$ grows; engineering then evaluates that curve at a concrete parameter choice (say $n = 3072$ for RSA) to hit a target like $2^{-128}$.
\end{trapbox}
\section{Advantage: how much better than guessing}
\label{ch08:advantage}

Many attacks do not recover a key outright; they \emph{distinguish}. Eve sees a ciphertext and tries to tell whether it encrypts ``attack at dawn'' or ``attack at noon,'' or to tell a cipher's output from uniform random bits. Because an adversary who ignores the ciphertext and flips a fair coin already guesses right half the time, raw win probability overstates her skill. What matters is how far she climbs above $\tfrac{1}{2}$.

\begin{definition}[Distinguishing advantage]
\label{ch08:def-adv}
Let $\mathcal D$ be a distinguisher (a $\PPT$ algorithm outputting a single bit $b' \in \{0,1\}$) placed in one of two experiments, $\mathsf{Exp}_0$ and $\mathsf{Exp}_1$, chosen by a hidden fair coin $b \getsr \{0,1\}$. Writing $\mathcal D = 1$ for the event that $\mathcal D$ outputs $1$, its \emph{advantage} is
\[
\Adv(\mathcal D) = \Bigl| \Pr[\mathcal D = 1 \mid \mathsf{Exp}_1] - \Pr[\mathcal D = 1 \mid \mathsf{Exp}_0] \Bigr|.
\]
In terms of guessing $b$, $\bigl|\Pr[b' = b] - \tfrac{1}{2}\bigr| = \tfrac{1}{2}\Adv(\mathcal D)$; whenever $\Pr[b' = b] \ge \tfrac{1}{2}$ (which any distinguisher can ensure by flipping its output bit if it does worse than chance),
\[
\Pr[b' = b] = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\Adv(\mathcal D).
\]
\end{definition}

Figure~\ref{ch08:fig-distinguishing-game} shows both views side by side. Blind coin-flipping gives $\Pr[b'=b] = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and $\Adv(\mathcal D) = 0$; a distinguisher that is always right gives $\Pr[b'=b] = 1$ and $\Adv(\mathcal D) = 1$. Rescaling the margin above $\tfrac{1}{2}$ by a factor of $2$ puts $\Adv(\mathcal D)$ on $[0,1]$, matching statistical distance $\Delta(P,Q)$ from Chapter~7. This is the same convention as Chapter~1. (Some texts omit the factor of $2$ and write $|\Pr[b'=b] - \tfrac{1}{2}| \in [0,\tfrac{1}{2}]$; because multiplying by $2$ never changes whether a function is negligible, both conventions define identical security.)

\begin{example}[A tiny advantage calculation]
\label{ch08:ex-adv}
Suppose a flawed cipher leaks the last bit of the plaintext into the ciphertext one time in eight; the rest of the time the ciphertext is uniform random noise. Eve's strategy: when the leak occurs, output the leaked bit as $b'$; otherwise flip a fair coin. With probability $\tfrac{1}{8}$ she sees the leak and wins with certainty, and with probability $\tfrac{7}{8}$ she guesses and wins half the time:
\begin{align*}
  \Pr[b' = b] &= \frac{1}{8}\cdot 1 + \frac{7}{8}\cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{8} + \frac{7}{16} = \frac{9}{16}, \\
  \Adv(\mathcal D) &= 2\Bigl(\Pr[b' = b] - \frac{1}{2}\Bigr) = 2\Bigl(\frac{9}{16} - \frac{8}{16}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{8}.
\end{align*}
A constant advantage of $\tfrac{1}{8}$ does not shrink with $n$, so it is noticeable and the cipher is broken.
\end{example}

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{What ``secure'' means, in one sentence.} A scheme is computationally secure when \emph{every} $\PPT$ adversary has only \emph{negligible} advantage as a function of the security parameter $n$: for every $\PPT$ algorithm $\mathcal A$ there is a negligible function $\negl$ such that $\Adv(\mathcal A) \le \negl(n)$. Every formal definition in later chapters -- hiding plaintexts under chosen-plaintext attack, preventing signature forgery, resisting hash collisions -- uses this template with a specific game plugged in.
\end{focusbox}

\section{One-way functions}
\label{ch08:owf}

A one-way function is the mathematical padlock: easy to apply, hard to undo, formalized with polynomial time and negligible probability. Write $\{0,1\}^{*}$ for the set of finite bit-strings of arbitrary length, and for any output $y$ write $f^{-1}(y) = \{x' \in \{0,1\}^{*} : f(x') = y\}$ for the set of \emph{preimages} of $y$ -- all inputs that map to $y$.

\begin{definition}[One-way function]
\label{ch08:def-owf}
A function $f : \{0,1\}^{*} \to \{0,1\}^{*}$ is \emph{one-way} if:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Easy to compute.} There is a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm that, given any bit-string $x$, outputs $f(x)$.
\item \textbf{Hard to invert.} For every $\PPT$ algorithm $\mathcal A$ there is a negligible function $\negl$ such that when an $n$-bit input $x \getsr \{0,1\}^{n}$ is chosen uniformly at random,
\[
\Pr_{x \getsr \{0,1\}^{n}}\bigl[\, \mathcal A\bigl(1^{n}, f(x)\bigr) \in f^{-1}\bigl(f(x)\bigr)\,\bigr] \;\le\; \negl(n),
\]
where the probability is taken over both the uniform choice of $x$ and $\mathcal A$'s internal coin tosses.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Three details in Definition~\ref{ch08:def-owf} close loopholes that would otherwise make the definition vacuous or unusable.

\begin{walkbox}
\emph{Why ``some preimage'' $x' \in f^{-1}(f(x))$ rather than the original $x$?} Because $f$ need not be injective (one-to-one). As Figure~\ref{ch08:fig-owf-preimages} shows, if $f(x) = f(x') = y$, any inverter that outputs $x'$ has inverted $y$. Demanding the exact $x$ that was sampled would ask $\mathcal A$ to recover information that $f(x)$ already erased; even an unbounded adversary fails at that when many inputs collide.

\medskip
\emph{Why is $x$ chosen uniformly at random?} One-wayness is an \emph{average-case} requirement, not a worst-case one. A problem that is hard only on rare pathological inputs is useless when keys are drawn at random; cryptography needs inversion to fail on almost every randomly chosen input.

\medskip
\emph{What is $1^{n}$ doing as an input to $\mathcal A$?} The notation $1^{n}$ means a string of $n$ ones -- the number $n$ written in \emph{unary}. Because a $\PPT$ algorithm's time budget is polynomial in the bit-length of its inputs, passing $1^{n}$ guarantees $\mathcal A$ gets time polynomial in $n$ even if $f(x)$ happens to be much shorter than $n$ bits.
\end{walkbox}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why both clauses are mandatory.} Drop ``easy to compute'' and legitimate users cannot evaluate $f(x)$ themselves. Drop ``hard to invert'' and there is no asymmetry left: $f(x) = x$ is easy to compute and trivial to invert, while the constant function $f(x) = 0$ is inverted on every valid output by returning any string at all (since every $x'$ satisfies $f(x') = 0 = f(x)$). Both clauses together create the one-way street.
\end{whybox}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Think of dropping a wine glass. Going from glass to shards takes a flick of the wrist. Going from shards back to an intact glass violates no law of mechanics, yet searching through the space of shard trajectories to reverse the smash is hopeless in practice. One-way functions do the digital equivalent: the forward map scrambles structure thoroughly enough that undoing it requires searching an astronomical space.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Candidate one-way functions}
\label{ch08:candidates}

\emph{Nobody has proved that one-way functions exist.} Proving they exist would immediately prove $\mathrm P \ne \mathrm{NP}$ (that fast-to-verify problems are not always fast to solve), which remains open. What we have instead are \emph{candidates}: concrete functions that have withstood decades of cryptanalysis and are conjectured to be one-way.

\begin{example}[Multiplication / the factoring candidate]
\label{ch08:ex-mult}
Define $f(p,q) = pq$ where $p$ and $q$ are random $n$-bit primes. Forward: integer multiplication in $O(n^2)$ time. Backward: given $N=pq$, recover $\{p,q\}$ -- integer factoring. With $p=61$ and $q=53$ we get $N=3233$; at $2048$ bits, inverting this map is the problem behind RSA.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Modular exponentiation / the discrete-log candidate]
\label{ch08:ex-exp}
Fix a large prime $p$ and a generator $g$ of $\Zns{p}$. Define $f(x) = g^{x} \bmod p$. Forward: repeated squaring in $O(n)$ modular multiplications. Backward: given $y=g^x \bmod p$, find $x$ -- the discrete logarithm problem. With $p=23$, $g=5$, and $x=6$ we get $y = 5^6 \bmod 23 = 8$; inverting means solving $5^{x}\equiv 8 \pmod{23}$ for $x$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A hash as a one-way function]
\label{ch08:ex-hash}
A cryptographic hash $\Hash:\{0,1\}^{*}\to\{0,1\}^{256}$ is designed to be \emph{preimage resistant}: given a digest $d$, finding any $m$ with $\Hash(m)=d$ should take roughly $2^{256}$ evaluations. Preimage resistance is one-wayness for $\Hash$. (Hashes also demand collision resistance, treated in Chapter~14.)
\end{example}

\begin{connectionbox}
Tracing Example~\ref{ch08:ex-exp} by hand shows the asymmetry in miniature. Forward in $\Zns{23}$ with $g=5$, each power multiplies the previous residue by $5$ modulo $23$:
\[
5^1 \equiv 5,\quad 5^2 \equiv 2,\quad 5^3 \equiv 10,\quad 5^4 \equiv 4,\quad 5^5 \equiv 20,\quad 5^6 \equiv 8 \pmod{23}.
\]
Backward, you are handed $8$ and have no obvious formula for its position in the scrambled sequence. For a prime $p$ of several hundred bits the cycle has more elements than atoms in the observable universe, and even cleverer algorithms like baby-step giant-step (worked out below) and Pollard's rho method still take exponential time in the bit-length of $p$. Appendix~A proves $5$ generates $\Zns{23}$, making $x \mapsto 5^x \bmod 23$ a bijection from $\{1,\dots,22\}$ onto $\Zns{23}$.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{implementbox}
A toy modular-exponentiation function and a brute-force inverter to see the cost gap directly. Real schemes use primes thousands of bits long; a $17$-bit prime like $65537$ is inverted in milliseconds.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
# LEARNING ONLY -- toy sizes, NOT secure. Do not use for anything real.
p = 65537          # a small prime (2^16 + 1)
g = 3              # a generator of the multiplicative group mod p
def f(x):
    # forward direction: O(log x) multiplications via repeated squaring
    return pow(g, x, p)
def invert_bruteforce(y):
    # backward direction: try every exponent until one matches.
    # Cost grows linearly in p (exponentially in the bit-length of p).
    for x in range(1, p):
        if pow(g, x, p) == y:
            return x
    return None
secret = 41234
y = f(secret)                       # fast: ~16 squarings
print("public value y =", y)
print("recovered x   =", invert_bruteforce(y))   # slow: scans up to p-1
\end{lstlisting}
Here \texttt{f} performs at most $32$ modular multiplications, whereas \texttt{invert\_bruteforce} loops up to $p - 1$ times. Scale $p$ to $2048$ bits and \texttt{f} still finishes in milliseconds while the loop becomes physically impossible.
\end{implementbox}

The brute-force loop tries up to $m = p - 1$ exponents. Discrete log is only \emph{conjectured} hard because better algorithms cut that exponent in half. Running Daniel Shanks's \emph{baby-step giant-step} algorithm by hand shows both that the forward/backward gap remains exponential and why group sizes must be doubled against square-root attacks.

\begin{labbox}
\textbf{Baby-step giant-step on a tiny group.} Let us solve $5^{x}\equiv 9 \pmod{23}$ in $\Zns{23}$ without scanning all $m = 22$ elements (recall the \emph{order} $m = |\Zns{23}| = 22$ is the number of group elements). Set the stride $t = \ceil{\sqrt{22}} = 5$, where $\ceil{u}$ denotes $u$ rounded up to the next integer. Any exponent $x \in \{0,\dots,21\}$ can be written in base-$t$ division form as $x = it + j$ with $0 \le j < t$ and $0 \le i < t$. Rewriting $5^{it+j} \equiv 9 \pmod{23}$ by multiplying both sides by $(5^{-t})^{i}$ separates $i$ and $j$:
\[
5^{it + j} \equiv 9 \pmod{23} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 5^{j} \equiv 9\cdot (5^{-t})^{i} \pmod{23}.
\]

\emph{Baby steps.} Compute the left-hand side $5^{j}\bmod 23$ for $j = 0,1,2,3,4$:
\[
5^{0} = 1,\quad 5^{1} = 5,\quad 5^{2} \equiv 2,\quad 5^{3} \equiv 10,\quad 5^{4} \equiv 4 \pmod{23}.
\]
Store the lookup table $\{(1,0),\,(5,1),\,(2,2),\,(10,3),\,(4,4)\}$ indexed by the residue $5^j$.

\medskip
\emph{Giant steps.} The backward stride factor is $5^{-t} = 5^{-5}\bmod 23$. Since $5^{5}\equiv 20 \pmod{23}$ and $20\cdot 15 = 300 = 13\cdot 23 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{23}$, we have $5^{-5} \equiv 15 \pmod{23}$. Now step $i = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ and check the baby table after each multiplication by $15$:
\begin{align*}
  i = 0 &: \quad 9\cdot 15^{0} \equiv 9 \pmod{23} && \text{(not in table)}, \\
  i = 1 &: \quad 9\cdot 15^{1} = 135 \equiv 20 \pmod{23} && \text{(not in table)}, \\
  i = 2 &: \quad 20\cdot 15 = 300 \equiv 1 \pmod{23} && \text{(match: } 1 = 5^{0}\text{, so } j = 0\text{)}.
\end{align*}
Matching at $i = 2$ and $j = 0$ gives $x = it + j = 2\cdot 5 + 0 = 10$ (Figure~\ref{ch08:fig-bsgs}).

\medskip
\emph{Check.} Since $5^{5}\equiv 20 \pmod{23}$, we have $5^{10}\equiv 20^{2} = 400 = 17\cdot 23 + 9 \equiv 9 \pmod{23}$.

\medskip
We found $x = 10$ using $5$ baby steps and $3$ giant-step checks ($O(\sqrt{m})$ work and table entries in general, rather than $O(m)$). For a $256$-bit group of order $m \approx 2^{256}$, $\sqrt{m} \approx 2^{128}$ is still out of reach, which is why $128$-bit security requires a $256$-bit group rather than a $128$-bit one.
\end{labbox}

\section{Trapdoors: a hidden shortcut}
\label{ch08:trapdoor}

A plain one-way function locks the door for everyone, including the person who set it up. That works well for password storage (store $\Hash(\text{password})$ so even a leaked database hides the password), yet fails for encrypted messaging: if Bob cannot invert the function either, he cannot read Alice's message. Public-key encryption needs a function that is hard to invert in general, yet fast to invert for whoever holds a secret piece of side information called a \emph{trapdoor}.

\begin{definition}[Trapdoor one-way function]
\label{ch08:def-tdf}
A \emph{trapdoor one-way function} is a family of functions $\{f_{pk} : \mathcal{D}_{pk} \to \mathcal{R}_{pk}\}$ together with a $\PPT$ key-generation algorithm $\Gen$ that, on input $1^{n}$, outputs a pair $(pk, td)$ -- a public key (or index) $pk$ and a secret trapdoor $td$ -- such that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Easy forward.} Given $pk$ and $x \in \mathcal{D}_{pk}$, one can sample $x \getsr \mathcal{D}_{pk}$ and compute $f_{pk}(x)$ in polynomial time.
\item \textbf{One-way without $td$.} For every $\PPT$ adversary $\mathcal A$ there is a negligible function $\negl$ such that for $(pk, td) \gets \Gen(1^{n})$ and $x \getsr \mathcal{D}_{pk}$,
\[
\Pr\bigl[\, \mathcal A\bigl(1^{n}, pk, f_{pk}(x)\bigr) \in f_{pk}^{-1}\bigl(f_{pk}(x)\bigr) \,\bigr] \;\le\; \negl(n).
\]
\item \textbf{Easy backward with $td$.} There is a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm that, given $td$ and $y = f_{pk}(x)$, outputs a preimage in $f_{pk}^{-1}(y)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Figure~\ref{ch08:fig-trapdoor} contrasts the three paths. A plain one-way function is a padlock with no keyhole: anyone can snap it shut, nobody reopens it. A trapdoor function adds the keyhole: anyone holding $pk$ snaps it shut, while only the holder of $td$ opens it. Publishing open padlocks while keeping the key is the mechanism of public-key encryption.

\begin{intuitionbox}
RSA (Chapter~19) is the classic trapdoor. The public key is $pk = (N, e)$ with $N = pq$; anyone can evaluate $f_{pk}(x) = x^{e}\bmod N$. The trapdoor is the prime pair $td = (p, q)$, which lets the owner compute $\varphi(N) = (p-1)(q-1)$ and the inverse exponent $d \equiv e^{-1} \pmod{\varphi(N)}$ that undoes the $e$-th power in milliseconds. With $p=61$ and $q=53$ ($N=3233$), knowing $(p,q)$ makes decryption fast, whereas knowing only $(N, e)$ leaves an eavesdropper trying to factor $N$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\section{Reductions: turning hardness into security}
\label{ch08:reductions}

We have conjectured-hard math problems (factoring, discrete log) and we want provably secure schemes. The bridge between them is a \emph{reduction}, used in nearly every security proof in the book. Its logic is \emph{contraposition}: proving ``if $Y$ is hard, then scheme $X$ is secure'' by proving the logically equivalent statement ``if scheme $X$ can be broken, then $Y$ can be solved.''

Directly listing every conceivable attack on $X$ is impossible. Assuming a hypothetical attacker exists and using it as a tool is concrete.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The four steps of a security reduction.} To prove ``scheme $X$ is secure assuming problem $Y$ is hard'':
\begin{enumerate}
\item Suppose, toward a contradiction, that some $\PPT$ adversary $\mathcal A$ breaks $X$ with noticeable advantage $\eps(n)$.
\item Construct a $\PPT$ algorithm $\mathcal B$ (the \emph{reduction}) that calls $\mathcal A$ as a subroutine to solve a given instance of $Y$.
\item Prove that $\mathcal B$ runs in polynomial time and solves $Y$ with noticeable probability (for instance $\eps(n)/p(n)$ for some polynomial $p$).
\item Conclude that a $\PPT$ solver for $Y$ exists, contradicting the assumption that $Y$ is hard. Therefore no such $\mathcal A$ exists, and $X$ is secure.
\end{enumerate}
In short: \emph{breaking $X$ lets you solve $Y$}; if $Y$ has no efficient solver, $X$ has no efficient attack.
\end{focusbox}

Step~2 is where the craft lies (Figure~\ref{ch08:fig-reduction}). Given a hard puzzle $y \in Y$, $\mathcal B$ crafts a simulated challenge for scheme $X$ that embeds $y$ and looks indistinguishable from a real run of $X$. $\mathcal B$ never inspects $\mathcal A$'s internal code -- it treats $\mathcal A$ as an \emph{oracle} (a black box that takes inputs and returns outputs) and algebraically translates $\mathcal A$'s winning answer on $X$ into the solution to $y$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]

\begin{example}[A worked reduction: square roots are as hard as factoring]
\label{ch08:ex-rabin}
Let $N=pq$ with $p$ and $q$ distinct odd primes, and consider $f(x)=x^{2}\bmod N$ (the basis of Michael Rabin's 1979 trapdoor scheme). Any nonzero residue of the form $y \equiv r^2 \pmod N$ with $\gcd(r,N)=1$ is called a \emph{quadratic residue} modulo $N$. We show: \emph{if you can compute modular square roots modulo $N$, you can factor $N$.}

Suppose $\mathcal A(N,y)$ returns a square root of $y$ modulo $N$ whenever one exists. Build $\mathcal B$ to factor $N$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Pick a random $r \in \{1,\dots,N-1\}$. If $\gcd(r,N) > 1$, return that $\gcd$ immediately -- it is already $p$ or $q$.
\item Otherwise compute $y = r^{2}\bmod N$ and call $s \gets \mathcal A(N,y)$, which satisfies $s^{2}\equiv y \equiv r^{2} \pmod N$.
\item Because $s^{2} - r^{2} = (s-r)(s+r)$ is a multiple of $N = pq$, the primes $p$ and $q$ must divide $(s-r)(s+r)$. By the Chinese Remainder Theorem (Chapter~4), $y$ has \emph{four} square roots modulo $N$, corresponding to $(\pm r \bmod p,\; \pm r \bmod q)$. Because $y = r^2 \bmod N$ contains zero information about which of those four roots $\mathcal B$ started with, $\mathcal A$ returns a mixed-sign root $s \not\equiv \pm r \pmod N$ with probability exactly $\tfrac{1}{2}$. Whenever $s \not\equiv \pm r \pmod N$, neither $s - r$ nor $s + r$ is divisible by both $p$ and $q$, so one prime divides $s - r$ and the other divides $s + r$ -- meaning $\gcd(s - r, N)$ equals $p$ or $q$.
\item If $s \equiv \pm r \pmod N$, repeat with a fresh random $r$. After $k$ independent tries the failure probability is $2^{-k}$, negligible for $k = n$.
\end{enumerate}
Test it on $N=3233=61\cdot 53$. Pick $r=2$, giving $y = 4$. The four square roots of $4$ modulo $3233$ are $2$, $1588$, $1645$, and $3231$ (Figure~\ref{ch08:fig-rabin-crt}). If $\mathcal A$ returns $s = 1645$ (which satisfies $1645 \equiv -2 \pmod{61}$ and $1645 \equiv +2 \pmod{53}$), then $s - r = 1643$ is divisible by $53$ and not by $61$, so $\gcd(1643, 3233) = 53$ exposes $q = 53$ in one Euclidean-algorithm call. \emph{Breaking $X$ (finding square roots) solved $Y$ (factoring).}
\end{example}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{What the reduction did and did not prove.} Example~\ref{ch08:ex-rabin} proved that computing square roots modulo $N$ is \emph{at least as hard} as factoring $N$: any efficient root-finder yields an efficient factorizer. It did \emph{not} prove factoring is hard -- that remains an empirical assumption backed by half a century of failed attacks. Reductions connect problems in a chain (\emph{scheme} reduces to \emph{root-finding} reduces to \emph{factoring}); the anchor at the bottom is always a conjectured-hard problem.
\end{whybox}

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Tightness matters.} Quantitative loss in a reduction determines concrete key sizes. If breaking $X$ with advantage $\eps$ after $q$ queries yields a $Y$-solver succeeding with probability $\eps/q^{2}$, then an attacker with $\eps = 1/2$ and $q = 2^{40}$ queries gives a $Y$-solver succeeding only $2^{-81}$ of the time -- weak leverage unless $Y$ is chosen much larger. A \emph{tight} reduction loses only a small constant factor, whereas a \emph{loose} reduction forces larger keys to compensate for the $q^2$ loss. Whenever you see ``provably secure,'' ask both \emph{under which assumption} and \emph{with what tightness loss}.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{When the assumption is the weak link.} A reduction guarantees ``no efficient attack on $X$ unless $Y$ is easy.'' If $Y$ turns out to be easy in the setting you chose, the theorem stays true while the scheme collapses:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Discrete log in weak groups.} If someone picks a prime $p$ for which $|\Zns{p}| = p - 1$ factors completely into small primes, the \emph{Pohlig--Hellman algorithm} solves discrete log mod each small prime power separately and reassembles $x$ via the Chinese Remainder Theorem in milliseconds. The reduction ``secure if discrete log is hard in $\Zns{p}$'' had no bug; discrete log was not hard for that $p$. Choosing a \emph{safe prime} $p = 2q + 1$ where $q$ is also prime leaves a large prime factor $q$ in $p - 1$ and blocks the shortcut.
\item \emph{Quantum computers.} Peter Shor's 1994 algorithm solves both integer factoring and discrete logarithms in polynomial time on a sufficiently large quantum computer. Every classical reduction to those problems remains valid, yet the underlying hardness assumption fails against a quantum adversary. Post-quantum cryptography (Chapter~35) swaps out the anchor for problems on high-dimensional geometric grids (\emph{lattices}) and \emph{error-correcting codes}, which resist known quantum algorithms.
\end{itemize}
A security proof is conditional: verify that its assumption holds for your parameters and threat model.
\end{attackbox}

\section{Standard hardness assumptions}
\label{ch08:landscape}

Later chapters build public-key schemes from five standard number-theoretic hardness assumptions.

\begin{definition}[Foundational hardness assumptions]
\label{ch08:def-assumptions}
\leavevmode
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Factoring.} Given $N=pq$ for unknown random $n$-bit primes $p$ and $q$, find $p$ and $q$. Best known classical method: GNFS, sub-exponential time. \emph{Powers:} RSA, Rabin.
\item \textbf{RSA problem.} Given $(N,e,y)$ with $\gcd(e, \varphi(N)) = 1$ and $y \getsr \Zns{N}$, find the unique $x \in \Zns{N}$ satisfying $x^{e}\equiv y \pmod N$ -- that is, invert the permutation $x \mapsto x^e \bmod N$ without the prime factors. No harder than factoring (factor $N$, compute $d \equiv e^{-1} \pmod{\varphi(N)}$, and output $y^d \bmod N$), and possibly easier, though no faster method is known. \emph{Powers:} RSA encryption and signatures.
\item \textbf{Discrete logarithm (DLP).} In a cyclic group $\G=\langle g\rangle$ of order $m$, given $y=g^{x}$ for random $x \getsr \{0,\dots,m-1\}$, find $x$. \emph{Powers:} Diffie--Hellman, ElGamal, the Digital Signature Algorithm (DSA), and -- in elliptic-curve groups -- Elliptic-Curve Diffie--Hellman (ECDH) and the Elliptic-Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA).
\item \textbf{Computational Diffie--Hellman (CDH).} In $\G = \langle g \rangle$, given $g^{a}$ and $g^{b}$ for random $a, b$, compute the group element $g^{ab}$. No harder than DLP (find $a$ from $g^a$, then compute $(g^b)^a = g^{ab}$). \emph{Powers:} key exchange.
\item \textbf{Decisional Diffie--Hellman (DDH).} In $\G = \langle g \rangle$, distinguish a real Diffie--Hellman triple $(g^a, g^b, g^{ab})$ from a random triple $(g^a, g^b, g^c)$ where $a, b, c$ are chosen independently at random. Unlike CDH, this is a \emph{decision} problem (a yes/no distinguishing question rather than computing a value); in some groups DDH is easy even where CDH appears hard, so group choice matters. \emph{Powers:} security proofs for ElGamal encryption and authenticated key exchange.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

Short reductions order these problems by difficulty (Figure~\ref{ch08:fig-assumptions-hierarchy}). Writing $A \le B$ to mean ``$A$ is no harder than $B$'' (an oracle for $B$ yields an efficient algorithm for $A$), we have $\text{DDH} \le \text{CDH} \le \text{DLP}$ on the discrete-log side, and $\text{RSA} \le \text{Factoring} \equiv \text{Square Roots mod } N$ on the factoring side. Whether $\text{CDH}$ equals $\text{DLP}$, or $\text{RSA}$ equals $\text{Factoring}$, remains open. Proving a scheme secure under a \emph{weaker} assumption (such as $\text{CDH}$ rather than $\text{DLP}$, or $\text{DDH}$ only in prime-order groups) gives a safer guarantee because fewer things can go wrong.

\begin{connectionbox}
The two cost analyses behind recommended key sizes come from the companion books. The algorithms book derives GNFS's sub-exponential running time for factoring and the $O(\sqrt{|\G|})$ cost of generic discrete-log algorithms (baby-step giant-step, Pollard's rho), where the square root arises from the birthday bound of Chapter~7. Because well-chosen elliptic-curve groups have no known sub-exponential attack, a $256$-bit elliptic-curve group ($\sqrt{|\G|} \approx 2^{128}$) and a $3072$-bit RSA modulus require comparable attack effort of roughly $2^{128}$ operations.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Where this lands in practice.} Opening an HTTPS connection runs an ECDH handshake (typically on curve \texttt{X25519}), whose secrecy rests on CDH/DDH in an elliptic-curve group, while the server's certificate signature uses RSA or ECDSA, resting on factoring or elliptic-curve discrete log. Password databases store slow, \emph{salted} hashes (\texttt{argon2} or \texttt{bcrypt}, which mix each password with a fresh per-user random string called a \emph{salt} before hashing so identical passwords do not produce identical digests). WPA3 WiFi uses a Diffie--Hellman handshake so recorded traffic resists offline dictionary attacks, and Signal's double ratchet chains fresh ECDH exchanges so a compromised key today cannot decrypt yesterday's messages. In each protocol, choose key sizes from current standards (or a vetted library's defaults), never from a textbook's toy examples.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Putting the pieces together}
\label{ch08:summary}

Because unconditional secrecy demands keys as long as everything we send, practical cryptography targets security against \emph{efficient} adversaries. Efficiency means polynomial time in the security parameter $n$, making the attacker a randomized $\PPT$ algorithm. A scheme is broken if some $\PPT$ adversary achieves \emph{noticeable} advantage over blind guessing, and secure if every $\PPT$ adversary's advantage is \emph{negligible} -- shrinking faster than $1/n^c$ for every $c$. Defining ``too small to matter'' asymptotically ensures that polynomial repetition and union bounds never turn a negligible risk into a noticeable one.

Within that framework, a \emph{one-way function} is easy to evaluate and hard to invert on random inputs, while a \emph{trapdoor one-way function} adds a secret key $td$ that restores fast inversion for its owner -- the blueprint for public-key encryption. Because proving one-way functions exist would settle $\mathrm{P} \ne \mathrm{NP}$, real systems rely on number-theoretic and algebraic candidates. A \emph{reduction} links a scheme to such a candidate by contraposition: showing that any $\PPT$ break of the scheme converts into a $\PPT$ solver for the underlying hard problem.

The rest of the book puts this framework to work. Chapter~9 proves Shannon's one-time-pad barrier, motivating Part~II's stream ciphers, block ciphers, hashes, and message authentication codes. Part~III builds key exchange, public-key encryption, and signatures from factoring, discrete logs, and elliptic curves, and Part~IV assembles them into TLS, WPA3, Signal, and SSH. Whenever a later theorem states ``secure assuming DDH'' or ``any forger inverts RSA,'' its meaning is the contract built here: for every $\PPT$ adversary, a reduction bounds the advantage by a negligible function of $n$.

\begin{recoverybox}
Before the exercises, self-check. Can you (1) say why we measure time in the input's \emph{bit-length}, and (2) decide whether $n^{-\log_2 n}$ is negligible and justify it? Can you (3) state the two clauses of a one-way function and the loophole each closes, (4) explain what a trapdoor adds and why publishing $pk$ is safe, and (5) recite the four steps of a reduction and the contradiction it reaches? If any of these is fuzzy, reread that section -- the rest of the book assumes all five.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch08:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Negligible or noticeable]
\label{ch08:ex-1}
For each function of $n$ (with logarithms in base $2$), decide whether it is negligible and justify your answer using Definition~\ref{ch08:def-negl}:
\[
\text{(a)}\; 2^{-\sqrt{n}}, \qquad
\text{(b)}\; \frac{1}{n^{10}+5n}, \qquad
\text{(c)}\; \frac{n^{3}}{2^{n}}, \qquad
\text{(d)}\; \frac{1}{(\log_2 n)^{n}}, \qquad
\text{(e)}\; 2^{-n} + n^{-2}.
\]
For the noticeable ones, exhibit a constant $c$ for which the function exceeds $1/n^{c}$ infinitely often.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Closure properties]
\label{ch08:ex-2}
Prove from the definition that if $\negl_1$ and $\negl_2$ are negligible then so is $\negl_1 + \negl_2$. Then show that for any fixed polynomial $p$, $p(n)\cdot\negl_1(n)$ is negligible. Finally, give a counterexample showing that the \emph{quotient} $\negl_1(n)/\negl_2(n)$ need not be negligible (indeed need not even be bounded).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Advantage arithmetic]
\label{ch08:ex-3}
A distinguisher $\mathcal D$ outputs its correct guess of the hidden bit $b$ with probability $\Pr[b'=b] = 0.62$. (a) What is its advantage? (b) A second distinguisher achieves advantage $0.001$; what is its $\Pr[b'=b]$? (c) Suppose a cipher leaks one specific plaintext bit with probability $1/16$ and otherwise looks uniform. Following Example~\ref{ch08:ex-adv}, compute the advantage of the obvious distinguisher.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Trace the toy one-way function]
\label{ch08:ex-4}
Work in $\Zns{23}$ with generator $g=5$. (a) Compute $5^{1},\dots,5^{11} \bmod 23$ by hand, each as ``previous $\times 5 \bmod 23$.'' (b) Use your table to solve the discrete log $5^{x}\equiv 9 \pmod{23}$. (c) Roughly how many table entries would the analogous brute-force search scan for a 256-bit modulus, and why does that make the forward/backward asymmetry usable?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Break a bad one-way function]
\label{ch08:ex-5}
Consider $f(x) = (3x + 7) \bmod 2^{n}$, claimed by a vendor to be one-way on $n$-bit inputs. Show it is \emph{not} one-way by giving an explicit polynomial-time inverter. (Hint: $3$ is invertible modulo $2^{n}$ because $\gcd(3,2^{n})=1$; find $3^{-1} \bmod 2^{n}$ and solve for $x$.) State the general lesson about why \emph{linear} functions can never be one-way.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A reduction by hand]
\label{ch08:ex-6}
Carry out the factoring-from-square-roots reduction of Example~\ref{ch08:ex-rabin} concretely with $N = 77 = 7 \cdot 11$. (a) List all four square roots of $y = 4$ modulo $77$ (use the Chinese Remainder structure: a root is $\pm 2 \bmod 7$ combined with $\pm 2 \bmod 11$). (b) Pick $r = 2$ and a returned root $s \ne \pm 2$; compute $\gcd(s - 2, 77)$ and verify you recover a prime factor. (c) Explain why the reduction fails to learn anything when $\mathcal A$ returns $s = \pm r$, and why retrying fixes this.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Order the assumptions]
\label{ch08:ex-7}
Using only the reduction idea ``an algorithm for the harder problem yields one for the easier,'' prove the two relations $\text{CDH} \le \text{DLP}$ and $\text{DDH} \le \text{CDH}$. That is: (a) show a discrete-log oracle lets you solve CDH; (b) show a CDH oracle lets you solve DDH. Conclude that a scheme proven secure under DDH is on weaker (hence safer) ground than one needing DLP to be hard.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Why the assumption can fail]
\label{ch08:ex-8}
A protocol designer proves their scheme secure ``assuming discrete log is hard in $\Zns{p}$'' and then picks $p$ such that $p - 1 = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdots$ is a product of small primes. (a) Without computing it in full, explain why Pohlig--Hellman makes discrete log in this group easy, by reducing it to discrete logs in the small prime-order subgroups. (b) Is the security \emph{proof} wrong? Argue precisely what broke -- the proof or the instantiation -- and what ``safe prime'' choice repairs it.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Trapdoor, conceptually]
\label{ch08:ex-9}
Explain in your own words, with the padlock analogy, why a \emph{plain} one-way function is enough to store passwords safely but \emph{not} enough to let a stranger send you a private message, whereas a \emph{trapdoor} one-way function enables both. Then identify, for RSA's trapdoor $f(x)=x^e \bmod N$, exactly which piece of data is the public index $pk$ and which is the trapdoor $td$, and why publishing $pk$ does not help an attacker.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Baby-step giant-step by hand]
\label{ch08:ex-11}
Reusing the method from the lab box, solve $5^{x}\equiv 6 \pmod{23}$ in $\Zns{23}$. (a) Reuse the baby table $\{5^{0},\dots,5^{4}\}$ and the giant stride $15$; tabulate $6\cdot 15^{i}\bmod 23$ until you hit a stored power, and read off $x = it + j$. (b) Verify by computing $5^{x}\bmod 23$. (c) You did at most $5+5 = 10$ multiplications; the brute-force loop would do up to $22$. State the general count, $O(\sqrt m)$ versus $O(m)$, and explain why this forces a $256$-bit group for $128$-bit security.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Find the crossover]
\label{ch08:ex-12}
Consider the negligible function $2^{-n}$ against the polynomial $1/n^{5}$. (a) By trial (or a few lines of code) find the smallest integer $n$ at which $2^{-n} < 1/n^{5}$, and confirm the inequality never flips back. (b) Explain why raising the polynomial to $1/n^{50}$ only moves this crossover to a larger $n$ rather than making $2^{-n}$ non-negligible. (c) Contrast with $1/n^{5}$ versus $1/n^{6}$: why do \emph{two} polynomials never settle the question of negligibility between them?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Tightness costs key bits]
\label{ch08:ex-10}
A scheme has a reduction showing that an adversary with advantage $\eps$ making $q$ queries yields a factoring algorithm succeeding with probability $\eps / q^{2}$. Suppose a realistic adversary has $\eps = 1$ and $q = 2^{40}$. (a) With what probability does the derived factorizer succeed, and why is that ``weak'' evidence of security at a modulus where factoring costs about $2^{112}$? (b) Argue qualitatively why a \emph{loose} reduction pushes you toward \emph{larger} key sizes than a tight one would, to keep the guaranteed security level fixed.
\end{exercise}
