\chapter{The Public-Key Idea and Diffie-Hellman}

In Chapter 17 we saw how to harvest physical entropy and stretch it into unpredictable random keys. Yet throughout Parts I and II, every system we constructed rested on an unstated assumption: Alice and Bob already share a secret. The one-time pad needs a shared pad. AES needs a shared key. HMAC needs a shared MAC key. Authenticated encryption needs both. Every one of these schemes is a machine that turns a small shared secret into confidentiality and integrity for a large conversation. None of them tells you how the shared secret got there in the first place.

This gap was the central practical problem of twentieth-century cryptography, and for most of that century the only known answer was physical: meet in person, or trust a courier. If you want to talk securely with $n$ people, you need $n$ separate keys, each one established over an \emph{out-of-band} channel -- a separate, trusted path outside the network you want to protect, such as an in-person handoff. A bank with a million customers needs a million keys, each delivered beforehand. Scale that to a planet of strangers buying things from each other online, and the courier model collapses. You cannot fly a briefcase to every website you have never visited before.

How can two people who have never met, communicating entirely over a wire that an eavesdropper reads in full, agree on a secret that the eavesdropper cannot compute? That sounds like two people shouting numbers across a crowded room and ending up with a secret nobody else in the room knows. Yet modular arithmetic makes it work. The construction is Diffie--Hellman key exchange, and modern secure communication depends on it: when your browser opens a TLS connection, when two phones set up a Signal session, or when an SSH client greets a server it has never spoken to, a descendant of Diffie--Hellman runs in the first few milliseconds.

\section{The key-distribution problem}
\label{ch18:key-distribution}

As before, Alice wants to talk to Bob, and Eve sits on the wire and reads every bit that passes. Now suppose Alice and Bob have \emph{never met}. They share no key, no pad, no password. They have a public channel and nothing else.

With only the tools of the previous chapters, this is hopeless. Symmetric encryption uses the same key to lock and unlock; if Alice sends that key to Bob over the wire, Eve reads it. Encrypting the key under another key only pushes the problem back a step: how did they agree on \emph{that} key? The recursion never bottoms out. Information-theoretically, if every bit Alice sends is visible to Eve, then anything Bob can compute from those bits alone, Eve can compute too. Bob and Eve are looking at the same \emph{transcript} -- the complete log of messages sent across the channel.

\begin{intuitionbox}
The key-distribution problem in one sentence: Bob and Eve see the exact same messages, so how can Bob end up knowing something Eve does not?

Bob holds something Eve lacks: a secret number he chose locally and never sent over the wire. Both Alice and Bob keep a private number to themselves and broadcast only a one-way function of it. Their two private numbers combine into a single shared value, and computing that value requires holding at least one of the private numbers. Eve holds neither.
\end{intuitionbox}

Before the 1970s, the key count made large networks unmanageable (Figure~\ref{ch18:fig-pairwise-keys}). A network of $n$ parties who all want to talk directly with each other needs a distinct key for every pair, which is
\[
  \binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}
\]
keys. For $n = 1{,}000$ that is $499{,}500$ keys, each established out of band and stored securely. Add one new member and you must distribute $n$ fresh keys to the existing members. Militaries and embassies lived with this using couriers and tamper-evident diplomatic pouches, whereas commerce and the open internet cannot.

\begin{historybox}
In 1974 Ralph Merkle conceived cryptographic ``puzzles'' in which legitimate parties performed $N$ steps of work while an eavesdropper needed $N^2$ steps -- a quadratic gap. Building on that direction, Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman published ``New Directions in Cryptography'' in 1976, laying out the public-key concept and the key-exchange protocol that now bears their names (Hellman has long argued the method should be called Diffie--Hellman--Merkle). The paper opens with a famous prediction: ``We stand today on the brink of a revolution in cryptography.'' Open publication sparked a decade-long clash with the US National Security Agency (NSA) over academic freedom and export controls.

Unknown outside the British Government Communications Headquarters (GCHQ), James Ellis, Clifford Cocks, and Malcolm Williamson had already discovered ``non-secret encryption'' (their term for public-key cryptography) and a Diffie--Hellman-like exchange between 1969 and 1973. Their work remained classified until 1997. The idea was invented twice: once in secret, where it stayed in a vault, and once in the open, where anyone could build on it.
\end{historybox}

\section{The intuition: mixing paint}
\label{ch18:paint}

An analogy with paint captures the mechanism before any algebra: the protocol is this picture translated into numbers.

Suppose mixing two colors of paint is easy, while \emph{un}-mixing -- recovering the original colors from a blend -- is hard. Diffie--Hellman rests on that asymmetry (Figure~\ref{ch18:fig-paint}).

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Alice and Bob publicly agree on a common starting color, say a particular yellow. Everyone, including Eve, sees this yellow.
  \item Alice picks a secret color, say red, and keeps it private. She mixes yellow~$+$~red into an orange blend and sends the orange to Bob over the public channel. Eve sees the orange but cannot un-mix it to recover Alice's red.
  \item Bob picks a secret color, say blue, keeps it private, mixes yellow~$+$~blue into a green blend, and sends the green to Alice. Eve sees the green but cannot extract Bob's blue.
  \item Alice stirs her secret red into the received green, while Bob stirs his secret blue into the received orange. Both arrive at the same three-color blend: \emph{yellow~$+$~red~$+$~blue}.
\end{enumerate}

Alice and Bob now share that final blend. Eve, watching the whole exchange, holds the public yellow, the orange, and the green. To reach the shared blend she would need to add either Alice's red or Bob's blue -- secret colors that were never sent in the clear, only mixed into blends she cannot pull apart.

We now need an arithmetic operation that behaves like paint: fast to apply, hard to invert, and independent of mixing order so that Alice's ``green then red'' equals Bob's ``orange then blue.'' Modular exponentiation provides that operation, and the order-independence comes from the law of exponents.

\section{Diffie--Hellman key exchange}
\label{ch18:dh-protocol}

We work in a cyclic group -- here, the nonzero residues modulo a prime under multiplication. Fix a large prime $p$ and a generator $g$ of the multiplicative group $\Zns{p} = \{1, 2, \dots, p-1\}$. Recall from Chapters~3 and~5 that a generator is an element whose powers $g^1, g^2, g^3, \dots$ cycle through every nonzero residue modulo $p$ before repeating; the group has order $p-1$, so $g^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$ and no smaller positive exponent gives $1$. Both $p$ and $g$ are \emph{public}. Eve knows them, and millions of connections can share the same standardized $(p, g)$ pair.

``Mixing the public color with a secret'' becomes ``raising $g$ to a secret exponent modulo $p$.''

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Diffie--Hellman key exchange.} Public parameters: a prime $p$ and a generator $g$ of $\Zns{p}$.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Alice samples a uniform secret exponent $a \getsr \{2, \dots, p-2\}$ (excluding $0, 1,$ and $p-1$, which yield the trivial public values $1$ or $g$), computes $A = g^a \bmod p$, and sends $A$ to Bob.
  \item Bob samples a uniform secret exponent $b \getsr \{2, \dots, p-2\}$, computes $B = g^b \bmod p$, and sends $B$ to Alice.
  \item Alice computes $s = B^a \bmod p$. Bob computes $s = A^b \bmod p$.
\end{enumerate}
Both arrive at the same value $s = g^{ab} \bmod p$, the shared secret.
\end{focusbox}

Correctness follows in one line from the laws of exponents:
\[
  B^a \equiv (g^b)^a = g^{ba} = g^{ab} = (g^a)^b \equiv A^b \pmod p.
\]
Alice's secret $a$ plays the role of her red paint, Bob's secret $b$ is his blue, $g$ is the public yellow, and $A, B$ are the two-color blends sent across the channel. The shared residue $g^{ab} \bmod p$ is the three-color blend, and integer multiplication's commutativity $ba = ab$ provides the order-independence the paint story needed. Eve sees $p$, $g$, $A = g^a \bmod p$, and $B = g^b \bmod p$; security rests on the claim that computing $g^{ab} \bmod p$ from those public values is infeasible.

\begin{whybox}
Why can Eve not read off $a$ from $A = g^a \bmod p$? Over the real numbers, $x \mapsto 5^x$ grows smoothly, so binary search or a logarithm recovers $x$ from $5^x$ right away. Modulo $p$, wrapping around $p$ folds and scatters the powers across $\{1, \dots, p-1\}$ with no visible order (Figure~\ref{ch18:fig-dlog-scramble}). Inverting modular exponentiation -- finding $a$ given $g$, $p$, and $g^a \bmod p$ -- is the \emph{discrete logarithm problem}. Forward evaluation takes a few thousand multiplications via square-and-multiply (Chapter~3); the fastest known general algorithms in the reverse direction grow sub-exponentially in the bit-length of $p$ (Chapter~8), putting inversion far beyond reach once $p$ has $2{,}048$ bits.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{A worked example with tiny numbers}
\label{ch18:tiny}

Real Diffie--Hellman uses primes thousands of bits long. To verify each step by hand, shrink the parameters to $p = 23$ and $g = 5$.

First check that $5$ generates $\Zns{23}$. The group has order $p - 1 = 22$; by Lagrange's theorem (Chapter~5), the order of $5$ must divide $22$, so $\ord_{23}(5) \in \{1, 2, 11, 22\}$. Repeated squaring modulo $23$ gives
\begin{align*}
  5^2 &= 25 \equiv 2 \pmod{23}, \\
  5^4 &\equiv 2^2 = 4 \pmod{23}, \\
  5^8 &\equiv 4^2 = 16 \pmod{23}, \\
  5^{11} &= 5^8 \cdot 5^2 \cdot 5^1 \equiv 16 \cdot 2 \cdot 5 = 160 = 6 \cdot 23 + 22 \equiv -1 \pmod{23}.
\end{align*}
Since $5^1 \not\equiv 1$, $5^2 \not\equiv 1$, and $5^{11} \not\equiv 1 \pmod{23}$, the order of $5$ cannot be $1$, $2$, or $11$; it must be $22$, so $5$ generates $\Zns{23}$. Figure~\ref{ch18:fig-dlog-scramble} plots all $22$ powers $5^x \bmod 23$.

Now run the protocol (summarized in Figure~\ref{ch18:fig-flow}):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Alice chooses $a = 6$.} Using $5^4 \equiv 4$ and $5^2 \equiv 2 \pmod{23}$ from above, she computes
  \[
    A = 5^6 \bmod 23 = (5^4 \cdot 5^2) \bmod 23 = (4 \cdot 2) \bmod 23 = 8,
  \]
  and sends $A = 8$ to Bob.
  \item \textbf{Bob chooses $b = 15$.} Writing $15 = 8 + 4 + 2 + 1$ in binary and reducing modulo $23$ at each multiplication, he computes
  \begin{align*}
    5^{15} &= 5^8 \cdot 5^4 \cdot 5^2 \cdot 5^1 \\
           &\equiv (16 \cdot 4) \cdot 2 \cdot 5 \equiv 18 \cdot 2 \cdot 5 &&\text{(since $64 = 2 \cdot 23 + 18$)} \\
           &\equiv 13 \cdot 5 = 65 \equiv 19 \pmod{23} &&\text{(since $36 \equiv 13$ and $65 = 2 \cdot 23 + 19$)},
  \end{align*}
  and sends $B = 19$ to Alice.
  \item \textbf{Alice derives $s = B^a \bmod 23 = 19^6 \bmod 23$.} Using $19 \equiv -4 \pmod{23}$,
  \begin{align*}
    19^6 &\equiv (-4)^6 = (4^3)^2 = 64^2 \\
         &\equiv 18^2 = 324 = 14 \cdot 23 + 2 \equiv 2 \pmod{23},
  \end{align*}
  so Alice obtains $s = 2$.
  \item \textbf{Bob derives $s = A^b \bmod 23 = 8^{15} \bmod 23$.} Because $8 \equiv 5^6 \pmod{23}$ and $5^{22} \equiv 1 \pmod{23}$, we know $8^{15} \equiv 5^{90} = (5^{22})^4 \cdot 5^2 \equiv 25 \equiv 2 \pmod{23}$. Bob reaches the same result directly from $A = 8$ without knowing $a = 6$: squaring gives $8^2 = 64 \equiv 18$, $8^4 \equiv 18^2 \equiv 2$, and $8^8 \equiv 2^2 = 4 \pmod{23}$, so
  \begin{align*}
    8^{15} &= 8^8 \cdot 8^4 \cdot 8^2 \cdot 8^1 \\
           &\equiv (4 \cdot 2) \cdot 18 \cdot 8 = 144 \cdot 8 \\
           &\equiv 6 \cdot 8 = 48 = 2 \cdot 23 + 2 \equiv 2 \pmod{23}.
  \end{align*}
\end{itemize}

\end{figure}

Both parties hold $s = 2$, computed by two different routes. Eve, watching the wire, knows $p = 23$, $g = 5$, $A = 8$, and $B = 19$. To find $s$ by recovering an exponent, she must find $a$ with $5^a \equiv 8 \pmod{23}$ or $b$ with $5^b \equiv 19 \pmod{23}$. With $p = 23$ she can try up to $22$ exponents and spot $a = 6$ on Figure~\ref{ch18:fig-dlog-scramble}: toy parameters carry zero security. Replace $23$ with a $2{,}048$-bit prime (an integer between $2^{2047}$ and $2^{2048}$) and brute-force search faces at least $2^{2047}$ candidates, which no machine can search.

\begin{implementbox}
A toy Diffie--Hellman in Python. This is for \textbf{learning only}: it uses a tiny prime, samples exponents with a non-cryptographic random number generator (RNG), and hashes a decimal string rather than using a proper key-derivation scheme. For real traffic, call a vetted library (\texttt{cryptography}, \texttt{libsodium}) that provides standardized parameters and constant-time arithmetic.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
import random, hashlib

p = 23          # toy prime -- real deployments use >= 2048 bits
g = 5           # generator of (Z/23Z)*

def keypair():
    x = random.randrange(2, p - 1)   # {2..p-2}; use secrets.randbelow in real code!
    X = pow(g, x, p)                 # public g^x mod p via square-and-multiply
    return x, X

a, A = keypair()                     # Alice
b, B = keypair()                     # Bob

# each side raises the OTHER's public value to its OWN secret
s_alice = pow(B, a, p)
s_bob   = pow(A, b, p)
assert s_alice == s_bob              # both get g^(ab) mod p

# turn the shared group element into a symmetric key
shared = s_alice
key = hashlib.sha256(str(shared).encode()).digest()
print("shared group element:", shared)
print("derived key (hex):", key.hex())
\end{lstlisting}
Compare \texttt{pow(B, a, p)} and \texttt{pow(A, b, p)}: each party raises the value it received to the secret it kept.
\end{implementbox}

\begin{trapbox}
Three pitfalls visible in the toy code, each fatal in production.

\textbf{Tiny or weak parameters.} A small $p$, or a $p$ where $p-1$ factors into small primes, lets attackers recover exponents quickly via the Pohlig--Hellman algorithm (Chapter~8 and Chapter~20), which solves the discrete log modulo each prime factor of $p-1$ separately. Finite-field deployments use large \emph{safe primes} $p = 2q+1$ where $q$ is also prime.

\textbf{Predictable randomness.} Python's \texttt{random.randrange} uses the Mersenne Twister, which is predictable after observing $624$ outputs (Chapter~17); predicting the secret exponent breaks the exchange immediately. Always draw exponents from the operating system CSPRNG.

\textbf{Using the raw group element as a key.} The integer $g^{ab} \bmod p$ lies in a structured subset $\{1, \dots, p-1\}$, not uniform bitstrings of fixed length. Pass it through a key-derivation function (KDF), such as the HMAC-based Key Derivation Function (HKDF, built from Chapter~15's HMAC), with a salt and a \emph{context label} -- a public byte string naming the protocol and endpoints so keys derived for one session cannot be reused in another.
\end{trapbox}

\section{What ``secure'' means here: three hardness assumptions}
\label{ch18:assumptions}

``Hard to un-mix'' is an analogy; a security proof needs an exact hardness statement. Three related problems underlie Diffie--Hellman, and the gaps between them decide what can be proved. We state all three in a cyclic group $\G = \langle g \rangle$ generated by $g$ of order $q$, with security parameter $n$ equal to the bit-length of $q$. In Section~\ref{ch18:dh-protocol} we took $\G = \Zns{p}$ of order $q = p-1$, though we will soon restrict to prime-order subgroups and elliptic-curve groups.

\begin{definition}[Discrete logarithm problem, DLP]
\label{ch18:dlp}
Given $g$ and $h = g^x \in \G$ for a uniform $x \getsr \{0, 1, \dots, q-1\}$, find $x$. The DLP is \emph{hard} in $\G$ if every probabilistic polynomial-time ($\PPT$, Chapter~8) algorithm finds $x$ with probability at most $\negl(n)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Computational Diffie--Hellman, CDH]
\label{ch18:cdh}
Given $g$, $g^a$, and $g^b$ in $\G$ for independent uniform $a, b \getsr \{0, 1, \dots, q-1\}$, compute the group element $g^{ab}$. The CDH assumption says every $\PPT$ algorithm succeeds with probability at most $\negl(n)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Decisional Diffie--Hellman, DDH]
\label{ch18:ddh}
Given $g$, $g^a$, $g^b$, and a fourth element $z \in \G$ that is \emph{either} $g^{ab}$ \emph{or} an independent uniform element $g^c$ (for independent uniform $a, b, c \getsr \{0, 1, \dots, q-1\}$, each case chosen with probability $1/2$), decide which case holds. The DDH assumption says that for every $\PPT$ distinguisher $\mathcal{A}$ outputting a guess bit in $\{0, 1\}$,
\[
  \bigl|\Pr[\,\mathcal{A}(g, g^a, g^b, g^{ab}) = 1\,] - \Pr[\,\mathcal{A}(g, g^a, g^b, g^c) = 1\,]\bigr| \le \negl(n).
\]
\end{definition}

These three problems form a ladder of strength (Figure~\ref{ch18:fig-hardness-ladder}). Any algorithm that solves DLP also solves CDH: recover $a$ from $g^a$, then compute $(g^b)^a = g^{ab}$. And any algorithm that solves CDH also solves DDH: compute $g^{ab}$ and test whether $z = g^{ab}$. Contrapositively, hardness flows in the opposite direction:
\[
  \text{DLP hard} \;\Longleftarrow\; \text{CDH hard} \;\Longleftarrow\; \text{DDH hard},
\]
reading ``$X \Longleftarrow Y$'' as ``if $Y$ is hard, then $X$ must be hard too.'' DDH is the strongest assumption (the easiest target for an attacker, requiring only a $1$-bit distinguishing edge), while DLP is the weakest assumption (requiring full exponent recovery). Whether the implications reverse in general is an open question, and in some groups DDH fails even though CDH and DLP remain hard.

\begin{whybox}
Why require DDH rather than stopping at CDH? Key exchange needs a shared value that \emph{cannot be told apart from random} by an eavesdropper, so that a key derived from it behaves like a fresh uniform key. CDH only rules out an attacker who outputs the entire element $g^{ab}$; it does not rule out leaking half the bits of $g^{ab}$ or telling even from odd. DDH guarantees that $(g^a, g^b, g^{ab})$ is computationally indistinguishable from $(g^a, g^b, g^c)$ with an independent random $g^c$, letting security proofs replace $g^{ab}$ with a truly random element at negligible cost.
\end{whybox}

\begin{trapbox}
DDH is \emph{false} in the full group $\Zns{p}$ for every odd prime $p$, broken by a one-line parity test. Recall from Chapter~8 that $x \in \Zns{p}$ is a \emph{quadratic residue} (QR) if $x \equiv r^2 \pmod p$ for some $r$. Write $x = g^k$ for the generator $g$. Since $g^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$ and $\ord_p(g) = p-1$, we have $g^{(p-1)/2} \equiv -1 \pmod p$, so raising $g^k$ to the $(p-1)/2$ power -- Euler's criterion for the \emph{Legendre symbol} -- reveals the parity of $k$:
\[
  (g^k)^{(p-1)/2} \equiv (-1)^k =
  \begin{cases}
    +1 \pmod p & \text{if $k$ is even ($g^k$ is a QR)}, \\
    -1 \pmod p & \text{if $k$ is odd ($g^k$ is a non-residue)}.
  \end{cases}
\]
Because $ab$ is even if and only if at least one of $a$ or $b$ is even, Eve tests $A^{(p-1)/2}$ and $B^{(p-1)/2} \bmod p$ directly from the public transcript to learn whether $ab$ is even or odd. She then computes $z^{(p-1)/2} \bmod p$: a real $g^{ab}$ always matches the predicted $\pm 1$ sign, whereas a uniform $g^c$ disagrees half the time. That single leaked bit breaks DDH.

The fix is to work in a \emph{prime-order subgroup} (Figure~\ref{ch18:fig-qr-subgroup}): choose a safe prime $p = 2q+1$ (with $q$ prime) and use a generator $g_q = g^2 \bmod p$ of the order-$q$ subgroup of quadratic residues. Every element in $\langle g_q \rangle$ already satisfies $x^q \equiv 1 \pmod p$, so the Legendre test outputs $+1$ on every element and leaks nothing. Standardized finite-field Diffie--Hellman (DH) groups restrict to such prime-order subgroups, and elliptic-curve groups (Chapter~21) are chosen with prime order (or prime order times a tiny cofactor) so DDH holds without a Legendre leak.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
The cost gap behind these assumptions is analyzed in Chapter~8 and the companion algorithms book. Evaluating $g^a \bmod p$ by square-and-multiply takes $O(\log a)$ modular multiplications -- a few thousand for a $2{,}048$-bit exponent. The fastest known classical attack on finite-field discrete logarithms, the general number field sieve, runs in sub-exponential time roughly
\[
  \exp\!\bigl(c\,(\ln p)^{1/3}(\ln \ln p)^{2/3}\bigr)
\]
(where $\ln$ is the natural logarithm and $c \approx 1.92$ is a constant) -- roughly $2^{112}$ operations at $2{,}048$ bits and $2^{128}$ at $3{,}072$ bits. On well-chosen elliptic curves that sieve does not apply, leaving only generic square-root attacks such as Pollard's rho (about $\sqrt{q}$ group operations), so a $256$-bit prime-order curve matches the classical security of a $3{,}072$-bit finite-field prime.
\end{connectionbox}

\subsection{From shared secret to usable key}
\label{ch18:kdf}

Even inside a prime-order group where DDH holds, $s = g^{ab}$ is a group element, not a symmetric key. Three steps remain before encrypting traffic. First, $s$ must be serialized into a canonical fixed-length byte string. Second, a uniform element of $\G$ is not a uniform $2{,}048$-bit string -- primes leave gaps in $\{0,1\}^{2048}$ -- so its entropy must be extracted into uniform bytes. Third, one handshake usually needs several independent keys (separate client-to-server and server-to-client encryption and MAC keys). A key-derivation function handles all three; modern protocols use HKDF (defined in Chapter~23 from Chapter~15's HMAC) to produce concatenated keys
\[
  k_1 \concat k_2 \concat \cdots = \operatorname{HKDF}(s,\ \text{salt},\ \text{context}),
\]
where $\text{salt}$ is a public random value (or all-zeros default) and $\text{context}$ binds the derived keys $k_1, k_2, \dots$ to the handshake transcript and participant identities. Feeding $s$ straight into AES is an implementation bug; the KDF step is mandatory.

\section{The man-in-the-middle problem}
\label{ch18:mitm}

Plain Diffie--Hellman defeats a \emph{passive} eavesdropper who only reads the wire, yet fails completely against an \emph{active} man-in-the-middle (MITM) attacker who can intercept and replace messages (Chapter~1). Nothing in the protocol ties the number $A$ to Alice or $B$ to Bob, so an active attacker on the wire can substitute her own public values (Figure~\ref{ch18:fig-mitm}).

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Man-in-the-middle on unauthenticated Diffie--Hellman.}

Suppose Eve steps into an active role on the link between Alice and Bob, intercepting packets rather than merely reading them. She runs two separate exchanges at once, posing as Bob toward Alice and as Alice toward Bob:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Alice sends $A = g^a \bmod p$, intended for Bob. Eve intercepts $A$; Bob never receives it.
  \item Eve chooses her own secret exponent $e$, computes $E = g^e \bmod p$, and sends $E$ to Bob as if it came from Alice. She also sends $E$ to Alice as if it came from Bob.
  \item Bob receives $E$, computes $s_B = E^b \equiv g^{eb} \pmod p$ (believing he shares it with Alice), and sends $B = g^b \bmod p$ toward Alice; Eve intercepts $B$ as well.
  \item Alice receives $E$ and computes $s_A = E^a \equiv g^{ea} \pmod p$, believing she shares it with Bob.
  \item Eve computes both shared secrets: $s_A = A^e \equiv g^{ae} \pmod p$ and $s_B = B^e \equiv g^{be} \pmod p$, then derives symmetric keys $k_A$ and $k_B$ from them.
\end{enumerate}

Eve now shares key $k_A$ with Alice and a separate key $k_B$ with Bob. When Alice encrypts a message $m$ under $k_A$ and sends $\Enc_{k_A}(m)$ ``to Bob,'' Eve decrypts it with $k_A$, reads or modifies $m$ to $m'$, re-encrypts under $k_B$, and forwards $\Enc_{k_B}(m')$ to Bob. Neither endpoint sees an error: each completed a valid Diffie--Hellman exchange and holds a working key, yet both are talking to Eve. Both confidentiality and integrity are lost.
\end{attackbox}

Key exchange and \emph{authentication} are separate problems, and Diffie--Hellman solves only the first. It establishes a shared secret with \emph{someone}, without saying \emph{who} is on the other end. Binding a Diffie--Hellman value to an identity requires an external trust anchor: a public key Alice already knows belongs to Bob, or a certificate signed by a trusted certificate authority (Chapter~1). Digital signatures (Chapter~22) and public-key infrastructure (PKI, Chapter~24) supply that binding. In a TLS handshake, the server signs its Diffie--Hellman public value using the private key paired with its certificate; if Eve substitutes $E$, the signature check fails and the browser aborts the connection.

\begin{intuitionbox}
\emph{Diffie--Hellman builds a private pipe to somebody; authentication tells you who holds the other end.} Both are required: encrypting traffic with an unbreakable cipher to an impostor protects nothing.
\end{intuitionbox}

\section{Ephemeral Diffie--Hellman and forward secrecy}
\label{ch18:forward-secrecy}

One more property explains why modern TLS uses Diffie--Hellman rather than having the client encrypt a session key directly under the server's long-term RSA public key. It concerns what happens \emph{after} a conversation ends, if a server's long-term keys are later stolen.

Compare two ways to run the exchange. In \emph{static} Diffie--Hellman, a server reuses one long-term secret exponent $b$ across many sessions, publishing $B = g^b \bmod p$ once for all clients. In \emph{ephemeral} Diffie--Hellman -- written DHE (Diffie--Hellman Ephemeral) over finite fields and ECDHE (Elliptic-Curve Diffie--Hellman Ephemeral) over elliptic curves -- each party generates a \emph{fresh} random exponent for every session and erases it from memory as soon as the session keys are derived.

Ephemeral keys provide \emph{forward secrecy} (also called perfect forward secrecy): stealing long-term keys in the future does not expose past recorded sessions. Suppose an adversary records Alice and Bob's encrypted traffic for years at times $t_1, t_2, \dots$, storing ciphertexts she cannot yet read, and later breaches Bob's server at time $t_3$ to steal every long-term private key on disk. What can she decrypt retroactively?

With ephemeral Diffie--Hellman, nothing (Figure~\ref{ch18:fig-forward-secrecy}). Session~$i$ derived its key from $g^{a_i b_i}$ using one-time exponents $a_i$ and $b_i$ that both sides erased right after the handshake. The server's long-term signing key $\mathsf{sk}_{\mathrm{sign}}$ was used only to authenticate the handshake transcript, never to encrypt or derive $k_i$. Past ciphertexts stay unreadable.

With static key transport -- an older TLS mode in which the client encrypted each session key $k_i$ under the server's long-term public key $\mathsf{pk}_{\mathrm{server}}$ -- the breach at $t_3$ exposes everything. Stealing the matching private decryption key $\mathsf{sk}$ lets the attacker decrypt every recorded $\Enc_{\mathsf{pk}_{\mathrm{server}}}(k_i)$ and read years of archived traffic at once.

\begin{whybox}
Forward secrecy comes from separating two jobs. The long-term key only authenticates -- answering ``who are you?'' -- while short-lived Diffie--Hellman exponents establish confidentiality and are destroyed right after the handshake. Because past session keys cannot be computed from any secret kept on disk, a future theft gives an attacker no help against old recordings.
\end{whybox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{TLS 1.3} (Chapter~25) removed static RSA key transport altogether. Every TLS~1.3 handshake runs ephemeral (EC)DHE, making forward secrecy mandatory: the client sends a fresh ECDHE share in its first flight, and the server replies with its own share plus a signature over the handshake transcript.

\textbf{Signal} (Chapter~27) combines several elliptic-curve Diffie--Hellman (ECDH) exchanges in its Extended Triple Diffie--Hellman (X3DH) handshake -- mixing identity keys, medium-term ``prekeys,'' and one-time ephemeral keys -- and its Double Ratchet then runs a fresh ECDH step as conversations turn over, giving both forward secrecy and \emph{post-compromise security} (healing future messages once an attacker loses access to a compromised device).

\textbf{SSH} (Chapter~28) and \textbf{Internet Protocol Security (IPsec)} via its \textbf{Internet Key Exchange (IKE)} protocol (Chapter~30) likewise run ephemeral Diffie--Hellman on every session, authenticated by certificates or \emph{host keys} (long-term public keys identifying a server).

Standardized groups prevent parameter-choice attacks: finite-field DHE uses the audited safe primes of RFC~7919, while ECDHE predominantly uses Curve25519 (Chapter~5), designed to avoid small-subgroup and timing pitfalls.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
The term ``perfect forward secrecy'' comes from 1990s authenticated key exchange -- notably the Station-to-Station (STS) protocol of Diffie, van Oorschot, and Wiener, and later IKE. For years many web servers skipped DHE and ran static RSA to save modular exponentiations. Edward Snowden's 2013 disclosures of classified intelligence programs, which documented bulk recording of internet traffic, made ``harvest now, decrypt later'' a concrete threat rather than a theoretical one. Within five years ephemeral Diffie--Hellman moved from optional to universal in TLS~1.3. The same ``harvest now, decrypt later'' threat against future quantum computers now drives post-quantum key exchange (Chapter~35).
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Looking ahead}
\label{ch18:ahead}

Diffie--Hellman lets two strangers agree on a shared secret over an open wire, while leaving two open needs for the chapters ahead. First, the man-in-the-middle attack showed that key exchange without authentication falls to an active attacker, motivating digital signatures (Chapter~22) and certificates (Chapter~24). Second, the discrete-logarithm structure developed here also powers ElGamal public-key encryption (Chapter~20), elliptic-curve cryptography (Chapter~21), and the Digital Signature Algorithm (DSA) and its elliptic-curve counterpart ECDSA (Chapter~22). Chapter~19 introduces the other classical foundation of public-key cryptography -- integer factoring and RSA -- which can encrypt and sign directly rather than only establish keys.

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch18:exercises}

\begin{exercise}
With $p = 23$ and $g = 5$ as in the chapter, suppose Alice's secret is $a = 4$ and Bob's is $b = 3$. Compute $A = g^a \bmod 23$, $B = g^b \bmod 23$, and the shared secret $s = g^{ab} \bmod 23$ two ways (as $B^a \bmod 23$ and as $A^b \bmod 23$), checking that they agree.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Take $p = 11$. Show that $g = 2$ is a generator of $\Zns{11}$ by listing the powers $2^1, 2^2, \dots, 2^{10} \bmod 11$ and confirming they hit all of $\{1, \dots, 10\}$. Then show that $g = 3$ is \emph{not} a generator by finding the order of $3$ modulo $11$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
(Breaking a toy.) Eve observes a Diffie--Hellman exchange with $p = 23$, $g = 5$, $A = 4$, and $B = 16$. By trying exponents (or by any method you like), recover Alice's secret $a$ and Bob's secret $b$, then compute the shared secret $s$. How many exponentiations did your search take in the worst case, and how does that count scale with the bit-length of $p$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Prove the reduction CDH $\le$ DLP: given a $\PPT$ algorithm that solves the discrete logarithm problem in $\G$, construct one that solves CDH in $\G$ with the same success probability. Then prove DDH $\le$ CDH similarly. State why neither reduction directly runs in the reverse direction.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
(The Legendre attack on DDH in $\Zns{p}$.) Let $p = 23$, so $g = 5$ is a generator and the quadratic residues modulo $23$ are the even powers of $5$. By Euler's criterion, $x \in \Zns{23}$ is a quadratic residue if and only if $x^{11} \equiv 1 \pmod{23}$ (and otherwise $x^{11} \equiv -1 \pmod{23}$). Given a DDH challenge $(g^a, g^b, z)$, explain how to predict the Legendre value $(g^{ab})^{11} \bmod 23 \in \{+1, -1\}$ from $(g^a)^{11}$ and $(g^b)^{11} \bmod 23$, and show why checking $z^{11} \bmod 23$ against that prediction distinguishes a genuine $g^{ab}$ from a uniform element of $\Zns{23}$ with success probability $3/4$ over a single fair-coin challenge. Why does restricting to the order-$11$ subgroup of quadratic residues stop this attack?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Carry out the man-in-the-middle attack of Section~\ref{ch18:mitm} concretely with $p = 23$, $g = 5$. Let Alice choose $a = 6$, Bob choose $b = 15$, and Eve choose $e = 9$. Compute the public value $E$ Eve sends to both, the shared secret $s_A = g^{ae} \bmod 23$ Eve establishes with Alice, and the shared secret $s_B = g^{be} \bmod 23$ Eve establishes with Bob. Verify that Alice and Bob each compute a value matching one of Eve's.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Explain in one or two paragraphs why hashing the shared group element through a KDF is necessary even when DDH holds. Give a specific example of something that could go wrong if you used the raw integer $g^{ab} \bmod p$ directly as an AES-128 key (consider its length and its distribution).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
A server runs \emph{static} Diffie--Hellman: it uses the same secret exponent $b$ for every client, publishing $B = g^b \bmod p$ as a long-lived public value. An attacker records a year of encrypted sessions and later steals $b$. Show that the attacker can now recompute the shared secret of every recorded session. Then explain which step of the ephemeral variant prevents this, and why erasing the ephemeral exponents is necessary.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
(Small-subgroup pitfall, preview of Chapter~21.) Suppose $p = 23$ so $\Zns{p}$ has order $22 = 2 \cdot 11$. The element $h = 22 \equiv -1 \pmod{23}$ has order $2$. Imagine Bob fails to validate Alice's incoming value and Eve sends $A = 22$ instead of a legitimate public value. Show that Bob's computed shared secret $A^b \bmod 23$ can then take only one of two values depending on the parity of $b$, leaking a bit of $b$. What general validation rule on incoming group elements prevents this class of attack?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Estimate, using the cost figures from Section~\ref{ch18:assumptions}, how the work of the legitimate parties compares to the work of an attacker for a $2{,}048$-bit prime. Roughly how many modular multiplications does Alice perform to compute $g^a \bmod p$? Contrast that with the sub-exponential cost of the number field sieve, and explain in words why this gap is what makes the scheme usable.
\end{exercise}
