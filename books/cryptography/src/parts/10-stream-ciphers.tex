\chapter{Stream Ciphers}

In Chapter~9, the one-time pad gave us a perfect cipher and immediately took it away: Shannon's bound demands a key as long as the message, used once and drawn from true randomness. Alice cannot hand Bob a terabyte of pre-shared key every time she wants to send him a terabyte of video.

Most internet encryption rests on a single compromise. Keep the XOR rule of the one-time pad, but generate the long random-looking pad -- the \emph{keystream} -- from a short secret key using a deterministic algorithm called a \emph{pseudorandom generator}, trading unconditional secrecy for security against any computationally bounded adversary. Two questions govern every cipher built this way: what makes a generator's output safe to use as a pad, and what breaks when the generator is flawed or its keystream is reused?

\section{From the one-time pad to a generator}
\label{ch10:from-otp}

Recall the one-time pad. Plaintext $m$ and key $k$ are bitstrings of the same length $\ell$. Encryption and decryption are both bitwise exclusive-or (XOR):
\[
  c = m \xor k, \qquad m = c \xor k .
\]
The strength of the pad is that if $k$ is uniformly random and used once, then $c$ is uniformly random and independent of $m$: the ciphertext leaks nothing. The catch is that $k$ must be as long as $m$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
What if we did not need a \emph{truly} random $k$ of length $\ell$? What if a key that merely \emph{looks} random to any efficient adversary were good enough? Then we could keep a short secret starting value, called a \emph{seed} -- say 256 bits -- and stretch it into a keystream of length $\ell$ with a public algorithm $G$. Alice and Bob both know the seed, so they both compute the same long bitstring $G(\text{seed})$ and XOR it with the data. Eve knows the algorithm $G$ but not the seed, and if $G$ is well built she cannot tell its output from fair coin flips.
\end{intuitionbox}

The deterministic function that does the stretching is the pseudorandom generator.

\begin{definition}[Pseudorandom generator, PRG]
\label{ch10:def-prg}
A \emph{pseudorandom generator} (PRG) is a deterministic, polynomial-time computable function
\[
  G : \set{0,1}^{s} \to \set{0,1}^{\ell(s)}, \qquad \ell(s) > s,
\]
that maps a short \emph{seed} of $s$ bits to a longer \emph{output} of $\ell(s)$ bits, where the output length $\ell(s)$ is a polynomial function of $s$ (written $\ell$ when $s$ is clear from context). The difference $\ell(s) - s$ is the \emph{stretch}.
\end{definition}

A stream cipher is the one-time pad with the random tape replaced by PRG output (Figure~\ref{ch10:fig-stream-arch}).

\begin{definition}[Stream cipher from a PRG]
\label{ch10:def-stream}
Let $G:\set{0,1}^s \to \set{0,1}^{\ell}$ be a PRG. Define encryption and decryption by
\[
  \Enc_k(m) = m \xor G(k), \qquad \Dec_k(c) = c \xor G(k),
\]
for plaintext $m \in \set{0,1}^{\ell}$, ciphertext $c \in \set{0,1}^{\ell}$, and secret key (seed) $k \in \set{0,1}^s$. The $\ell$-bit string $G(k)$ is the \emph{keystream}.
\end{definition}

Correctness holds for the same reason as in the one-time pad, because every bit satisfies $x \xor x = 0$:
\begin{align*}
  \Dec_k(\Enc_k(m))
  &= (m \xor G(k)) \xor G(k) \\
  &= m \xor (G(k) \xor G(k)) && \text{(associativity of $\xor$)} \\
  &= m \xor 0^\ell = m.
\end{align*}
Here $0^\ell$ denotes the string of $\ell$ zeros.

\begin{focusbox}
The \emph{operation} on the data is identical to the one-time pad: a bitwise XOR. The \emph{source} of the pad changed from a true-random tape to a deterministic expansion of a short seed. Every security question about stream ciphers comes down to that swap: how unpredictable is $G(k)$, and what happens if you ever reuse the same keystream?
\end{focusbox}

\subsection{Why this cannot be perfectly secret}
\label{ch10:not-perfect}

Stretching a short key costs us perfect secrecy. A stream cipher with a 256-bit key and a 1-gigabyte message ($10^9\text{ bytes} = 8 \cdot 10^9\text{ bits}$) cannot be perfectly secret, for a counting reason that does not depend on the design of $G$ (Figure~\ref{ch10:fig-sparse-reach}).

\begin{whybox}
Shannon's theorem says perfect secrecy requires the key space to be at least as large as the message space. Here there are $2^{256}$ keys, whereas a 1-gigabyte message comes from a space of $2^{8 \cdot 10^9}$ bitstrings. For a fixed ciphertext $c$, varying $k$ across all $2^{256}$ seeds produces at most $2^{256}$ candidate plaintexts $c \xor G(k)$: every other $\ell$-bit message is an \emph{impossible} decryption of $c$. An unbounded adversary who sees $c$ could, in principle, try all $2^{256}$ seeds, compute $c \xor G(k)$ for each, and cross out the remaining $2^{8 \cdot 10^9} - 2^{256}$ plaintexts. Ruling out messages changes their probabilities, so perfect secrecy fails whenever $s < \ell$.
\end{whybox}

We therefore aim for \emph{computational} secrecy: security against adversaries that run in feasible time. Everything that follows rests on one assumption -- that secure pseudorandom generators exist.

\section{What we demand of a PRG}
\label{ch10:prg-security}

The intuitive requirement is ``the output looks random.'' Cryptography makes that precise with a game played by a distinguisher $D$ (Chapter~8). With probability $\tfrac12$ we feed $D$ a truly random $\ell$-bit string $r$; with probability $\tfrac12$ we feed $D$ the generator output $G(k)$ for a uniformly random seed $k \getsr \set{0,1}^s$. The distinguisher outputs a single bit: $1$ for ``pseudorandom'' and $0$ for ``random.'' If $D$ outputs $1$ noticeably more (or less) often on $G(k)$ than on $r$, it has \emph{distinguished} $G$ from uniform randomness, and $G$ has failed.

\begin{definition}[PRG security]
\label{ch10:def-prg-secure}
A PRG $G:\set{0,1}^s \to \set{0,1}^{\ell}$ is \emph{secure} if for every probabilistic polynomial-time ($\PPT$) distinguisher $D$,
\[
  \Adv_{\mathrm{prg}}(D)
  \;=\;
  \abs[\big]{\,
    \Pr_{k \getsr \set{0,1}^s}\!\big[D(G(k)) = 1\big]
    \;-\;
    \Pr_{r \getsr \set{0,1}^{\ell}}\!\big[D(r) = 1\big]
  \,}
  \;\le\; \negl(s).
\]
\end{definition}

The first probability is taken over a uniform $s$-bit seed $k$ (and $D$'s internal random coins); the second is over a uniform $\ell$-bit string $r$. Their absolute difference $\Adv_{\mathrm{prg}}(D)$ is $D$'s distinguishing advantage. Requiring $\Adv_{\mathrm{prg}}(D) \le \negl(s)$ means that as the seed length $s$ grows, any feasible attacker's edge shrinks faster than $1/p(s)$ for every polynomial $p$.

\begin{trapbox}
A PRG is \emph{not} merely an algorithm whose output passes standard randomness test batteries such as Diehard or the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) statistical test suite. Those check fixed surface properties -- equal counts of zeros and ones, a long \emph{period} (number of outputs before the sequence repeats), uncorrelated adjacent bytes. Passing a chi-squared test (which checks whether observed bit or byte counts match uniform frequencies) is necessary, never sufficient. A cryptographic PRG must defeat \emph{every} efficient test, including ones tailored specifically to the known code of $G$. Many badly broken generators pass general statistical suites.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Next-bit unpredictability}
\label{ch10:next-bit}

An equivalent and more operational view of PRG security is due to Andrew Yao and to Manuel Blum and Silvio Micali. Instead of asking ``can you tell the whole output from random,'' ask ``given a prefix of the output, can you predict the next bit?''

\begin{definition}[Next-bit test]
\label{ch10:def-nextbit}
Let $(g_1, g_2, \dots, g_\ell) = G(k)$ for a uniform seed $k \getsr \set{0,1}^s$. The generator $G$ passes the \emph{next-bit test} if for every $\PPT$ predictor $A$ and every prefix length $i \in \set{0, 1, \dots, \ell - 1}$,
\[
  \Pr_{k \getsr \set{0,1}^s}\!\big[A(g_1, g_2, \dots, g_i) = g_{i+1}\big] \;\le\; \tfrac12 + \negl(s).
\]
(For $i = 0$, $A$ receives the empty prefix and predicts $g_1$.)
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Yao]
\label{ch10:thm-yao}
A PRG is secure (Definition~\ref{ch10:def-prg-secure}) if and only if it passes the next-bit test.
\end{theorem}

The ``only if'' direction is short: a successful next-bit predictor is already a distinguisher, because on a truly random string no algorithm predicts bit $i{+}1$ with probability above $\tfrac12$, so any predictor beating $\tfrac12 + \eps$ separates $G(k)$ from random with advantage at least $\eps$. The ``if'' direction -- that defeating every next-bit predictor defeats every distinguisher -- is proved by a \emph{hybrid argument}, illustrated in Figure~\ref{ch10:fig-hybrids}.

\begin{whybox}
The hybrid argument appears throughout cryptography. Define $\ell + 1$ distributions $H_0, H_1, \dots, H_\ell$, where hybrid $H_j$ outputs the first $j$ bits from $G(k)$ and the remaining $\ell - j$ bits uniformly at random. Then $H_0$ is uniform random and $H_\ell$ is $G(k)$. Write $p_j = \Pr[D(H_j) = 1]$, and suppose $D$ separates $H_\ell$ from $H_0$ with advantage $\eps$; flipping $D$'s output bit if needed, assume $p_\ell - p_0 = \eps \ge 0$. Across the $\ell$ steps of the ladder, at least one adjacent pair $(H_j, H_{j+1})$ must satisfy $p_{j+1} - p_j \ge \eps/\ell$.

Those two hybrids agree everywhere except bit $j{+}1$, which is uniform in $H_j$ and equals $g_{j+1}$ in $H_{j+1}$. A predictor $A$ given only $(g_1,\dots,g_j)$ guesses $g_{j+1}$ without knowing $k$: it samples a uniform bit $u \getsr \set{0,1}$ and random suffix bits $(r_{j+2},\dots,r_\ell)$, runs $D$ on $x = (g_1,\dots,g_j, u, r_{j+2},\dots,r_\ell)$, and outputs $u$ if $D(x) = 1$ (or $1-u$ if $D(x) = 0$). Unconditionally $x$ follows $H_j$, while conditioned on $u = g_{j+1}$ it follows $H_{j+1}$. Since $p_j = \tfrac12 p_{j+1} + \tfrac12 \Pr[D(x)=1 \mid u \ne g_{j+1}]$, $A$ succeeds with probability
\begin{align*}
  \Pr[A \text{ outputs } g_{j+1}]
  &= \tfrac12 \Pr[D(x)=1 \mid u = g_{j+1}] + \tfrac12 \Pr[D(x)=0 \mid u \ne g_{j+1}] \\
  &= \tfrac12 p_{j+1} + \tfrac12 \bigl(1 - (2p_j - p_{j+1})\bigr)
  \;=\; \tfrac12 + (p_{j+1} - p_j) \;\ge\; \tfrac12 + \frac{\eps}{\ell}.
\end{align*}
Taking the contrapositive (reversing the implication): if no $\PPT$ predictor beats $\tfrac12 + \negl(s)$, then $\eps/\ell$ -- and hence $\eps$, since $\ell$ is polynomial in $s$ -- must be negligible.
\end{whybox}

\begin{connectionbox}
The step finding a gap of size at least $\eps/\ell$ uses a \emph{telescoping sum}, in which intermediate terms cancel in pairs:
\[
  p_\ell - p_0
  \;=\; \sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1} (p_{j+1} - p_j).
\]
If $\ell$ numbers sum to $\eps$, their average is $\eps/\ell$, so at least one term is at least $\eps/\ell$. The companion probability volume develops this averaging-over-a-telescope pattern alongside union bounds and the triangle inequality for statistical distance.
\end{connectionbox}

\subsection{Security of the stream cipher, granted a good PRG}
\label{ch10:cipher-security}

With a secure PRG in hand, the stream cipher achieves computational secrecy for a single message: \emph{semantic security} (meaning the ciphertext teaches an efficient eavesdropper nothing computable about the plaintext beyond its length), which is equivalent to winning no better than a coin flip in the eavesdropper indistinguishability game (IND-EAV) from Chapter~1.

\begin{theorem}[Stream-cipher security]
\label{ch10:thm-stream-secure}
If $G$ is a secure PRG, then the stream cipher of Definition~\ref{ch10:def-stream} is semantically secure against a passive eavesdropper for a \emph{single} encryption.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
The proof is a reduction from PRG distinguishing to breaking the cipher. Suppose a $\PPT$ eavesdropper $\mathcal{A}$ picks equal-length messages $m_0, m_1 \in \set{0,1}^\ell$ and guesses which one was encrypted from $c = \Enc_k(m_b)$ for $b \getsr \set{0,1}$. Build a PRG distinguisher $D$ that takes a challenge string $p \in \set{0,1}^\ell$: $D$ samples $b \getsr \set{0,1}$, hands $\mathcal{A}$ the ciphertext $m_b \xor p$, and outputs $1$ if and only if $\mathcal{A}$ guesses $b$ correctly.
\begin{itemize}
  \item When $p = G(k)$, the input $m_b \xor G(k)$ is a real stream-cipher encryption, so $\Pr[D(G(k)) = 1] = \Pr[\mathcal{A} \text{ wins}]$.
  \item When $p = r$ is truly uniform, $m_b \xor r$ is a one-time-pad ciphertext, which hides $b$ perfectly (Chapter~9), so $\Pr[D(r) = 1] = \tfrac12$.
\end{itemize}
Subtracting the two probabilities gives
\[
  \Adv_{\mathrm{prg}}(D)
  \;=\; \abs[\big]{\Pr[D(G(k)) = 1] - \Pr[D(r) = 1]}
  \;=\; \abs[\big]{\Pr[\mathcal{A} \text{ wins}] - \tfrac12}.
\]
Because $G$ is a secure PRG, $\Adv_{\mathrm{prg}}(D) \le \negl(s)$, so $\mathcal{A}$'s advantage over a coin flip is negligible too.
\end{whybox}

Theorem~\ref{ch10:thm-stream-secure} holds only for a \emph{single} encryption. Reuse the same keystream on a second message and the proof collapses, because the reduction needed $p$ to mask one ciphertext. The next section shows what happens when a keystream is used twice.

\section{Keystream reuse: the two-time pad disaster}
\label{ch10:reuse}

Encrypt two messages $m_1, m_2 \in \set{0,1}^\ell$ with the \emph{same} keystream $z = G(k)$:
\[
  c_1 = m_1 \xor z, \qquad c_2 = m_2 \xor z.
\]
Eve XORs the two ciphertexts together, and the keystream cancels:
\begin{align*}
  c_1 \xor c_2
  &= (m_1 \xor z) \xor (m_2 \xor z) \\
  &= m_1 \xor m_2 \xor (z \xor z) && \text{(commutativity and associativity of $\xor$)} \\
  &= m_1 \xor m_2 . && \text{($z \xor z = 0^\ell$)}
\end{align*}
The keystream is gone, leaving Eve with the bitwise XOR of the two plaintexts. This is the same two-time pad failure from Chapter~9, and no property of $G$ can rescue it once $z$ cancels. Figure~\ref{ch10:fig-twotime} shows the cancellation.

\begin{intuitionbox}
$m_1 \xor m_2$ may look scrambled at first glance, yet natural language and network formats (HTTP requests, file headers) are heavily redundant. In ASCII, uppercase and lowercase letters differ by $32$ (\texttt{0x20} in base-16 hexadecimal), which is also the byte code for the space character (\texttt{' '}). Wherever one message has a space and the other has a lowercase letter, $m_1 \xor m_2$ flips that bit and yields an uppercase letter, betraying word boundaries. Guessing any stretch of $m_1$ hands over the matching stretch of $m_2$, since $m_2 = (m_1 \xor m_2) \xor m_1$. That is the crib-dragging attack from Chapter~9: slide a common string such as \texttt{" the "} along $m_1 \xor m_2$, and wherever the output reads sensibly, both messages stand revealed. With three or more ciphertexts under one keystream, recovery is typically complete.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{example}[A four-bit two-time pad by hand]
\label{ch10:ex-twotime}
Take a 4-bit keystream $z = 1011$ and two messages $m_1 = 1100$, $m_2 = 0110$:
\[
  c_1 = 1100 \xor 1011 = 0111, \qquad c_2 = 0110 \xor 1011 = 1101.
\]
Eve never learns $z$, yet she computes
\[
  c_1 \xor c_2 = 0111 \xor 1101 = 1010 = m_1 \xor m_2 .
\]
Check: $m_1 \xor m_2 = 1100 \xor 0110 = 1010$. If Eve knows $m_1 = 1100$ (for instance, a fixed protocol header), she recovers $m_2 = 1010 \xor 1100 = 0110$ in full. How strong $G$ was made no difference; reusing $z$ broke the cipher.
\end{example}

\begin{attackbox}
\label{ch10:attack-wep}
\textbf{WEP: keystream reuse at industrial scale.} Wired Equivalent Privacy (WEP), the original 1999 Wi-Fi encryption standard, used the stream cipher RC4 (detailed in the next section). RC4 takes a single input key, so to encrypt many packets under one shared Wi-Fi password, WEP prepended a 24-bit \emph{initialization vector} (IV) -- a public per-packet value -- to the secret key, sent the IV unencrypted in the packet header, and ran RC4 on $\text{IV} \concat \text{key}$ (where $\concat$ denotes bitstring concatenation). For a fixed Wi-Fi password, the packet's keystream was therefore determined completely by its 24-bit IV.

Twenty-four bits gives only $2^{24} \approx 16.7\text{ million}$ distinct IVs. By the birthday bound (Chapter~7), random IV collisions appear after roughly $\sqrt{2^{24}} = 4096$ packets, and even a sequential counter wraps within hours on a busy access point -- while many network cards reset the IV to zero on every reboot, colliding immediately. Two packets with the same IV share the \emph{same keystream}: a two-time pad. Wi-Fi frames begin with predictable bytes such as Address Resolution Protocol (ARP) requests and Logical Link Control (LLC) headers (\texttt{0xAA AA 03 00 00 00}), so an eavesdropper XORs colliding packets, XORs out the known header bytes, and reads traffic.

A second flaw was worse. Weaknesses in RC4's \emph{key schedule} (the setup step that mixes the key into the cipher's internal state) let Scott Fluhrer, Itsik Mantin, and Adi Shamir (2001) recover the \emph{secret WEP key itself} from tens of thousands of captured packets, rather than merely decrypting colliding pairs. Automated tools such as \texttt{aircrack-ng} cracked 104-bit WEP keys in minutes. WEP was deprecated in 2004 in favor of Wi-Fi Protected Access (WPA, and then WPA2).
\end{attackbox}

\begin{focusbox}
The operational rule applies far beyond Wi-Fi: \textbf{never reuse a keystream.} Because replacing the long-term secret key on every message would recreate the key-distribution problem of the one-time pad, practical stream ciphers take two inputs: a secret key $k$ and a public \emph{nonce} (number used once) $n$, producing an independent keystream $G(k, n)$ for each pair. Keep $(k, n)$ unique for every message -- never reset a counter nonce across reboots, never pick nonces from a tiny space -- or encryption collapses back to a two-time pad regardless of the cipher's strength.
\end{focusbox}

\section{RC4: simple, ubiquitous, and broken}
\label{ch10:rc4}

\begin{historybox}
RC4 (``Rivest Cipher 4'') was designed by Ron Rivest at RSA Security in 1987 for fast software execution on 8- and 32-bit microprocessors, using a dynamic 256-byte permutation array indexed by two moving byte pointers. RSA Security kept it as a trade secret until September 1994, when compatible source code was anonymously posted to the Cypherpunks mailing list and circulated (for trademark reasons) under the name ARCFOUR. Taking only a few lines of C with no precomputed constant tables, RC4 went on to protect a large fraction of internet traffic across WEP, WPA's Temporal Key Integrity Protocol (TKIP), Secure Sockets Layer (SSL), and Transport Layer Security (TLS). Statistical biases in its keystream eventually broke it in practice, and Internet standard RFC~7465 prohibited RC4 in TLS in 2015.
\end{historybox}

RC4 keeps a secret internal state consisting of an array $S[0\dots 255]$ holding a permutation (reordering) of the 256 byte values $0, 1, \dots, 255$, together with two byte indices $i$ and $j$. Given a secret key of $\text{keylen}$ bytes (typically $5$ to $32$ bytes), RC4 runs in two phases.

\textbf{Key-scheduling algorithm (KSA).} Initialize $S$ to the identity permutation $S[i] = i$, then shuffle it using the key bytes:
\begin{align*}
  &\text{for } i = 0 \text{ to } 255:\ S[i] \gets i \\
  &j \gets 0 \\
  &\text{for } i = 0 \text{ to } 255:\\
  &\qquad j \gets (j + S[i] + \text{key}[i \bmod \text{keylen}]) \bmod 256 \\
  &\qquad \text{swap } S[i],\, S[j].
\end{align*}

\textbf{Pseudorandom generation algorithm (PRGA).} Reset $i$ and $j$, then emit one keystream byte per step (Figure~\ref{ch10:fig-rc4-step}):
\begin{align*}
  &i \gets 0,\ j \gets 0 \\
  &\text{each step:}\\
  &\qquad i \gets (i+1) \bmod 256 \\
  &\qquad j \gets (j + S[i]) \bmod 256 \\
  &\qquad \text{swap } S[i],\, S[j] \\
  &\qquad \text{output } S[(S[i] + S[j]) \bmod 256].
\end{align*}

Each output byte is XORed with the next plaintext byte.

\begin{implementbox}
\label{ch10:impl-rc4}
\textbf{RC4 in a few lines of Python -- learning only.} This is a teaching model so you can trace the bytes. RC4 is broken; do not use it in real software.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
def rc4(key, data):
    # Key-scheduling algorithm (KSA): shuffle S with the key.
    S = list(range(256))
    j = 0
    for i in range(256):
        j = (j + S[i] + key[i % len(key)]) % 256
        S[i], S[j] = S[j], S[i]
    # Pseudorandom generation (PRGA): emit keystream, XOR into data.
    out = bytearray()
    i = j = 0
    for byte in data:
        i = (i + 1) % 256
        j = (j + S[i]) % 256
        S[i], S[j] = S[j], S[i]
        k = S[(S[i] + S[j]) % 256]   # one keystream byte
        out.append(byte ^ k)
    return bytes(out)

key = b"Key"
ct = rc4(key, b"Plaintext")
print(ct.hex())                       # bbf316e8d940af0ad3
print(rc4(key, ct))                   # b'Plaintext'  (XOR is its own inverse)
\end{lstlisting}
Encryption and decryption are the same function, because both XOR the keystream into their input. A standard \emph{test vector} -- a published input-output pair used to verify implementations -- for key \texttt{"Key"} and plaintext \texttt{"Plaintext"} is hexadecimal ciphertext \texttt{BBF316E8D940AF0AD3}; running the code reproduces it.
\end{implementbox}

\subsection{Why RC4 is broken}
\label{ch10:rc4-breaks}

RC4 fails Definition~\ref{ch10:def-prg-secure} because its keystream is \emph{not} indistinguishable from uniform bytes: early output positions carry strong statistical biases, long-term biases persist forever, and its key schedule leaks key bytes when a public IV is concatenated with a secret key.

\begin{attackbox}
\label{ch10:attack-rc4-bias}
\textbf{The biases that killed RC4.}

\emph{Second-byte bias} (Mantin--Shamir, 2001). Whenever the KSA leaves $S[2] = 0$ and $S[1] \ne 2$ -- an event of probability roughly $1/256$ -- trace two steps of PRGA: step~1 swaps $S[1]$ with $S[S[1]]$ without touching $S[2]$, and step~2 adds $S[2] = 0$ to $j$, swaps $S[2]$ with $S[j]$ (putting $0$ into $S[j]$ and $j$ into $S[2]$), and outputs $S[(j + 0) \bmod 256] = S[j] = 0$ with certainty. The remaining $255/256$ of states still output $0$ about $1/256$ of the time, so the second keystream byte $z_2$ equals $0$ with total probability near $2/256$ -- twice the uniform rate $1/256$ (Figure~\ref{ch10:fig-rc4-bias}). If a fixed plaintext byte $m_2$ (such as a character of an HTTP session cookie) is encrypted under many independent random keys, then $c_2 = m_2 \xor z_2$ equals $m_2$ whenever $z_2 = 0$, making $m_2$ appear twice as often as any other byte in the collected ciphertexts.

\emph{Fluhrer--Mantin--Shamir (FMS), 2001.} When RC4's key is $\text{IV} \concat K$ with a public IV (the WEP construction), certain ``weak'' IV values allow the first keystream byte to reveal linear combinations of bytes of $K$. Capturing enough packets with weak IVs recovers $K$ byte by byte.

\emph{Breaking RC4 in TLS} (AlFardan et al., 2013). Combining biases across the first 256 bytes with long-term adjacent-byte (\emph{digraph}) biases found by Fluhrer and McGrew let researchers recover TLS-encrypted HTTP cookies from roughly $2^{30}$ encryptions of the same secret -- feasible when a script in a victim's browser triggers repeated HTTPS requests. That result led browsers and RFC~7465 to ban RC4 altogether.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{trapbox}
A once-popular workaround was \emph{RC4-drop$N$}: discard the first $N$ keystream bytes (say $N = 768$ or $3072$) to skip the worst early biases. Dropping initial bytes slows attacks down, yet long-term digraph biases and key-schedule weaknesses remain. When a cryptographic primitive accumulates workarounds to stay upright, replace it rather than patching it.
\end{trapbox}

\section{ChaCha20: the modern stream cipher}
\label{ch10:chacha}

\begin{applicationbox}
Daniel J.\ Bernstein designed Salsa20 in 2005 for the European eSTREAM stream-cipher competition, then refined it into ChaCha20 in 2008 with faster \emph{diffusion} (spreading each input bit's influence across the state) per round. ChaCha20 is now the standard software stream cipher: it protects TLS~1.3 (paired with Poly1305), the WireGuard virtual private network (VPN), OpenSSH, the Linux kernel's \texttt{getrandom} cryptographically secure pseudorandom number generator (CSPRNG), and mobile devices whose CPUs lack dedicated Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) instructions. Because it uses no secret-dependent table lookups, it runs in \emph{constant time} -- its execution time and memory-access pattern never depend on secret bits -- avoiding the CPU cache-timing side channels that threaten table-based AES software.
\end{applicationbox}

Unlike RC4, ChaCha20 does not step a mutable secret array forward byte by byte. Instead, it works in \emph{counter mode}: to generate the $i$-th 64-byte block of keystream, it evaluates a fixed 512-bit mixing function from scratch on an input built from the secret key, the message nonce, and the block number $i$.

\noindent Everything except the key is public: row~0 encodes the 16-byte ASCII string \texttt{"expand 32-byte k"} so an attacker cannot choose the entire input matrix, and incrementing the counter $b_0$ between blocks gives every 64-byte block its own input.

The core mixing step is the \emph{quarter-round} on four 32-bit words $(a,b,c,d)$. It uses only three operations -- addition modulo $2^{32}$, bitwise XOR, and circular bitwise left rotation -- giving the family name \emph{ARX} (Add, Rotate, XOR):
\begin{align*}
  a &\gets (a + b) \bmod 2^{32}, &\quad d &\gets d \xor a, &\quad d &\gets d \lll 16, \\
  c &\gets (c + d) \bmod 2^{32}, &\quad b &\gets b \xor c, &\quad b &\gets b \lll 12, \\
  a &\gets (a + b) \bmod 2^{32}, &\quad d &\gets d \xor a, &\quad d &\gets d \lll 8, \\
  c &\gets (c + d) \bmod 2^{32}, &\quad b &\gets b \xor c, &\quad b &\gets b \lll 7.
\end{align*}
Here $x \lll r$ denotes a 32-bit left rotation by $r$ bit positions: the bits of $x$ shift $r$ places toward the most-significant end, and the top $r$ bits wrap around into the bottom $r$ positions.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Why those three operations? Addition modulo $2^{32}$ mixes bits with \emph{carries} that ripple from low bits toward high bits; because a carry depends on the AND of two bits, integer addition is nonlinear over $\F_2$. Bitwise XOR combines words without carries. Bitwise rotation moves bit positions \emph{across} the 32-bit word so high bits can influence low bits on the next addition. Alone, each operation is easy to invert and analyze; interleaved over many rounds they produce the \emph{avalanche effect}: flipping a single input bit flips roughly half of the output bits in an unpredictable pattern. And because every CPU executes add, XOR, and rotate in fixed register instructions with no table lookups, ChaCha20 is fast and constant-time on every processor.
\end{intuitionbox}

An odd-numbered round applies the quarter-round to each of the four columns of the $4\times 4$ matrix; an even-numbered round applies it to the four wrap-around diagonals (Figure~\ref{ch10:fig-chacha-qr}). ChaCha20 runs $20$ rounds ($10$ column rounds alternating with $10$ diagonal rounds, hence ``20''). Afterward, it adds each word of the initial matrix modulo $2^{32}$ to the corresponding output word -- a \emph{feed-forward} step that stops an attacker from running the 20 reversible rounds backward from a known keystream block to recover the key. Writing out the sixteen 32-bit words in \emph{little-endian} byte order (least-significant byte of each word first) produces 64 keystream bytes.

\subsection{From core to cipher}
\label{ch10:chacha-cipher}

Given a 256-bit key $k$ and a 96-bit nonce $n$, ChaCha20 evaluates $\operatorname{ChaChaBlock}(k, n, i)$ for block counters $i = 0, 1, 2, \dots$, concatenates the 64-byte outputs (truncating the last block to match the message length), and XORs the stream with the plaintext $m$ (Figure~\ref{ch10:fig-chacha-stream}):
\[
  \text{keystream} = \operatorname{ChaChaBlock}(k, n, 0) \concat \operatorname{ChaChaBlock}(k, n, 1) \concat \cdots,
  \qquad
  c = m \xor \text{keystream}.
\]

\begin{walkbox}
How much data can one $(k, n)$ pair safely encrypt? The 32-bit block counter addresses $2^{32}$ blocks of 64 bytes each, or $2^{32}\cdot 64 = 2^{38}\text{ bytes} = 256\text{ GiB}$ ($256 \times 2^{30}$ bytes, roughly 275 gigabytes) before $b_0$ wraps back to $0$ and repeats keystream. In practice you switch to a fresh nonce per message well before that limit. The 96-bit nonce supports $2^{96}$ possible values per key: a sequential per-message \emph{counter} nonce never collides by construction, while a uniformly \emph{random} 96-bit nonce stays safe up to billions of messages before birthday collisions become a concern (Exercise~\ref{ch10:ex-nonce-budget}). Pick one discipline and never repeat a nonce under the same key.
\end{walkbox}

\begin{implementbox}
\label{ch10:impl-chacha}
\textbf{The ChaCha20 block function -- learning only.} Teaching code: it omits constant-time hardening and message authentication (Poly1305). In real software, call a vetted library such as libsodium, BoringSSL, OpenSSL, or your language's standard cryptography package.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
import struct

def rotl(x, n):
    return ((x << n) | (x >> (32 - n))) & 0xffffffff

def quarter_round(s, a, b, c, d):
    s[a] = (s[a] + s[b]) & 0xffffffff; s[d] = rotl(s[d] ^ s[a], 16)
    s[c] = (s[c] + s[d]) & 0xffffffff; s[b] = rotl(s[b] ^ s[c], 12)
    s[a] = (s[a] + s[b]) & 0xffffffff; s[d] = rotl(s[d] ^ s[a], 8)
    s[c] = (s[c] + s[d]) & 0xffffffff; s[b] = rotl(s[b] ^ s[c], 7)

def chacha20_block(key, counter, nonce):
    const = b"expand 32-byte k"
    state = list(struct.unpack("<4I", const))
    state += list(struct.unpack("<8I", key))      # 256-bit key
    state += [counter]
    state += list(struct.unpack("<3I", nonce))    # 96-bit nonce
    work = state[:]
    for _ in range(10):                           # 10 double-rounds = 20 rounds
        # column rounds
        quarter_round(work, 0, 4, 8, 12)
        quarter_round(work, 1, 5, 9, 13)
        quarter_round(work, 2, 6, 10, 14)
        quarter_round(work, 3, 7, 11, 15)
        # diagonal rounds
        quarter_round(work, 0, 5, 10, 15)
        quarter_round(work, 1, 6, 11, 12)
        quarter_round(work, 2, 7, 8, 13)
        quarter_round(work, 3, 4, 9, 14)
    out = [(work[i] + state[i]) & 0xffffffff for i in range(16)]  # feed-forward
    return struct.pack("<16I", *out)              # 64 keystream bytes

def chacha20(key, nonce, data, counter=0):
    out = bytearray()
    for blk in range(0, len(data), 64):
        ks = chacha20_block(key, counter + blk // 64, nonce)
        chunk = data[blk:blk+64]
        out += bytes(x ^ y for x, y in zip(chunk, ks))
    return bytes(out)
\end{lstlisting}
The feed-forward step \texttt{work[i] + state[i]} is essential: without it, the 20 rounds form an invertible permutation that an attacker could run backward from a single known keystream block to read off the secret key.
\end{implementbox}

\subsection{Why ChaCha20 is trusted}
\label{ch10:chacha-why}

Nobody can unconditionally \emph{prove} ChaCha20 is a secure PRG -- doing so would imply $\mathrm{P} \ne \mathrm{NP}$ (Chapter~8). Confidence rests instead on cryptanalytic margin and structural simplicity.

\begin{whybox}
Two properties stand out. \emph{Security margin:} the best published attacks break reduced-round variants of roughly 7 out of 20 rounds using \emph{differential-linear cryptanalysis} (tracking how chosen input bit differences propagate into biased XOR combinations of output bits, studied by Jean-Philippe Aumasson and others), leaving 13 unbroken rounds of headroom. \emph{Clean structure:} counter mode reduces stream security to one question -- whether $\operatorname{ChaChaBlock}(k, \cdot, \cdot)$ behaves like a random function on distinct $(n, \text{counter})$ inputs. Full diffusion across all 512 bits is reached within 3 to 4 rounds, and the absence of key-dependent branches or memory lookups eliminates timing and cache side channels by design.
\end{whybox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\label{ch10:app-chacha}
\textbf{ChaCha20 in deployment.} In TLS~1.3, the cipher suite \texttt{TLS\_CHACHA20\_POLY1305\_SHA256} pairs ChaCha20 with the Poly1305 message authentication code (MAC) to provide \emph{authenticated encryption with associated data} (AEAD) -- confidentiality and tamper detection together. Browsers and servers prefer it on phones and embedded chips without AES hardware instructions, where it runs faster than AES in Galois/Counter Mode (AES-GCM). WireGuard uses ChaCha20-Poly1305 as its sole data cipher, avoiding cipher-negotiation bugs and \emph{downgrade attacks} (where an active attacker tricks endpoints into picking an older, weaker cipher). OpenSSH offers \texttt{chacha20-poly1305@openssh.com}, and Linux's \texttt{getrandom()} and \texttt{/dev/urandom} draw random bytes from a ChaCha20-based generator. Raw ChaCha20 is never sent over a network without a MAC, because unauthenticated stream ciphers let an active attacker flip chosen plaintext bits at will (Chapter~16).
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{trapbox}
Unauthenticated stream-cipher ciphertext is \emph{malleable} (Figure~\ref{ch10:fig-malleability}). Because $c = m \xor z$, an active attacker who XORs a chosen mask $\Delta$ into the ciphertext produces $c' = c \xor \Delta$, which Bob decrypts as
\begin{align*}
  \Dec_k(c')
  &= (c \xor \Delta) \xor z \\
  &= (m \xor z \xor z) \xor \Delta \;=\; m \xor \Delta.
\end{align*}
Without knowing $k$ or $z$, flipping specific bits of $c$ flips the \emph{exact same} bits of $m$. If Alice encrypts \texttt{"transfer \$0100"} and Mallory XORs $\texttt{'0'} \xor \texttt{'9'} = \texttt{0x30} \xor \texttt{0x39} = \texttt{0x09}$ into the thousands byte of $c$, Bob decrypts \texttt{"transfer \$9100"}. Encryption hides content; it does \emph{not} detect tampering. Always pair encryption with a MAC via an AEAD construction such as ChaCha20-Poly1305 (Chapter~16).
\end{trapbox}

\section{Linear feedback shift registers and the curse of linearity}
\label{ch10:lfsr}

Before fast software ciphers, hardware designs generated keystreams with \emph{linear-feedback shift registers} (LFSRs): a row of $n$ single-bit memory cells that shifts one step every clock tick, emitting the bit that falls off one end and filling the vacated cell with the XOR of selected ``tap'' positions. In silicon an LFSR costs a handful of flip-flops and XOR gates, runs at clock speed, and produces long, \emph{statistically flat} bit sequences (nearly equal counts of zeros, ones, and short bit patterns). Used directly as a keystream, an LFSR also demonstrates why \emph{linear} generators are cryptographically insecure.

\subsection{A tiny LFSR by hand}
\label{ch10:lfsr-example}

An $n$-bit LFSR has $2^n$ possible states. If the register ever holds all zeros ($00\cdots 0$), the XOR of any taps is $0$, so the next state is $00\cdots 0$ again -- a \emph{fixed point} (a state that maps to itself forever). Any cycle of useful states can therefore visit at most the $2^n - 1$ nonzero states, and an LFSR that traverses all $2^n - 1$ of them in one cycle is called \emph{maximal-period}. Algebraically, the tap positions correspond to a feedback polynomial over $\F_2$ (for a 4-bit register tapping $s_1$ and $s_4$, $x^4 + x + 1$); the register achieves maximal period $2^n - 1$ if and only if that polynomial is \emph{primitive}, meaning it is irreducible over $\F_2$ and $x$ generates the full multiplicative group $\F_{2^n}^*$ of order $2^n - 1$ (Chapter~6).

Figure~\ref{ch10:fig-lfsr-arch} shows this maximal-period $4$-bit LFSR: seeded with $(s_1 s_2 s_3 s_4) = 1001$ and tapping $s_1 \xor s_4$, it outputs the rightmost bit $s_4$ each tick, shifts right, and feeds $s_1 \xor s_4$ in on the left ($1001 \to 0100 \to 0010 \to 0001 \to 1000 \to \cdots$), cycling through all $2^4 - 1 = 15$ nonzero states in Panel~B before repeating.

\begin{walkbox}
Trace tick~0 to~1 yourself. State $1001$: output the rightmost bit, $1$. Feedback $= s_1 \xor s_2 = 1 \xor 0 = 1$. Shift all four cells right and drop the new feedback bit into $s_1$: new state $= 1\,|\,100 = 1100$. Tick~1 to~2: output $0$, feedback $1\xor 1 = 0$, new state $0\,|\,110 = 0110$. Because this register does not use a primitive feedback polynomial (and does not even tap the outgoing cell $s_4$), it falls into a short 3-state loop ($0110 \to 1011 \to 1101 \to 0110$) instead of reaching period $15$.
\end{walkbox}

Even when primitive taps give the full $2^n - 1$ period and balanced bit counts, an LFSR output is cryptographically broken on sight.

\subsection{Why linearity is fatal}
\label{ch10:lfsr-weak}

Over the two-element field $\F_2 = \set{0,1}$ (Chapter~6), addition is XOR and multiplication is AND. An \emph{$\F_2$-linear combination} of the seed bits $(s_1, \dots, s_n)$ is the XOR of some subset of those variables (such as $s_1 \xor s_4$). Because each LFSR tick only shifts cells and XORs linear combinations together -- never multiplying two state bits -- every output bit $z_t$ at every future tick $t$ is a fixed $\F_2$-linear combination of the initial seed bits.

\begin{attackbox}
\label{ch10:attack-lfsr}
\textbf{Breaking an LFSR with known plaintext.} Suppose Eve knows (or guesses) a short stretch of plaintext bytes and XORs them with the ciphertext to learn the corresponding keystream bits $z_0, z_1, \dots$ An $n$-bit LFSR has $n$ unknown seed bits $(s_1,\dots,s_n)$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Known taps ($n$ bits suffice):} Each observed keystream bit gives one linear equation over $\F_2$ in the $n$ unknown seed bits. Given $n$ linearly independent equations, Eve solves the $n \times n$ system by \emph{Gaussian elimination over $\F_2$} -- standard row reduction, where adding one equation to another is bitwise XOR -- and recovers $(s_1,\dots,s_n)$, letting her generate every subsequent keystream bit (and, whenever $s_n$ is tapped, step backward to earlier bits too).
  \item \emph{Unknown taps ($2n$ bits suffice):} Every $n$-bit LFSR obeys a fixed \emph{linear recurrence}, expressing each new output bit as the XOR of fixed earlier output bits (for Figure~\ref{ch10:fig-lfsr-arch}, $z_{t+4} = z_{t+3} \xor z_t$). Given $2n$ consecutive keystream bits, the \emph{Berlekamp--Massey algorithm} recovers both the shortest LFSR tap polynomial and its initial state in $O(n^2)$ bit operations. That shortest register length $n$ is the \emph{linear complexity} of the sequence: any generator with small linear complexity falls to a tiny known-plaintext sample.
\end{itemize}
\end{attackbox}

\begin{example}[Solving a 4-bit LFSR over $\F_2$]
\label{ch10:ex-lfsr-solve-demo}
Take the maximal-period 4-bit LFSR of Figure~\ref{ch10:fig-lfsr-arch} ($s_{\text{new}} = s_1 \xor s_4$, outputting the rightmost bit) with unknown initial seed $(s_1, s_2, s_3, s_4)$. If Eve catches the very first four keystream bits $z_0, z_1, z_2, z_3$, the shift wires hand her the seed with no algebra at all, since $z_0 = s_4$, $z_1 = s_3$, $z_2 = s_2$, and $z_3 = s_1$. Suppose instead she joins mid-stream and observes bits $(z_2, z_3, z_4, z_5) = (0, 1, 0, 0)$. Expressing each output in terms of the initial seed gives four linear equations over $\F_2$:
\begin{align*}
  z_2 &= s_2 = 0, \\
  z_3 &= s_1 = 1, \\
  z_4 &= s_1 \xor s_4 = 0, \\
  z_5 &= s_1 \xor s_3 \xor s_4 = 0.
\end{align*}
XORing the second equation ($s_1 = 1$) into the third gives $s_4 = 0 \xor 1 = 1$; XORing the third ($s_1 \xor s_4 = 0$) into the fourth leaves $s_3 = 0$. Eve recovers the full seed $(s_1,s_2,s_3,s_4) = (1,0,0,1)$ from four leaked bits. For a 128-bit LFSR, 128 known bits (16 bytes of known header) recover the entire keystream in microseconds.
\end{example}

\begin{whybox}
Theorem~\ref{ch10:thm-yao} explains the failure in one sentence: a secure PRG must pass the next-bit test, whereas in an $n$-bit LFSR bit $z_{2n}$ is an \emph{exact} linear function of the preceding bits. A predictor running Gaussian elimination or Berlekamp--Massey predicts the next bit with probability $1$, as far from $\tfrac12 + \negl(n)$ as possible. Statistical flatness cannot disguise low linear complexity.
\end{whybox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Berlekamp--Massey was invented in coding theory to decode Bose--Chaudhuri--Hocquenghem (BCH) and Reed--Solomon error-correcting codes (used in QR codes, solid-state drives, and deep-space links), where finding an error pattern reduces to finding the shortest linear recurrence behind a sequence. The companion algorithms volume develops Gaussian elimination over $\F_2$ and $O(n^2)$ recurrence-finding in full.
\end{connectionbox}

\subsection{Trying to rescue LFSRs}
\label{ch10:lfsr-rescue}

Hardware designers kept LFSRs alive in constrained radios by breaking their linearity in two ways: feeding outputs of several LFSRs into a \emph{nonlinear combining function} (using AND gates alongside XOR), or adding \emph{irregular clocking} so one register's bits control whether another register steps.

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Historical attacks on LFSR ciphers: GSM A5/1 and Bluetooth E0.} The 2G Global System for Mobile Communications (GSM) voice cipher \textbf{A5/1} combines three LFSRs of lengths 19, 22, and 23 bits with \emph{majority-vote irregular clocking}: each register has a designated clock-control bit, and at each tick only the registers whose clock bit agrees with the majority of the three step forward. Its 64-bit state and sparse clocking yielded to \emph{time--memory tradeoff} attacks (Alex Biryukov and Adi Shamir, 2000, and later open-source \emph{rainbow-table} projects that precompute compressed chains of state-to-keystream mappings once so individual call keys can be looked up from a few seconds of known speech frames). \textbf{E0}, the classic Bluetooth stream cipher combining four LFSRs, was likewise broken well below brute force. By contrast, \textbf{Trivium} -- a 288-bit eSTREAM hardware finalist built from three shift registers coupled with AND gates -- remains unbroken, showing that shift-register designs require both ample state and thorough nonlinearity.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\label{ch10:app-streams}
\textbf{Where stream ciphers run today.} Hardware-oriented shift-register ciphers survive in cellular radio links: 4G Long-Term Evolution (LTE) and 5G standardize \textbf{SNOW~3G} and \textbf{ZUC}, both built around wide word-oriented LFSRs paired with nonlinear finite-state machines and byte-substitution tables (Chapters~11 and~12). In general-purpose software, encryption standardized on ChaCha20 (TLS~1.3, WireGuard, SSH, operating-system randomness) and on AES run in counter mode (Chapter~13). Every surviving design obeys the three rules established here: stretch a short key nonlinearly into an unpredictable keystream, never reuse a $(k, \text{nonce})$ pair, and authenticate the ciphertext before trusting its decryption.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Summary}
\label{ch10:summary}

A stream cipher replaces the random pad of the one-time pad with the output of a pseudorandom generator $G$. Because the seed is shorter than the message, perfect secrecy is impossible by counting, yet if $G$ is indistinguishable from uniform randomness to all $\PPT$ distinguishers -- equivalently, passes Yao's next-bit test -- a reduction shows the cipher is semantically secure for a single message.

Operational security begins with one rule: \textbf{never reuse a keystream.} Encrypting two messages under the same keystream cancels the pad ($c_1 \xor c_2 = m_1 \xor m_2$) and exposes both plaintexts to crib dragging, as WEP's 24-bit IV demonstrated. RC4 showed a second hazard: measurable keystream biases (Mantin--Shamir's $2/256$ second-byte spike, FMS weak-IV leaks, and multi-byte TLS attacks) break confidentiality across repeated sessions and demand retirement rather than workarounds. ChaCha20 represents modern practice: a 512-bit ARX block core run in counter mode with a 256-bit key, 96-bit nonce, and 32-bit block counter, constant-time by construction and paired with Poly1305 so active attackers cannot exploit XOR malleability. Finally, LFSRs illustrate why statistical balance does not imply cryptographic security: purely $\F_2$-linear generators fall to Gaussian elimination and the Berlekamp--Massey algorithm from $n$ to $2n$ known keystream bits.

Instead of stretching a short key into an ongoing keystream, Chapter~11 takes a second route to symmetric encryption: splitting data into fixed-size blocks and transforming each block through a keyed, reversible permutation -- a \emph{block cipher}.

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch10:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Two-time pad recovery]
\label{ch10:ex-twotime-recover}
A stream cipher reuses keystream $z$ on two 2-byte messages. You intercept (in hexadecimal) $c_1 = \texttt{0x3A1F}$ and $c_2 = \texttt{0x2B0E}$, and you know the first message begins with the ASCII byte \texttt{'H'} (\texttt{0x48}). Compute $c_1 \xor c_2$, then recover the first byte of $m_2$. (Hint: $m_2 = (m_1 \xor m_2) \xor m_1$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[RC4 key schedule by hand]
\label{ch10:ex-rc4-ksa}
Run the RC4 KSA on an array of size \emph{8} instead of 256 (so all index arithmetic is modulo~8), with key bytes $[1, 2]$. Start with $S = [0,1,2,3,4,5,6,7]$ and trace all eight swaps for $i = 0, \dots, 7$, writing $S$ after each step. Then run one PRGA step and give the first keystream value.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Why dropping bytes is not enough]
\label{ch10:ex-rc4-drop}
Explain, using the Mantin--Shamir second-byte bias, why a long-lived secret (such as an HTTP cookie) sent at a \emph{fixed} position in many RC4-encrypted streams is recoverable even though each stream uses a fresh random key. Why does discarding the first 256 keystream bytes (RC4-drop256) reduce the attack speed without eliminating the underlying risk?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[ChaCha quarter-round trace]
\label{ch10:ex-chacha-qr}
Apply one ChaCha20 quarter-round to the four 32-bit words $a = \texttt{0x11111111}$, $b = \texttt{0x01020304}$, $c = \texttt{0x9b8d6f43}$, $d = \texttt{0x01234567}$. Carry out the operations in order (addition modulo $2^{32}$, XOR, left rotation) and give the final $(a,b,c,d)$. Then flip the lowest bit of the input $b$ and check how many output bits change (avalanche).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Nonce budget]
\label{ch10:ex-nonce-budget}
ChaCha20 uses a 32-bit block counter and a 96-bit nonce. (a) How many bytes of keystream can one $(k, \text{nonce})$ pair produce before the counter wraps? (b) If you choose nonces uniformly at random, roughly how many messages can you send under one key before the probability of a nonce collision reaches $\tfrac12$? Use the birthday approximation $n \approx 2^{(b+1)/2}$ from Chapter~7 for a $b$-bit nonce, and state what goes wrong when two messages collide on their nonce.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Cranking an LFSR]
\label{ch10:ex-lfsr-crank}
Take a 4-bit LFSR with feedback bit $s_1 \xor s_2$ (taps at the two leftmost cells), outputting the rightmost bit $s_4$, starting from seed $1011$. Write the state and output bit for the first 8 ticks ($t = 0, \dots, 7$). Does this seed and tap choice reach the maximal period of $15$? What happens if an LFSR ever reaches the all-zero state?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Solve an LFSR by linear algebra]
\label{ch10:ex-lfsr-solve}
An unknown 3-bit LFSR has feedback bit $s_1 \xor s_3$, outputs the rightmost bit $s_3$ each tick, and produces the observed keystream bits $z_0 z_1 z_2 z_3 z_4 = 1,1,0,1,0$. Express $z_0, \dots, z_4$ as linear equations over $\F_2$ in the unknown initial seed $(s_1,s_2,s_3)$, solve for $(s_1,s_2,s_3)$ and verify consistency across all five bits, then predict $z_5$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Malleability]
\label{ch10:ex-malleability}
A banking client encrypts the ASCII string \texttt{AMOUNT=00100} with an unauthenticated stream cipher and sends the ciphertext. An active attacker knows the plaintext format and byte offsets. Specify which ciphertext byte index (0-based) the attacker modifies, and the exact hexadecimal value to XOR into it, so the server decrypts \texttt{AMOUNT=90100} (in ASCII, \texttt{'0'} is \texttt{0x30} and \texttt{'9'} is \texttt{0x39}). What cryptographic mechanism prevents this attack, and why does encryption alone not stop it?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[PRG distinguisher from a bias]
\label{ch10:ex-prg-distinguisher}
Suppose a candidate PRG outputs bits that are each independently $1$ with probability $0.51$ instead of $0.5$. Describe a distinguisher $D$ that, given an $N$-bit output, decides ``PRG'' versus ``random,'' and estimate what $N$ suffices to reach advantage at least $0.9$. (Hint: let $S$ be the number of ones in $N$ bits, compare $\mathbb{E}[S]$ under $p=0.5$ versus $p=0.51$, and apply Chebyshev's inequality $\Pr[\abs{S - \mathbb{E}[S]} \ge t] \le \operatorname{Var}(S)/t^2$ using $\operatorname{Var}(S) = Np(1-p) \le N/4$.) Conclude why even a small per-bit bias disqualifies a generator.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Hybrid argument]
\label{ch10:ex-hybrid}
Let $G$ be a PRG with output length $\ell$. Suppose a distinguisher $D$ satisfies $\Pr[D(G(k))=1] - \Pr[D(r)=1] = \eps > 0$ for uniform $k \getsr \set{0,1}^s$ and $r \getsr \set{0,1}^\ell$. Using the hybrids $H_j$ (first $j$ bits from $G(k)$, last $\ell - j$ bits uniform), prove there exists an index $j \in \{0,\dots,\ell-1\}$ with $\Pr[D(H_{j+1})=1] - \Pr[D(H_j)=1] \ge \eps/\ell$, and show step by step how a predictor given $(g_1,\dots,g_j)$ uses $D$ to output $g_{j+1}$ with probability at least $\tfrac12 + \eps/\ell$.
\end{exercise}
