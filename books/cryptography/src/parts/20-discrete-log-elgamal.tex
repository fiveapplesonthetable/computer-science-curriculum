\chapter{The Discrete Logarithm Problem and ElGamal}

In Chapter~19 we built public-key encryption around the hardness of factoring; in Chapter~18 we met a second one-way function. Given a generator $g$ of a finite group and an exponent $x$, computing $g^x$ takes only a few hundred multiplications even for thousand-bit numbers, whereas recovering $x$ from $g^x$ takes infeasible time for well-chosen groups. That inverse task is the \emph{discrete logarithm problem}, and nearly all of public-key cryptography outside factoring rests on it -- including Diffie--Hellman key exchange, the ElGamal encryption scheme developed here, the digital signature schemes of Chapter~22, and the elliptic-curve cryptography of Chapter~21. How hard is it to recover an exponent in a finite group, what attacks govern key sizes, and how do we turn that hardness into public-key encryption?

\chapterline

\section{The discrete logarithm, precisely}
\label{ch20:dlp}

Fix a finite cyclic group $\G = \langle g \rangle$ of order $n$, written multiplicatively, with generator $g$. That $g$
generates $\G$ means every element of $\G$ equals $g^k$ for some integer $k$, and the map
\[
  k \;\longmapsto\; g^k, \qquad \Zn{n} \longrightarrow \G,
\]
is a bijection (a \emph{group isomorphism}, meaning a bijection that turns addition mod $n$ into group multiplication: $g^{a+b}=g^a g^b$). The \emph{discrete
logarithm} of an element $h \in \G$ to base $g$ is the exponent that produces it.

\begin{definition}[Discrete logarithm]
\label{ch20:def-dlog}
Let $\G = \langle g \rangle$ be cyclic of order $n$. For $h \in \G$, the discrete logarithm
$\dlog_g h$ is the unique residue $x \in \Zn{n}$ with $g^x = h$. The \emph{discrete logarithm
problem} (DLP) is: given $\G$, $g$, and $h$, compute $x$.
\end{definition}

The word ``logarithm'' is the right analogy. Over the real numbers, $\log_b y$ is the
exponent you raise $b > 1$ to in order to get $y$, and finding it is fast because the real exponential $b^x$ is continuous
and strictly increasing -- you can binary-search for the input that hits $y$ (Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-dlog-scramble}a). The word ``discrete'' is the warning. In a finite group
the powers $g^0, g^1, g^2, \dots$ do not march steadily upward; they hop around the group with no
useful numerical order (Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-dlog-scramble}b). There is nothing to binary-search. Even when group elements are written as integers, landing near $h$ tells you nothing about whether your trial exponent $x$ is close to $\dlog_g h$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Think of a dial with $n$ positions and a hand that, on each tick, jumps to a scrambled position determined by multiplying by $g$. Computing where the hand points after $x$ ticks takes only repeated squaring (Chapter~3). But if someone only shows you where the hand ended up, the scrambling gives you no sense of ``warmer'' or ``colder'' -- going forward is a direct calculation, while going backward is a search.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{The concrete case: \texorpdfstring{$\Zns{p}$}{Z\_p*}}

The primary group for classical discrete-log cryptography is $\Zns{p}$, the nonzero residues mod a
prime $p$ under multiplication. This group is cyclic of order $p-1$ (a fact proved in Chapter~6;
every finite subgroup of the multiplicative group of a field is cyclic), so it has
generators. A generator of $\Zns{p}$ is traditionally called a \emph{primitive root} mod $p$.

\begin{example}[A primitive root mod 7]
\label{ch20:ex-modp}
Take $p = 7$, so $\Zns{7} = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$ has order $6$. Try $g = 3$ and list its powers modulo $7$:
\[
  3^0 \equiv 1,\quad 3^1 \equiv 3,\quad 3^2 \equiv 2,\quad 3^3 \equiv 6,\quad 3^4 \equiv 4,\quad 3^5 \equiv 5 \pmod 7,
\]
and $3^6 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$, closing the loop. The powers $1,3,2,6,4,5$ run through every nonzero residue, so $3$
is a primitive root. Now read the table backwards. Because $3^4 \equiv 4 \pmod 7$, $\dlog_3 4 = 4$; because
$3^5 \equiv 5 \pmod 7$, $\dlog_3 5 = 5$. There is no pattern relating the value $4$ to its log $4$, or $5$ to
its log $5$; the coincidence is an artifact of this tiny example. Exponents $0,1,2,3,4,5$
map to residues $1,3,2,6,4,5$.
\end{example}

Even at $p=7$ you can see the difficulty in miniature. Given $g=3$ and $h=6$,
you cannot reason ``$6$ is close to $7$ so the exponent is large.'' You walk the
powers $3^1 \equiv 3$, $3^2 \equiv 2$, $3^3 \equiv 6$ to find exponent $3$. With $p$ a $2048$-bit prime, the table has about $2^{2048}$
rows, far beyond physical reach.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Two flavors of ``hard.''} The DLP is the \emph{search} problem: recover the exponent $x$ from $g^x$.
Cryptography often needs a weaker-looking task to stay hard, the \emph{Computational
Diffie--Hellman} (CDH) problem: given $g$, $g^a$, and $g^b$, compute $g^{ab}$ without necessarily
finding $a$ or $b$. ElGamal's secrecy proof rests on a weaker task still, \emph{Decisional
Diffie--Hellman} (DDH): \emph{distinguish} $g^{ab}$ from a uniform group element. Writing $X \le Y$ for ``problem $X$ is no harder than problem $Y$,'' these form a hierarchy $\text{DDH} \le \text{CDH} \le \text{DLP}$: any efficient algorithm for a problem on the right gives one for the problems to its left. Accordingly, assuming DDH is hard is the strongest assumption of the three, and assuming DLP is hard is the weakest (Chapter~18).
\end{focusbox}

\subsection{Why we believe it is hard}

There is no proof that the discrete logarithm problem is hard -- proving unconditional hardness would imply $\mathrm{P} \ne \mathrm{NP}$ (Chapter~8). Confidence rests instead on decades
of cryptanalysis. In $\Zns{p}$, elements are ordinary integers in $\{1, \dots, p-1\}$, so some powers $g^k \bmod p$ factor completely over a chosen set of small primes called a \emph{factor base}. \emph{Index-calculus} algorithms -- culminating in the general number field sieve (Chapter~8) -- collect enough such factored powers to form a system of linear equations modulo $p-1$ in the discrete logs of those small primes, solve the linear system at once, and then express $\dlog_g h$ in terms of them. Even with that shortcut, the best known classical runtime is \emph{subexponential} (growing faster than every polynomial in the bit-length $\log_2 p$, yet slower than any $2^{\epsilon \log_2 p}$ with $\epsilon > 0$):
\[
  L_p[1/3, c] \;=\; \exp\!\Bigl( (c+o(1))\,(\ln p)^{1/3} (\ln\ln p)^{2/3} \Bigr)
\]
operations. Here $\exp(z) = e^z$, $\ln p$ is proportional to the bit-length of $p$, $c = (64/9)^{1/3} \approx 1.923$ is a positive constant, $o(1)$ denotes lower-order terms that tend to $0$ as $p \to \infty$, and the parameter $1/3$ in $L_p[\alpha, c]$ marks the exponent interpolating between polynomial time ($\alpha = 0$) and full exponential time ($\alpha = 1$). Because integer factoring has this exact $L_N[1/3, c]$ cost, mod-$p$ DLP keys and RSA keys need comparable lengths: $2048$-bit and $3072$-bit primes are standard today. On elliptic-curve groups (Chapter~21), points cannot be factored into a small factor base, so index calculus does not apply; the cost stays fully exponential and a $256$-bit elliptic-curve key matches a $3072$-bit mod-$p$ key in strength.

\begin{historybox}
The hardness of discrete logs is the core ingredient of the 1976 Diffie--Hellman paper, \emph{New Directions in Cryptography} -- the paper that opened public-key cryptography. Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman did not have a public-key \emph{encryption} scheme; they had a key-\emph{exchange} protocol. Taher ElGamal, in his 1985 paper ``A Public Key Cryptosystem and a Signature Scheme Based on Discrete Logarithms,'' turned the Diffie--Hellman idea into both an encryption scheme and a signature scheme. Because ElGamal did not patent his algorithm, NIST based its 1994 Digital Signature Algorithm (DSA) on an ElGamal variant, triggering a licensing dispute with Public Key Partners (which held the Diffie--Hellman and Merkle patents). ElGamal later became chief scientist at Netscape and helped design Secure Sockets Layer (SSL), the ancestor of Transport Layer Security (TLS), putting the ideas of this chapter directly into web browsers.
\end{historybox}

\section{Attacking the DLP: generic algorithms}
\label{ch20:generic}

Two classic algorithms attack the DLP in \emph{any}
cyclic group using only the group operation and equality tests, without exploiting how elements are represented.
These \emph{generic} attacks set a baseline cost that no DLP-based scheme can avoid. A theorem of Victor Shoup (1997) proves that any generic algorithm needs at least on the order of $\sqrt{n}$ group
operations in a prime-order group of size $n$, and both algorithms below match that bound.

The main consequence: \textbf{against generic attacks, the security of a DLP group is governed by the largest prime factor
of its order, at a cost of roughly the square root of that prime.} If the order $n$ splits into small prime factors, the Pohlig--Hellman attack solves the discrete log modulo each prime-power factor separately and reassembles the answer (Section~\ref{ch20:params} and Exercise~\ref{ch20:exc-pohlig}). In $\Zns{p}$, where $n = p - 1$ is always even, we therefore choose a \emph{safe prime} $p = 2q + 1$ with $q$ also prime, making $n = 2q$ and working inside the order-$q$ subgroup.

\subsection{Baby-step giant-step}

Daniel Shanks's baby-step giant-step (BSGS) algorithm is a time-memory trade-off. Given $g$ and $h = g^x$ in a group of order $n$, we want $x \in \{0, \dots, n-1\}$. Set $m = \ceil{\sqrt{n}}$ (the smallest integer at least $\sqrt{n}$) and write $x$ in base $m$ with quotient $i$ and remainder $j$:
\[
  x \;=\; i \cdot m + j, \qquad 0 \le i < m,\quad 0 \le j < m.
\]
Every $x < n$ has such a representation because $i \cdot m + j$ covers all integers from $0$ through $m^2 - 1 \ge n - 1$. Letting $g^{-m} = (g^m)^{-1}$ denote the group inverse of $g^m$, rewrite $g^{im+j} = h$ as
\[
  g^{j} \;=\; h \cdot (g^{-m})^{i}.
\]
The left side depends only on $j$ (the ``baby steps,'' single-step powers of $g$); the right side depends only
on $i$ (the ``giant steps,'' jumps by $g^{-m}$). The algorithm runs in two phases:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Baby steps.} Compute $g^0, g^1, \dots, g^{m-1}$ and store each pair $(g^j, j)$ in a hash table (a lookup table giving $O(1)$ average search time by group element). Cost: $m$ multiplications and $m$ table entries.
  \item \textbf{Giant steps.} Compute $u = g^{-m}$ once. Then for $i = 0, 1, \dots, m-1$, compute
    $h \cdot u^{i}$ and look it up in the table. As soon as $h \cdot u^{i}$ matches a stored
    $g^{j}$, we have $g^{j} = h \cdot g^{-im}$, so $x = i \cdot m + j$.
\end{enumerate}

Total work is $O(\sqrt{n})$ group operations and $O(\sqrt{n})$ memory. For $n \approx 2^{256}$, $\sqrt{n} \approx 2^{128}$ operations and $2^{128}$ table entries remain infeasible, yet that square-root speedup over $2^{256}$ brute-force trials is why a group must have prime order at least $2^{256}$ to deliver $128$-bit security.

\begin{example}[BSGS by hand, $p=29$]
\label{ch20:ex-bsgs}
Let $p = 29$, so $\Zns{29}$ has order $n = 28$, and take the primitive root $g = 2$. We want $\dlog_2 17$. Set
$m = \ceil{\sqrt{28}} = 6$.

\emph{Baby steps} -- powers $2^j \bmod 29$ for $j = 0, \dots, 5$:
\[
  2^0 \equiv 1,\quad 2^1 \equiv 2,\quad 2^2 \equiv 4,\quad 2^3 \equiv 8,\quad 2^4 \equiv 16,\quad 2^5 = 32 \equiv 3 \pmod{29}.
\]
Store the table $\{1{:}0,\ 2{:}1,\ 4{:}2,\ 8{:}3,\ 16{:}4,\ 3{:}5\}$.

\emph{Giant-step multiplier.} We need $u = 2^{-6} \bmod 29$. First $2^{6} = 64 \equiv 6 \pmod{29}$, and
$6^{-1} \equiv 5 \pmod{29}$ since $6 \cdot 5 = 30 \equiv 1 \pmod{29}$. Thus $u = 5$.

\emph{Giant steps} -- compute $17 \cdot 5^{i} \bmod 29$ for $i = 0, 1, \dots$ and check the table:
\begin{align*}
  i = 0:\quad 17 \cdot 5^0 &\equiv 17 \pmod{29} && \text{(not in table)} \\
  i = 1:\quad 17 \cdot 5\phantom{^0} &= 85 \equiv 27 \pmod{29} && \text{(not in table)} \\
  i = 2:\quad 27 \cdot 5\phantom{^0} &= 135 \equiv 19 \pmod{29} && \text{(not in table)} \\
  i = 3:\quad 19 \cdot 5\phantom{^0} &= 95 \equiv 8 \pmod{29} && \text{(\textbf{hit:} matches $2^3 \equiv 8$, so $j = 3$).}
\end{align*}
Thus $i = 3$, $j = 3$, and $x = i \cdot m + j = 3\cdot 6 + 3 = 21$ (Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-bsgs}). To verify: $2^5 \equiv 3$, so $2^{10} \equiv 9$, $2^{20} \equiv 81 \equiv 23$, and $2^{21} \equiv 46 \equiv 17 \pmod{29}$, confirming $\dlog_2 17 = 21$.
\end{example}

\begin{connectionbox}
BSGS is the same idea as the meet-in-the-middle attack on double encryption from Chapter~11,
and the same idea as a two-sided search in the algorithms companion. You trade an
exponential-time one-sided search for two square-root-sized searches that ``meet'' through a hash
table. The cost analysis -- $O(\sqrt{n})$ time, $O(\sqrt{n})$ space, balanced when both halves have size
$\sqrt{n}$ -- is a classic time-memory trade-off.
\end{connectionbox}

\subsection{Pollard's rho: square-root time, constant memory}

BSGS has a practical bottleneck: it needs $\sqrt{n}$ \emph{memory}, and at cryptographic sizes memory runs out long before time does. John Pollard's rho method (1978) achieves the same $O(\sqrt{n})$
\emph{time} using only a constant number of group elements in memory, replacing the stored table with a
pseudorandom walk and the birthday bound.

Start at $x_0 = 1 = g^0 h^0$ and define a deterministic ``random-looking'' step function $x_{k+1} = f(x_k)$ on the group that multiplies the current element by $g$, multiplies it by $h$, or squares it, depending on which subset of $\G$ the current element falls into. At every step $k$ we update known exponents $a_k, b_k \in \Zn{n}$ satisfying
\[
  x_k \;=\; g^{a_k} h^{b_k}.
\]
Because the group is finite, the sequence $x_0, x_1, x_2, \dots$ must eventually repeat: after a tail of $\mu$ distinct elements it enters a cycle of length $\lambda$ at $x_\mu = x_{\mu+\lambda}$, tracing the shape of the Greek letter $\rho$ (Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-rho}). By the birthday
bound (Chapter~14), the first repeat $x_i = x_j$ ($i \ne j$) appears after an average of about
$\sqrt{\pi n / 2}$ steps. Equating the two representations of that collided element gives $x$:
\begin{align*}
  g^{a_i} h^{b_i} &= g^{a_j} h^{b_j} && \text{(collision $x_i = x_j$)} \\
  g^{a_i - a_j} &= h^{b_j - b_i} = g^{x(b_j - b_i)} && \text{(rearranging, since $h = g^x$)} \\
  a_i - a_j &\equiv x\,(b_j - b_i) \pmod n && \text{(comparing exponents in $\G$ of order $n$).}
\end{align*}
Whenever $\gcd(b_j - b_i, n) = 1$ (which holds automatically when $n$ is prime and $b_j \not\equiv b_i \pmod n$), $b_j - b_i$ has a multiplicative inverse modulo $n$ and
\[
  x \;\equiv\; (a_i - a_j)\,(b_j - b_i)^{-1} \pmod n.
\]

To detect a collision without storing past states, Pollard uses Robert Floyd's cycle-finding (``tortoise-and-hare'') algorithm: in round $k$, the slow pointer (\emph{tortoise}) holds $T_k = x_k$ by applying $f$ once, while the fast pointer (\emph{hare}) holds $H_k = x_{2k}$ by applying $f$ twice. Once both enter the cycle, the hare closes the gap by one position per round and must land on the tortoise within $\mu + \lambda$ rounds, keeping only two group elements and four exponents in memory.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why $\sqrt{n}$ and not less, generically.} Both BSGS and rho are instances of a general
theorem: in a \emph{black-box group} -- an idealized model where an algorithm may only multiply elements, invert them, and test equality without inspecting their bit encodings -- any
algorithm needs $\Omega(\sqrt{n})$ operations to find a discrete log in a group of prime order $n$ (Shoup, 1997). The reason is
the birthday bound in reverse: until two computed combinations $g^{a_i} h^{b_i}$ and $g^{a_j} h^{b_j}$ collide, every query looks like a fresh distinct label, and collisions in a set of size $n$ take on the order of $\sqrt{n}$ queries. Index-calculus attacks on $\Zns{p}$ beat $\sqrt{n}$ by inspecting integer bit patterns to factor residues into small primes; elliptic curves offer no known analogue of prime factorization and therefore retain the $\sqrt{n}$ bound.
\end{whybox}

\begin{example}[Extracting $x$ from a rho collision]
\label{ch20:ex-rho}
The exponent bookkeeping goes cleanest in a prime-order
group, where every nonzero difference modulo the order is invertible. Work in a subgroup of prime order
$q = 11$ with generator $g$ and target $h = g^{x}$. Suppose the walk finds a collision between
$x_i = g^{8} h^{2}$ and $x_j = g^{3} h^{9}$. Equating them gives
\begin{align*}
  g^{8} h^{2} &= g^{3} h^{9} \\
  g^{8-3} &= h^{9-2} = g^{7x} && \text{(since $h = g^x$)} \\
  5 &\equiv 7x \pmod{11} && \text{(since $\ord(g) = 11$).}
\end{align*}
Because $7 \cdot 8 = 56 \equiv 1 \pmod{11}$, we have $7^{-1} \equiv 8 \pmod{11}$, giving
\[
  x \;\equiv\; 5 \cdot 8 \;=\; 40 \;\equiv\; 7 \pmod{11}.
\]
If the group order were composite and $b_j - b_i$ shared a factor with it, $(b_j - b_i)^{-1}$ would not exist directly -- we would either test a few candidates or restart the walk with a randomized starting point.
\end{example}

\begin{implementbox}
A compact Pollard rho implementation for discrete logs in $\Zns{p}$, with the exponent bookkeeping spelled out.
\textbf{Learning only -- this code lacks constant-time discipline (its runtime varies with the inputs, which can leak secrets via timing; see Chapter~37) and parameter validation, and is meant solely to make the algorithm concrete.}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
from math import gcd

def pollard_rho_dlog(g, h, p):
    n = p - 1                      # order of the group Z_p^*
    def step(x, a, b):
        # partition the group into 3 zones by x mod 3
        if   x % 3 == 0:           # zone 0: multiply by g
            return (x*g) % p, (a+1) % n, b
        elif x % 3 == 1:           # zone 1: multiply by h
            return (x*h) % p, a, (b+1) % n
        else:                      # zone 2: square
            return (x*x) % p, (2*a) % n, (2*b) % n
    # tortoise (one step) and hare (two steps)
    x, a, b = 1, 0, 0
    X, A, B = 1, 0, 0
    for _ in range(n):
        x, a, b = step(x, a, b)
        X, A, B = step(*step(X, A, B))
        if x == X:                 # collision found
            r = (b - B) % n        # need to solve  r * dlog = (A - a)  (mod n)
            if r == 0:
                return None        # rare degenerate case; restart with new walk
            t = (A - a) % n
            d = gcd(r, n)          # n is composite here, so d may exceed 1
            if t % d != 0:
                return None        # no solution this run; restart with new walk
            # d candidate residues mod n; exponentiate to pick the right one
            base = (t // d) * pow(r // d, -1, n // d) % (n // d)
            for k in range(d):
                cand = (base + k * (n // d)) % n
                if pow(g, cand, p) == h % p:
                    return cand
            return None
    return None

# Demo (toy parameters): dlog of 17 base 2 mod 29 prints 21.
print(pollard_rho_dlog(2, 17, 29))
\end{lstlisting}
The single modular inverse of prime-order Example~\ref{ch20:ex-rho} becomes a \texttt{gcd}
check and candidate loop here because $n = p-1$ is even (hence composite): the difference $r$ can share a
factor $d$ with $n$, leaving $d$ candidate logs that one modular exponentiation each resolves. In a
prime-order subgroup that branch collapses to a single inverse -- one more reason deployed protocols work in prime-order subgroups. The cost is $O(\sqrt{n})$ multiplications and a handful of integers in memory. For $n \approx 2^{256}$ that is about $2^{128}$ multiplications with no giant table to store, which is why Pollard's rho (along with parallelized multi-walk variants) is the generic attack used to set elliptic-curve key sizes.
\end{implementbox}

\section{From hard problem to encryption: ElGamal}
\label{ch20:elgamal}

ElGamal turns Diffie--Hellman key exchange into public-key encryption with one extra step.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Recall Diffie--Hellman (Chapter~18). Alice and Bob agree on a generator $g$ of a public group. Alice picks a secret $a$ and
publishes $A = g^a$; Bob picks a secret $b$ and publishes $B = g^b$. Both compute the shared secret
$g^{ab} = A^b = B^a$, while an eavesdropper Eve seeing $(g, g^a, g^b)$ faces the CDH problem.

ElGamal's insight is that Alice does not need to be online. Her published value $A = g^a$ serves as her permanent public
key. Whenever Bob wants to send Alice a message $M$, he runs his half of a
\emph{fresh} Diffie--Hellman exchange against $A$: he picks a one-time random exponent $y$, forms $c_1 = g^y$, computes the
shared secret $s = A^y = g^{ay}$, and masks $M$ by multiplying inside the group, sending
$c_2 = M \cdot s$. Alice, holding $a$, reconstructs the same mask from $c_1$ via
$s = c_1^{a} = g^{ya}$, multiplies by $s^{-1}$, and recovers $M$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{The scheme}

\begin{definition}[ElGamal encryption]
\label{ch20:def-elgamal}
Fix a cyclic group $\G = \langle g \rangle$ of prime order $q$ in which DDH is believed hard, with plaintexts $M$ represented as elements of $\G$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Key generation $\Gen$:} sample $a \getsr \{1,\dots,q-1\}$ uniformly at random and set $A = g^{a}$. Output the public
    key $pk = (\G, g, A)$ and secret key $sk = a$.
  \item \textbf{Encryption $\Enc_{pk}(M)$:} for $M \in \G$, sample a fresh uniform $y \getsr \{1,\dots,q-1\}$ and output the
    ciphertext
    \[
      (c_1, c_2) \;=\; \bigl(g^{y},\; M \cdot A^{y}\bigr).
    \]
  \item \textbf{Decryption $\Dec_{sk}(c_1, c_2)$:} compute $s = c_1^{a}$, find its group inverse $s^{-1} \in \G$, and output
    $M = c_2 \cdot s^{-1}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Correctness.} Both parties arrive at the same blinding mask $s \in \G$:
\begin{align*}
  c_1^{a} &= (g^{y})^{a} = g^{ay} = (g^{a})^{y} = A^{y}, \\
  c_2 \cdot (c_1^a)^{-1} &= (M \cdot A^y) \cdot (A^y)^{-1} = M.
\end{align*}
The factor $A^y$ is the Diffie--Hellman shared secret between Alice's long-term public key $A=g^a$ and Bob's ephemeral public value
$c_1 = g^y$.
\end{whybox}

\begin{example}[ElGamal by hand, $p = 23$]
\label{ch20:ex-elgamal}
Work in $\Zns{23}$ so the arithmetic fits by hand. Its order is $22$ (not prime, so this full group is insecure -- Section~\ref{ch20:params} explains why), but the mechanics are identical. Take the primitive root $g = 5$.

\emph{Key generation.} Alice picks secret key $a = 6$ and computes her public key $A = 5^6 \bmod 23$:
\[
  5^2 = 25 \equiv 2,\qquad 5^3 \equiv 10,\qquad A = 5^6 = (5^3)^2 \equiv 100 \equiv 8 \pmod{23}.
\]

\emph{Encryption of $M = 10$.} Bob picks one-time randomness $y = 3$ and computes
\begin{align*}
  c_1 &= g^{y} = 5^{3} = 125 \equiv 10 \pmod{23}, \\
  s &= A^{y} = 8^{3} = 8 \cdot 64 \equiv 8 \cdot 18 = 144 \equiv 6 \pmod{23}, \\
  c_2 &= M \cdot s = 10 \cdot 6 = 60 \equiv 14 \pmod{23},
\end{align*}
sending the ciphertext $(c_1, c_2) = (10, 14)$.

\emph{Decryption.} Alice reconstructs $s = c_1^a = 10^6 \bmod 23$, inverts it, and unmasks $M$:
\begin{align*}
  s &= 10^6 = (10^2)^3 \equiv 8^3 \equiv 6 \pmod{23}, \\
  s^{-1} &\equiv 6^{-1} \equiv 4 \pmod{23} && \text{(since $6 \cdot 4 = 24 \equiv 1 \pmod{23}$)}, \\
  M &= c_2 \cdot s^{-1} = 14 \cdot 4 = 56 \equiv 10 \pmod{23}.
\end{align*}
Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-elgamal} traces the full exchange.
\end{example}

\subsection{Why it is secure}

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Threat model.} The adversary is a probabilistic polynomial-time ($\PPT$) eavesdropper who sees the
public key and a ciphertext. The security target is \emph{indistinguishability under
chosen-plaintext attack} (IND-CPA, Chapter~13): she chooses two plaintexts $M_0, M_1 \in \G$, receives an encryption of $M_b$ for a random bit $b \getsr \{0,1\}$, and cannot guess $b$ with probability non-negligibly better than $1/2$. Raw ElGamal does \emph{not} achieve \emph{indistinguishability under chosen-ciphertext attack} (IND-CCA, Chapter~16), where the attacker may also query a \emph{decryption oracle} that decrypts any ciphertext other than the challenge ciphertext itself: as Subsection~\ref{ch20:homomorphic} shows, raw ElGamal ciphertexts are malleable, so deployed systems wrap the shared secret in authenticated encryption.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{definition}[Decisional Diffie--Hellman]
\label{ch20:def-ddh}
Let $\G = \langle g\rangle$ have prime order $q$. For an algorithm $\mathcal{D}$ and independent, uniformly random exponents $a, b, c \getsr \{0,\dots,q-1\}$, define the DDH distinguishing advantage
\[
  \Adv^{\mathrm{ddh}}_{\G}(\mathcal{D})
  \;=\;
  \Bigl|\Pr\bigl[\mathcal{D}(g, g^{a}, g^{b}, g^{ab}) = 1\bigr]
  - \Pr\bigl[\mathcal{D}(g, g^{a}, g^{b}, g^{c}) = 1\bigr]\Bigr|.
\]
DDH is \emph{hard} in $\G$ if $\Adv^{\mathrm{ddh}}_{\G}(\mathcal{D})$ is negligible for every $\PPT$ distinguisher $\mathcal{D}$: a real Diffie--Hellman tuple $(g, g^a, g^b, g^{ab})$ is computationally indistinguishable from one whose fourth element $g^c$ is uniform and independent.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[ElGamal is IND-CPA under DDH]
\label{ch20:thm-ind-cpa}
If DDH is hard in the prime-order group $\G$, then ElGamal encryption over $\G$ is IND-CPA secure.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Proof sketch.} Let $\mathcal{A}$ be any $\PPT$ IND-CPA adversary against ElGamal. In the real IND-CPA game, $\mathcal{A}$ receives $pk = g^a$ and challenge ciphertext $(g^y,\ M_b \cdot g^{ay})$ for a random bit $b \getsr \{0,1\}$ (Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-ind-cpa-hybrid}, left).

Step to a hybrid experiment (Chapter~7) in which the mask $g^{ay}$ is replaced by an independent, uniformly random group element $g^c$ (Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-ind-cpa-hybrid}, right). Given a DDH challenge $(g, g^a, g^y, z)$, a reduction $\mathcal{B}$ picks $b \getsr \{0,1\}$, hands $\mathcal{A}$ the public key $g^a$ and ciphertext $(g^y, M_b \cdot z)$, and outputs $1$ if $\mathcal{A}$ guesses $b$ correctly: when $z = g^{ay}$ this simulates real ElGamal (up to the at most $2/q$ probability that $a = 0$ or $y = 0$), and when $z = g^c$ it simulates the hybrid.

In the hybrid world, $g^c$ is uniform on $\G$ and independent of $M_b$. Because multiplication by $M_b$ is a bijection on $\G$, the product $M_b \cdot g^c$ is also uniformly distributed over $\G$ regardless of $b$ -- a group one-time pad carrying zero information about $b$, so $\mathcal{A}$ wins with probability exactly $1/2$. Therefore
\[
  \Adv^{\mathrm{ind\text{-}cpa}}_{\mathrm{ElGamal}}(\mathcal{A})
  \;=\;
  2\Bigl|\Pr[b' = b] - \frac{1}{2}\Bigr|
  \;\le\;
  2\,\Adv^{\mathrm{ddh}}_{\G}(\mathcal{B}) + \frac{4}{q},
\]
which is negligible whenever DDH holds in $\G$.
\end{whybox}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{DDH fails in the full group $\Zns{p}$.} In $\Zns{p}$ for an odd prime $p$, half the elements are \emph{quadratic residues} (QRs -- squares $z^2 \bmod p$, identified efficiently by Euler's criterion $x^{(p-1)/2} \equiv +1 \pmod p$, the Legendre test of Chapter~18) and half are quadratic non-residues ($\equiv -1$). For a primitive root $g$, $g^k$ is a QR if and only if $k$ is even. Consequently, $g^{ab}$ is a QR if and only if $a$ is even or $b$ is even -- a fact an attacker can read directly off $g^a$ and $g^b$, whereas a uniform $g^c$ is a QR with probability $1/2$. Comparing the predicted and actual Legendre symbols breaks DDH (Exercise~\ref{ch20:exc-legendre}). \textbf{Fix:} choose a safe prime $p = 2q + 1$ and work inside the order-$q$ subgroup of quadratic residues, where every element has Legendre symbol $+1$ and the leak disappears.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{The homomorphic property}
\label{ch20:homomorphic}

ElGamal has an algebraic property that can be either a feature or a vulnerability:
multiplying two ciphertexts produces an encryption of the product of their plaintexts (Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-homomorphic}). Take encryptions of $M$ and
$M'$ under the same public key $A$, with independent randomness $y, y'$:
\[
  (c_1, c_2) = \bigl(g^{y},\; M A^{y}\bigr),
  \qquad
  (c_1', c_2') = \bigl(g^{y'},\; M' A^{y'}\bigr).
\]
Multiplying componentwise in $\G \times \G$ gives
\[
  (c_1 c_1',\; c_2 c_2')
  \;=\;
  \bigl(g^{y+y'},\; (M M')\,A^{y+y'}\bigr),
\]
which is a valid ElGamal encryption of $M M'$ under $A$ with combined randomness $y + y' \bmod q$. Because encryption preserves multiplication, ElGamal is \emph{multiplicatively homomorphic}.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Homomorphism lets an untrusted party compute on encrypted data without decrypting it. A tallying server can multiply encrypted ballots together, and only the keyholder learns the combined result -- never the individual inputs. Chapter~33 develops homomorphic encryption further. Encoding integer messages $v_i$ in the exponent as $g^{v_i}$ (\emph{exponential ElGamal}) turns ciphertext multiplication into \emph{addition} $g^{\sum v_i}$ of plaintexts, which fits vote counting whenever the final sum $\sum v_i$ is small enough to recover by a short BSGS discrete-log search.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Malleability: the flip side.} The same algebra breaks chosen-ciphertext security. Suppose
Eve intercepts a ciphertext $(c_1, c_2)$ for an unknown bid $M$ and wants to double it to $2M$. Without knowing $pk$'s secret $a$ or $M$, she forwards $(c_1,\ 2 c_2)$, which decrypts to
$2 c_2 \cdot s^{-1} = 2M$ (Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-elgamal-malleability}a). Worse, given a decryption oracle that refuses only the exact challenge $(c_1, c_2)$, Eve recovers $M$ in one query: pick a known random $r \in \G$, ask the oracle to decrypt $(c_1,\ r\cdot c_2)$ to obtain $r M$, and multiply by $r^{-1}$ to read $M$ (Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-elgamal-malleability}b).

Because of this malleability, raw ElGamal is never deployed directly for general encryption. Instead, production systems use the \emph{Key Encapsulation Mechanism / Data Encapsulation Mechanism} (KEM/DEM) hybrid pattern, standardized over elliptic curves as the \emph{Elliptic Curve Integrated Encryption Scheme} (ECIES): pass the Diffie--Hellman secret $g^{ay}$ through a key-derivation function (KDF) such as HKDF to derive symmetric keys, and protect the message with authenticated encryption (AES-GCM or encrypt-then-MAC, Chapter~16) so any tampering causes decryption to reject.
\end{attackbox}

\section{Choosing safe groups and parameters}
\label{ch20:params}

Deploying ElGamal or Diffie--Hellman safely comes down to three parameter rules.

\textbf{1. Use a prime-order subgroup.} Work in a subgroup of prime order $q$, not in the full group $\Zns{p}$.
The standard construction picks a \emph{safe prime} $p = 2q + 1$ with $q$ also prime. The
quadratic residues mod $p$ then form a cyclic subgroup of prime order $q$, and squaring any $h \in \{2, \dots, p-2\}$ gives a generator $g = h^2 \bmod p$ of that subgroup. Prime order buys several protections at once: by Lagrange's theorem every non-identity element has order $q$, there are no smaller nontrivial subgroups, the Pohlig--Hellman decomposition (Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-pohlig-hellman}) has no small prime factors to exploit, and DDH's Legendre-symbol leak vanishes.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Why prime order stops Pohlig--Hellman and small-subgroup attacks.} Suppose a group's order factors into prime powers as $n = q_1^{e_1} q_2^{e_2} \cdots q_k^{e_k}$. For any prime factor $q_i \mid n$, raising $g$ and $h = g^x$ to the power $n / q_i$ projects the problem into a subgroup of order $q_i$, revealing $x \bmod q_i$ in $O(\sqrt{q_i})$ steps; lifting to $q_i^{e_i}$ takes $O(e_i \sqrt{q_i})$ steps, and the Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT, Chapter~4) reassembles $x \bmod n$ from the pieces. Consequently, any small factor $q_i$ leaks $x \bmod q_i$ cheaply, and an order built entirely from small primes -- even $2^{40}$ times other small primes -- falls on a laptop in a few dozen tiny discrete-log steps rather than $\sqrt{n}$. Separately, if a group contains a small subgroup of order $r$, an active attacker can substitute an element of order $r$ to confine a shared secret to at most $r$ values. Working in a verified subgroup of prime order $q$ eliminates both attacks: $q$ is the sole prime factor, so generic recovery costs the full $\sqrt{q}$.
\end{focusbox}

\textbf{3. Fresh, uniform randomness for $y$ every time.} The per-message exponent $y$ must be
sampled fresh from a cryptographic random generator and never reused.

\begin{attackbox}
\textbf{Nonce reuse breaks ElGamal completely.} Suppose Bob reuses the same exponent $y$ for two plaintexts
$M_1, M_2 \in \G$. The ciphertexts $(g^y, M_1 A^y)$ and $(g^y, M_2 A^y)$ share the same first component $c_1 = g^y$, revealing the reuse on the wire, and multiplying $c_2 = M_1 A^y$ by the inverse of $c_2' = M_2 A^y$ cancels the mask $A^y$:
\[
  (M_1 A^y) \cdot (M_2 A^y)^{-1} \;=\; M_1 \cdot M_2^{-1},
\]
often written $M_1 / M_2$ in the group. An eavesdropper learns the group quotient of the two plaintexts without knowing $a$ or $y$; if she knows $M_1$ (say from a predictable header), she recovers $M_2 = M_1 \cdot (M_1 M_2^{-1})^{-1}$ immediately. As with IV reuse in stream ciphers (Chapter~10) and nonce reuse in AES-GCM (Chapter~16), reusing per-message randomness breaks confidentiality. In the Digital Signature Algorithm (DSA, Chapter~22), reusing $y$ is worse still: it reveals the signer's \emph{secret key}, the flaw that broke code signing on the Sony PlayStation~3.
\end{attackbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
The cost of the \emph{forward} direction -- computing $g^y$, $A^y$, and $c_1^a$ -- is square-and-multiply
modular exponentiation (Chapter~3): $O(\log q)$ modular multiplications on $(\log_2 p)$-bit integers. Public-key cryptography lives in the gap between that polynomial $O(\log q)$ forward cost and the exponential $O(\sqrt{q})$ (or subexponential $L_p[1/3, c]$) backward cost.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Where ElGamal ideas run in practice.} Raw mod-$p$ ElGamal is uncommon in modern network transport -- modular exponentiation on $3072$-bit integers is slow and ciphertexts are twice as long as plaintexts -- yet its design appears across deployed cryptography:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GnuPG / OpenPGP} (email and file encryption tools) supports ElGamal encryption keys paired with DSA
    signing keys: ElGamal encrypts a random symmetric session key, which then encrypts the bulk file or email.
  \item \textbf{ECIES} moves ElGamal onto an elliptic-curve group in KEM/DEM form: derive symmetric keys from the Diffie--Hellman secret via a KDF, then apply authenticated encryption. It is a standard public-key encryption construction in messaging protocols and hardware wallets.
  \item \textbf{TLS} uses ephemeral Diffie--Hellman (\textbf{DHE}) and elliptic-curve ephemeral Diffie--Hellman (\textbf{ECDHE}) per session to achieve forward secrecy -- the subject of Chapter~23.
  \item \textbf{Cryptographic voting} systems (such as Helios) use exponential ElGamal for its additive homomorphism, multiplying encrypted ballots to tally totals without decrypting individual votes.
\end{itemize}
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Relationship to Diffie--Hellman}
\label{ch20:dh-relationship}

Diffie--Hellman key exchange and ElGamal encryption share the same algebra and differ in interaction:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Diffie--Hellman key exchange} is interactive: both parties pick fresh ephemeral exponents $a, b$, exchange $g^a$ and $g^b$, and derive a shared key from $g^{ab}$. Neither party sends a message during the exchange; CDH prevents key recovery and DDH ensures $g^{ab}$ is indistinguishable from random.
  \item \textbf{ElGamal encryption} is non-interactive: the recipient publishes $A = g^a$ once and can be offline when the sender picks a fresh $y$ and transmits $(g^y,\ M \cdot A^y)$. Confidentiality rests on DDH (Theorem~\ref{ch20:thm-ind-cpa}).
\end{itemize}

The shared mask $g^{ay}$ in ElGamal plays the exact role of $g^{ab}$ in Diffie--Hellman, and the hardness hierarchy tracks what breaks if an assumption fails: solving DLP recovers secret keys outright; solving CDH lets an eavesdropper compute $g^{ab}$ and unmask ElGamal ciphertexts without finding $a$; breaking only DDH may not recover $M$ in full, yet distinguishes ciphertexts well enough to break IND-CPA.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Summary.} Forward exponentiation $(g, x) \mapsto g^x$ takes $O(\log q)$ multiplications; inverting $(g, g^x) \mapsto x$ is the DLP. Diffie--Hellman uses two fresh exponents to agree on a shared secret across that gap, while ElGamal makes the recipient's exponent long-lived so any sender can mask a message with a one-time secret $g^{ay}$.
\end{focusbox}

\section{Capabilities and Limitations of ElGamal}
\label{ch20:roadmap}

Standing alongside RSA (Chapter~19), ElGamal illustrates both what discrete-log encryption buys and what working in $\Zns{p}$ costs.

\begin{whybox}
Unlike textbook RSA, which is deterministic and leaks when two plaintexts are equal, ElGamal samples a fresh exponent $y$ on every call to $\Enc$, so encrypting the same message twice produces independent ciphertexts and achieves IND-CPA security directly under DDH. At the same time, its multiplicative homomorphism $(c_1 c_1', c_2 c_2') = \Enc(M M')$ supports computing on encrypted data, zero-knowledge proofs (Chapter~31), and verifiable electronic voting.
\end{whybox}

\begin{trapbox}
Over $\Zns{p}$, those capabilities carry three concrete costs. First, every ciphertext $(c_1, c_2)$ holds two group elements, doubling ciphertext size ($6144$ bits for a $3072$-bit modulus). Second, subexponential index-calculus attacks $L_p[1/3, c]$ force $p$ to roughly $3072$ bits for $128$-bit security. Third, raw ElGamal is malleable under chosen-ciphertext attacks and must be wrapped in a KEM/DEM construction in practice -- while remaining, like RSA, vulnerable to Shor's quantum algorithm, which solves both factoring and discrete logarithms in polynomial time on a sufficiently large quantum computer (Chapter~35).
\end{trapbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Later chapters build directly on both halves of this balance. Chapter~21 moves the discrete logarithm problem onto elliptic curves, where the absence of integer-style factor bases keeps generic $\sqrt{q}$ attacks best known and shrinks keys from $3072$-bit primes to $256$-bit curve points. Chapter~22 turns ElGamal's per-message exponent into digital signatures (which a signer cannot later disown) via DSA and Schnorr. Chapter~33 extends multiplicative homomorphism to fully homomorphic schemes supporting arbitrary circuits on ciphertexts, and Chapter~35 builds post-quantum encryption over high-dimensional geometric grids of points called \emph{lattices} to resist Shor's algorithm.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch20:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Hand-cranking discrete logs]
\label{ch20:exc-table}
Work in $\Zns{13}$ with $g = 2$. (a) Verify $2$ is a primitive root by listing all powers
$2^0,\dots,2^{11} \bmod 13$. (b) Read off $\dlog_2 6$, $\dlog_2 7$, and $\dlog_2 11$ from your table.
(c) Confirm the identity $\dlog_2(6\cdot 7) \equiv \dlog_2 6 + \dlog_2 7 \pmod{12}$, and explain in
one line why this additivity must hold.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Baby-step giant-step]
\label{ch20:exc-bsgs}
Let $p = 41$, $g = 6$ (a primitive root mod $41$). Use baby-step giant-step with $m = \ceil{\sqrt{40}}
= 7$ to compute $\dlog_6 29$. Show the baby-step table, the giant-step value $g^{-m} \bmod 41$, and
the iteration that produces the hit. Verify your answer by direct exponentiation.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Pollard rho extraction]
\label{ch20:exc-rho}
In a group of prime order $q = 101$ with generator $g$, a rho walk reports a collision between
$x_i = g^{12} h^{7}$ and $x_j = g^{83} h^{4}$, where $h = g^{x}$ is the unknown target. Solve the
linear congruence for $x \bmod 101$. (You will need $3^{-1} \bmod 101$; find it.) Why was it
essential that the order be prime for this step to go through cleanly?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[ElGamal round trip]
\label{ch20:exc-elgamal}
Use $\Zns{23}$, $g = 5$, secret key $a = 9$. (a) Compute Alice's public key $A$. (b) Encrypt
$M = 15$ using one-time randomness $y = 4$, giving $(c_1, c_2)$. (c) Decrypt your ciphertext as Alice
and confirm you recover $15$. Show every modular reduction.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Homomorphic combination]
\label{ch20:exc-homo}
Continuing in $\Zns{23}$ with $A$ from the previous exercise: encrypt $M_1 = 3$ with $y_1 = 2$ and
$M_2 = 4$ with $y_2 = 5$. Multiply the two ciphertexts componentwise, decrypt the product ciphertext,
and verify it yields $M_1 M_2 = 12 \bmod 23$. Then state what randomness the product ciphertext
uses, and confirm it is $y_1 + y_2 \bmod 22$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Nonce reuse]
\label{ch20:exc-nonce}
Bob encrypts two messages $M, M' \in \Zns{p}$ (with $p > 1000$) to Alice under the same public key and accidentally reuses the same $y$,
producing $(c_1, c_2)$ and $(c_1, c_2')$. (a) How can an eavesdropper detect the reuse from the
ciphertexts alone? (b) Show that the eavesdropper learns the group quotient $M \cdot (M')^{-1} \bmod p$ without the secret key. (c) If a
known header tells her $M = 1000$ and she computes $M \cdot (M')^{-1} \equiv 8 \pmod p$, what is $M'$? (d) Contrast with DSA,
where reusing the per-signature nonce leaks something far worse -- name what.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The DDH leak in the full group]
\label{ch20:exc-legendre}
Let $p$ be an odd prime and $g$ a primitive root, so $g$ is a quadratic non-residue. (a) Show that
$g^{x}$ is a quadratic residue if and only if $x$ is even. (b) Given $g^a$ and $g^b$, explain how to
predict whether $g^{ab}$ is a quadratic residue, and use this to build a DDH distinguisher in
the full group $\Zns{p}$ with distinguishing advantage $\Adv^{\mathrm{ddh}} = 1/2$ (guessing a 50/50 real-versus-random challenge with probability $3/4 = 1/2 + 1/4$). (c) Explain why restricting to the order-$q$ subgroup of quadratic residues
(with $p = 2q+1$) shuts this distinguisher down.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Pohlig--Hellman, why prime order matters]
\label{ch20:exc-pohlig}
Suppose a group has \emph{smooth} order $n = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 = 210$ (meaning all of its prime factors are small). Sketch how
Pohlig--Hellman reduces one discrete log in this group to four small discrete logs (mod $2,3,5,7$),
each solvable by inspection, recombined by the Chinese Remainder Theorem. Estimate the total work and
compare it to $\sqrt{210}$. What does this say about ever using a group whose order has only small
prime factors?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Malleability into a CCA break]
\label{ch20:exc-cca}
You are given an ElGamal decryption oracle (it decrypts anything except the exact challenge
ciphertext $(c_1, c_2)$) and the challenge ciphertext itself, encrypting unknown $M$. Design a single
oracle query that recovers $M$, using the multiplicative homomorphism. Why does encrypt-then-MAC over
the ciphertext defeat your attack?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Sizing a real parameter set]
\label{ch20:exc-sizing}
You want $128$-bit security for ElGamal over $\Zns{p}$ with a safe prime $p = 2q + 1$. (a) Using the
generic $\sqrt{q}$ bound, what is the minimum bit-length of $q$? (b) Given that index calculus on $p$
costs roughly $L_p[1/3, c]$, mainstream guidance puts $p$ at about $3072$ bits for this level -- explain
why $p$ must be \emph{so much} larger than $q$. (c) An elliptic curve achieves the same $128$-bit
security with a $256$-bit field. In one or two sentences, what structural difference lets the curve
get away with the smaller parameter?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Prove the homomorphism]
\label{ch20:exc-prove-homo}
Prove from the definitions that ElGamal is multiplicatively homomorphic: if $(c_1, c_2)$ encrypts $M$
with randomness $y$ and $(c_1', c_2')$ encrypts $M'$ with randomness $y'$, then
$(c_1 c_1',\, c_2 c_2')$ is a valid encryption of $M M'$, and identify its randomness modulo $q$. Then prove the
exponential ElGamal claim: if instead you encrypt $g^{M}$ and $g^{M'}$, the product ciphertext
decrypts to $g^{M + M'}$, so reading off $M + M'$ requires one discrete log.
\end{exercise}
