\chapter*{Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Preface}

There is a particular kind of frustration that brings people to cryptography. You read that Transport Layer Security (TLS), the protocol behind \texttt{https}, ``encrypts your connection,'' that Signal offers ``end-to-end encryption'' (meaning only the sender and recipient can read a message, not even the server carrying it), and that Wi-Fi Protected Access 3 (WPA3) guards your wireless password. Underneath all of it sits mathematics you were told to trust but never got to see. The padlock in the browser is a promise, and this book is about the mathematics that backs it.

I wrote it because the books that taught me cryptography each covered only part of the subject. Some gave me clean theorems and security proofs but never showed bytes on a network wire. Some gave me practical recipes -- use AES-GCM (the Advanced Encryption Standard in Galois/Counter Mode, a standard cipher that both scrambles messages and detects tampering) and never ``roll your own crypto'' (write custom cryptographic code for real systems) -- without explaining \emph{why} AES-GCM and not something else, or what custom code breaks. And some gave me clear intuition with hand-waving right where I wanted rigor. The goal here is to put all three on the same page: the math, the intuition, and the engineering practice.

\section*{What this book is, and who it is for}

You are smart, motivated, and willing to do arithmetic by hand when it teaches you something. You do not need to know number theory (the arithmetic of whole numbers, remainders, and primes) or abstract algebra (the study of arithmetic rules on general sets) ahead of time -- we build both from the ground up. You should be comfortable with high-school algebra, with the idea of a function, and with the patience to follow a small calculation to its end. If you have written a few hundred lines of code in any language, you will get more out of the toy implementations, though coding experience is not required.

By the end you should be able to start work as an entry-level cryptographer, or as a security engineer who understands the cryptographic \emph{primitives} -- basic building blocks such as ciphers, hash functions, and digital signatures. Concretely, that means you can:

\begin{itemize}
  \item read a \emph{protocol} specification -- a step-by-step rulebook for how computers exchange messages securely, such as a TLS Request for Comments (RFC, an official internet standard document), the Signal documentation, or the WPA3 standard -- and follow the cryptographic reasoning;
  \item look at a scheme and state its \emph{threat model} -- who the attacker is, what they can observe, what they can alter, and what ``secure'' means against them;
  \item work the math by hand on small numbers: raising numbers to powers using remainder arithmetic, building a tiny Rivest--Shamir--Adleman (RSA) public-key pair, adding points on a curve over a finite number system, and estimating how soon random outputs collide;
  \item recognize the classic failure modes -- reusing a \emph{nonce} (a value meant to be used only once), leaking secrets through error messages or execution time, or encrypting data without checking whether an attacker modified it -- and explain how each attack works rather than only naming it;
  \item choose the right tool -- \emph{authenticated encryption} (which both hides a message and detects tampering) rather than a raw \emph{block cipher} (which only scrambles fixed-size chunks of bits), and a \emph{key-derivation function} (which stretches a shared secret or password into strong keys) rather than a bare \emph{hash function} (which compresses data into a fixed-length fingerprint) -- and say why.
\end{itemize}

That last skill matters most. Memorizing algorithms does not make someone a cryptographer; reasoning about what an adversary can do, and proving -- or arguing carefully -- that the adversary cannot achieve their goal, does.

\section*{How to read this book}

Each scheme is introduced in the same order: a story, a small worked example, the precise math, and a security argument.

First comes the \emph{story}. Alice wants to send Bob a message; Eve is listening on the wire, and sometimes Mallory is actively tampering with it. We start there because every cryptographic protocol is a contest between parties with conflicting goals, and the algorithms are the moves in that contest. Naming the players keeps you precise about who knows what.

\begin{marginnotebox}
The cast you will meet over and over: \textbf{Alice} and \textbf{Bob} want to communicate. \textbf{Eve} is a passive eavesdropper -- she reads traffic on the channel without changing it. \textbf{Mallory} is an active, in-the-middle attacker -- she can drop, replay, reorder, and forge messages. \textbf{Trent} is a trusted third party, such as a \emph{certificate authority} (an organization that vouches for who owns a public key). When a scheme protects against Eve while failing against Mallory, that gap is usually where practical attacks happen.
\end{marginnotebox}

Then come \emph{tiny numbers}. Before any general formula, we trace the idea through an example small enough to check on paper: a calculation like $7^{13} \bmod 23$, where $a \bmod n$ (read ``$a$ modulo $n$'') means the remainder left when $a$ is divided by $n$; RSA with primes $p = 61$ and $q = 53$; a four-bit toy block cipher you can encrypt by hand; or an \emph{elliptic curve} (points $(x, y)$ satisfying a cubic equation $y^2 = x^3 + ax + b$ for fixed constants $a$ and $b$) over $\F_{11}$, the eleven-element number system $\{0, 1, \dots, 10\}$ where arithmetic wraps around modulo $11$. Working through tiny examples by hand makes the general formulas concrete.

Then come the \emph{precise math} and a \emph{security argument}. We state what the scheme is, what hardness assumption it rests on -- that splitting a large integer into its prime factors is hard, that recovering a hidden exponent $x$ from $g^x \bmod n$ for a known base $g$ and modulus $n$ (the \emph{discrete logarithm} problem) is hard, or that a hash function behaves like an unpredictable random mapping -- and we argue why breaking the scheme would break that assumption. Some of these arguments are full mathematical \emph{reductions} (proofs showing how any fast attack on the scheme could be turned into a fast solver for the underlying hard problem); others are careful informal sketches. Either way, you should finish each section able to explain why we believe a scheme is secure without appealing to authority.

You do not have to read front to back, though the early chapters are foundations. Part~I builds the vocabulary every later chapter uses: \emph{modular arithmetic} (arithmetic with remainders) and \emph{groups} (sets equipped with a single reversible operation obeying clean algebraic laws). If you already know both well, skim Part~I and start at the \emph{symmetric ciphers} (encryption where sender and receiver share the same secret key); if you do not, resist the urge to skip -- you will only end up back there confused.

\section*{The security mindset}

Before any specific scheme, you need a way of thinking that runs against ordinary engineering instincts. An ordinary engineer asks ``does it work?'' -- does the system do the right thing when used the right way. A cryptographer asks the adversary's question: ``how does it fail when someone is \emph{trying} to make it fail?'' You design against the attacker, not the typical user.

This flips the burden of proof. In normal software, a feature is correct until a bug is found. In cryptography, a scheme is \emph{broken} until there is a convincing argument that it is not -- and ``I couldn't break it'' is not such an argument, because the person who breaks it may be cleverer, better funded, or have more time than you. That is why the field leans so heavily on definitions and proofs. A security definition is a promise made precise: it says which attacker, with which powers, fails to achieve which goal. Without that precision, ``secure'' is a marketing word.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Think of security as a game. We hand the attacker a generous set of powers: she may choose readable messages (\emph{plaintexts}) to be encrypted, inspect the resulting scrambled outputs (\emph{ciphertexts}), and sometimes even request decryptions of ciphertexts she chooses. Then we name one goal she must fail to achieve, such as telling which of two plaintexts was encrypted or forging a valid authentication \emph{tag} (a short cryptographic check value proving a message was not forged or altered). The scheme is secure if, \emph{even with all those powers}, her chance of winning beats blind guessing by at most a \emph{negligible} amount -- a gap so small that no realistic amount of computation can exploit it.
\end{intuitionbox}

Two principles follow from this mindset and recur on nearly every page.

\subsection*{Kerckhoffs's principle: the secret is the key, never the design}

In 1883 the Dutch linguist Auguste Kerckhoffs laid down a rule for military ciphers that the entire modern field has adopted: a cryptographic system should remain secure even if everything about it is public knowledge \emph{except the key}. The algorithm, the protocol, the source code, the hardware layout -- assume the enemy has all of it. The only thing you keep secret is a short string of bits, the key, which is cheap to generate fresh, easy to replace, and small enough to guard carefully.

\begin{historybox}
Claude Shannon, founding the mathematical theory of secrecy in 1949, restated Kerckhoffs's 1883 rule as a blunt slogan: ``the enemy knows the system.'' Design as if your adversary has read your code, bought your hardware, and reverse-engineered your firmware -- because eventually someone will. Historically, governments and vendors resisted this, relying on secret designs known as ``security through obscurity.'' That stance has a long record of failure. The A5/1 cipher protecting Global System for Mobile Communications (GSM) phone calls was kept secret for years, and once its design leaked and faced open analysis, it fell quickly (Chapter~29). Open standards like AES earn trust only after thousands of independent \emph{cryptanalysts} (specialists who test ciphers by trying to break them) attack them in public; today, a major challenge is defending open algorithms against hardware \emph{side channels} -- physical leaks from a running device, such as execution time and power draw.
\end{historybox}

\begin{whybox}
Why insist on this? Because secrets that are hard to change will eventually leak and then cannot be repaired. An algorithm baked into a billion devices cannot be swapped overnight; a leaked key can be replaced in seconds. Kerckhoffs's principle puts all the fragility into the one component that is cheap to replace. It also brings a decisive practical benefit: a public algorithm can be tested by the world's cryptanalysts for years before you trust it. Every primitive in this book recommended for real systems -- AES, the Secure Hash Algorithm families (SHA-2 and SHA-3), the elliptic-curve system Curve25519 -- earned trust by surviving open, published attack. A secret design earns nothing, because no independent outsider could test it.
\end{whybox}

\begin{trapbox}
Suppose a vendor boasts, ``We use a proprietary, undisclosed encryption algorithm for extra security.'' Read that marketing claim for what it says: no qualified outsider has checked the math. Secrecy of the design is not a second layer of protection on top of a strong cipher; historically it is a poor substitute for one. Extra obscurity is harmless as a thin outer wrapper to slow down a casual attacker, provided your security never rests on it keeping secret.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection*{Don't roll your own crypto -- and why this book seems to do exactly that}

The second principle sounds like it contradicts this book: do not write your own cryptographic code for production use. We will write plenty of cryptographic code here. The distinction lies in production use versus study. Implementing a toy version of a scheme is what makes you competent to \emph{use} a real library safely -- to pick the right \emph{mode of operation} (the rule for applying a block cipher across a multi-block message), supply a fresh nonce, and verify an authentication tag before trusting a plaintext. Yet the gap between textbook-correct code and deployment-safe code is wide. \begin{attackbox}
A textbook RSA decryption can compute the right answer every time and still leak your secret decryption key (the \emph{private key}), because the time it takes varies with the secret exponent's bits. A textbook AES implementation in \emph{cipher block chaining} (CBC) mode -- which links each encrypted block to the previous one and fills out a short final block with extra \emph{padding} bytes -- can also compute the right answer and still surrender the plaintext to a \emph{padding oracle}. If a server reveals even one bit of error feedback -- whether decrypted bytes ended with valid padding -- an attacker can submit modified ciphertexts and peel off the message one byte at a time without ever finding the key. Later chapters build both attacks by hand. They are the reason production cryptography is constant-time, authenticated, and written by specialists. Once you have mounted them yourself, you are far less likely to ship them.
\end{attackbox}

So implement everything here to learn it, and trust none of it in production. Understanding schemes deeply while refusing to deploy unvetted code is the habit every \textbf{Implement it} box aims to build.

\section*{How the book is organized: from modular arithmetic to TLS, Wi-Fi, and Signal}

The book has six main parts followed by two reference appendices, each part building on the ones before it.

\textbf{Part~I -- Foundations and the Mathematics.} We start with what cryptography protects -- \emph{confidentiality} (keeping contents secret), \emph{integrity} (detecting unauthorized changes), \emph{authentication} (verifying identity), and \emph{non-repudiation} (proving origin to a third party) -- and see how classical letter-substitution ciphers fall to frequency counting. Then we build the math: modular arithmetic, primes and the \emph{greatest common divisor} (the largest integer dividing two numbers), algebraic structures (\emph{groups}, \emph{rings}, and \emph{fields}, including \emph{finite fields} where addition, multiplication, and division wrap around inside a finite set), probability and \emph{information theory} (the mathematical measurement of uncertainty and secrecy), and \emph{computational hardness} -- problems easy to state and infeasible to solve at scale, on which public-key cryptography rests.

\textbf{Part~II -- Symmetric Cryptography.} Alice and Bob already share a secret key; what can they do with it? We begin with the \emph{one-time pad} and \emph{perfect secrecy} -- a cipher provably unbreakable even with unlimited computing power, and a clear lesson in why ``unbreakable'' is not the same as practical, since its single-use key must be as long as the message. From there we build \emph{stream ciphers} (which expand a short key into a long pseudorandom bit stream), block ciphers (the Data Encryption Standard (DES), AES, and the reversible round networks inside them), and modes of operation for multi-block messages. Next come hash functions, \emph{message authentication codes} (MACs, keyed tags that detect tampering) including hash-based MACs (HMAC), authenticated encryption, and \emph{cryptographically secure pseudorandom number generators} (CSPRNGs, which turn random seed bits into unpredictable key material).

\textbf{Part~III -- Public-Key Cryptography.} Here we drop the assumption that Alice and Bob already share a key, and confront the question that launched modern cryptography: how do two strangers establish a secret over a wire that an enemy is reading? Using the hardness assumptions from Part~I, we build Diffie--Hellman key exchange, RSA, discrete-logarithm and ElGamal encryption, and elliptic-curve cryptography, followed by \emph{digital signatures} (publicly verifiable tags that only the private-key holder can produce) and key exchange with \emph{forward secrecy} (keeping past recorded sessions safe even if a long-term key leaks later).

\textbf{Part~IV -- Protocols and Applications.} We start with digital certificates and \emph{public-key infrastructure} (PKI, the system of certificate authorities that lets your browser verify who owns a public key), then study protocols you use every day: TLS behind every \texttt{https} connection, Wi-Fi security through WPA3, and Signal's \emph{double ratchet} (which rolls keys forward with each message for both forward secrecy and \emph{post-compromise recovery} after a temporary key leak). We also examine SSH, cellular network security, and \emph{virtual private networks} (VPNs, encrypted tunnels across untrusted networks). Combining secure pieces does not automatically produce a secure whole, and these chapters show why.

\textbf{Part~V -- Advanced Topics.} \emph{Zero-knowledge proofs} (proving a claim is true without revealing why), \emph{commitments} (locking in a hidden choice to reveal later) and \emph{secret sharing} (splitting a secret across several holders), \emph{homomorphic encryption} (computing directly on encrypted data without decrypting it), \emph{multiparty computation} (computing a joint result from private inputs while keeping each input secret), \emph{post-quantum cryptography} (schemes built to resist future quantum computers), and the cryptography behind blockchains.

\textbf{Part~VI -- Attacks and Safe Implementation.} Hardware side-channel and timing attacks, a catalog of recurring engineering pitfalls, and how to select and integrate vetted cryptographic libraries safely in real systems.

\textbf{Part~VII -- Mathematical Background.} Two self-contained appendices collecting the number theory, algebra, and probability results used across the book for quick reference.

\begin{connectionbox}
This is one of three companion ground-up readers. Where we need the full version of a probabilistic argument -- the birthday bound behind \emph{hash collisions} (two distinct inputs producing the same hash), or the error bound of a randomized \emph{primality test} (an algorithm checking whether a large integer is prime) -- the probability book carries the proof. Where we need the cost of an algorithm -- how many multiplications modular exponentiation takes, or why fast multiplication matters for big keys -- the algorithms book develops it. Cryptography sits at the meeting point of ``what is hard to compute'' and ``what happens by chance,'' so you will see those pointers often.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{focusbox}
One theme runs through the whole book: the shift from \emph{information-theoretic} security to \emph{computational} security. The one-time pad achieves the first -- secure even against infinite computing power, yet hobbled by needing a key as long as the message. Nearly everything else settles for the second, assuming an adversary with bounded computing resources in exchange for schemes practical enough to run the internet. Almost all of modern cryptography lives in that second world, and much of its subtlety comes from stating ``bounded resources'' precisely.
\end{focusbox}

\section*{One worked example, so you know what ``by hand'' means}

To set expectations, here is the kind of small-number calculation you will do throughout, using an operation that drives nearly every public-key scheme: modular exponentiation, computed efficiently.

Suppose we want $7^{13} \bmod 23$. The naive route -- multiply $7$ by itself twelve times to get $7^{13}$, then divide by $23$ -- works on tiny inputs yet fails completely at scale; with the thousand-bit exponents of real cryptography it would never finish. Instead we use \emph{square-and-multiply}. Write the exponent $13$ in base~$2$ (binary):
\[
  13 = 1101_2 = 1 \cdot 2^3 + 1 \cdot 2^2 + 0 \cdot 2^1 + 1 \cdot 2^0 = 8 + 4 + 1.
\]
By the law of exponents $a^{x+y} = a^x \cdot a^y$, this splits $7^{13}$ into three powers of two:
\[
  7^{13} = 7^{8 + 4 + 1} = 7^{8} \cdot 7^{4} \cdot 7^{1}.
\]
We reach $7^2, 7^4, 7^8$ by repeated squaring, reducing modulo $23$ at every step so the intermediate numbers stay below $23^2 = 529$. Here we write $a \equiv b \pmod{23}$ (read ``$a$ is congruent to $b$ modulo $23$'') to mean that $a$ and $b$ leave the same remainder when divided by $23$ -- equivalently, $a - b$ is a multiple of $23$:
\begin{align*}
  7^{1} &\equiv 7 \pmod{23}, \\
  7^{2} &= 49 \equiv 3 \pmod{23} && (\text{since } 49 = 2 \cdot 23 + 3), \\
  7^{4} &\equiv 3^{2} = 9 \pmod{23}, \\
  7^{8} &\equiv 9^{2} = 81 \equiv 12 \pmod{23} && (\text{since } 81 = 3 \cdot 23 + 12).
\end{align*}
Now multiply together the pieces selected by the $1$-bits of $1101_2$ (for $8$, $4$, and $1$), again reducing modulo $23$ after each product:
\begin{align*}
  7^{13} &= 7^{8} \cdot 7^{4} \cdot 7^{1} \\
  &\equiv (12 \cdot 9) \cdot 7 \pmod{23} \\
  &\equiv 16 \cdot 7 \pmod{23} && (\text{since } 108 = 4 \cdot 23 + 16) \\
  &\equiv 20 \pmod{23} && (\text{since } 112 = 4 \cdot 23 + 20).
\end{align*}

Three squarings and two multiplications (five modular products in all) replace twelve sequential multiplications. Because each extra bit in the exponent adds one squaring and at most one multiplication, a $2048$-bit modular exponentiation finishes in roughly three thousand modular multiplications -- a few milliseconds on modern hardware.

\needspace{10\baselineskip}
\begin{implementbox}
The same algorithm in Python, written to be read rather than deployed. Python's built-in three-argument \texttt{pow(base, exp, mod)} is fast and production-grade; this loop exists only to expose the bit-by-bit mechanics.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
def modexp(base, exp, mod):
    # Square-and-multiply. LEARNING ONLY:
    # not constant-time -- leaks the exponent's bits via timing.
    result = 1
    base = base % mod
    while exp > 0:
        if exp & 1:                  # low bit is 1 -> fold in this power
            result = (result * base) % mod
        base = (base * base) % mod    # square for the next bit
        exp >>= 1                     # move to the next bit of the exponent
    return result

print(modexp(7, 13, 23))   # -> 20
\end{lstlisting}

Here \texttt{exp \& 1} tests whether the lowest binary bit of \texttt{exp} is $1$, and \texttt{exp >>= 1} shifts the bits right by one place (dividing \texttt{exp} by $2$ and discarding the remainder). Take the warning comment seriously: the branch \texttt{if exp \& 1} performs an extra multiplication only when the current exponent bit is $1$, so the total running time varies with the secret exponent -- a timing leak that lets an attacker recover private key bits. Production libraries work hard to eliminate secret-dependent branches.
\end{implementbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Square-and-multiply runs in time proportional to the \emph{number of bits} $b \approx \log_2(\text{exp})$ in the exponent rather than the exponent's magnitude $\approx 2^b$ -- a logarithmic speedup, as plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:preface-scaling}. The companion algorithms reader develops this cost model in detail.
\end{connectionbox}

That single operation -- raise to a power, reduce modulo $n$ -- is nearly the entire calculation in RSA. To show that ``by hand'' means by hand, here is a complete RSA encryption and decryption on numbers small enough to check on paper, using the primes $p = 61$ and $q = 53$ that recur in later chapters. Multiply them to form the public modulus $n$, and compute \emph{Euler's totient} $\varphi(n)$:
\[
  n = pq = 61 \cdot 53 = 3233, \qquad
  \varphi(n) = (p - 1)(q - 1) = 60 \cdot 52 = 3120,
\]
where $\varphi(n)$ counts the positive integers from $1$ to $n$ that are \emph{coprime} to $n$ -- sharing no common factor larger than $1$ with $n$. Pick a public exponent $e = 17$, which is coprime to $3120$. The private exponent $d$ is the \emph{modular inverse} of $e$ modulo $\varphi(n)$, written $d \equiv e^{-1} \pmod{3120}$: the integer with $e\,d \equiv 1 \pmod{3120}$. The extended Euclidean algorithm (a fast remainder-chaining procedure developed in Part~I) finds $d = 2753$. You can verify the inverse property directly by multiplying:
\[
  17 \cdot 2753 = 46801 = 15 \cdot 3120 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{3120}.
\]
Bob publishes the pair $(n, e) = (3233, 17)$ as his \emph{public key} so anyone can encrypt messages to him, and keeps $d = 2753$ secret as his \emph{private key} (along with $p$, $q$, and $\varphi(n)$, since knowing any of those lets an attacker compute $d$).

Now encrypt the message $m = 65$ (say, the ASCII byte for \texttt{A}). The ciphertext is
\[
  c = m^{e} \bmod 3233 = 65^{17} \bmod 3233.
\]
Write $17 = 10001_2 = 16 + 1$ and square four times modulo $3233$:
\begin{align*}
  65^{1} &\equiv 65 \pmod{3233}, \\
  65^{2} &= 4225 \equiv 992 \pmod{3233} && (4225 = 1 \cdot 3233 + 992), \\
  65^{4} &\equiv 992^{2} = 984064 \equiv 1232 \pmod{3233} && (984064 = 304 \cdot 3233 + 1232), \\
  65^{8} &\equiv 1232^{2} = 1517824 \equiv 1547 \pmod{3233} && (1517824 = 469 \cdot 3233 + 1547), \\
  65^{16} &\equiv 1547^{2} = 2393209 \equiv 789 \pmod{3233} && (2393209 = 740 \cdot 3233 + 789).
\end{align*}
The binary representation $10001_2$ selects $65^{16}$ and $65^1$, giving
\[
  c \equiv 65^{16} \cdot 65^{1} \equiv 789 \cdot 65 = 51285 = 15 \cdot 3233 + 2790 \equiv 2790 \pmod{3233}.
\]
To decrypt, Bob raises the ciphertext $c = 2790$ to his private exponent $d = 2753$:
\[
  m = c^{d} \bmod 3233 = 2790^{2753} \bmod 3233 = 65,
\]
computed by the same square-and-multiply method across the twelve bits of $2753$, recovering the original byte. Figure~\ref{fig:preface-rsa} summarizes the full round trip: anyone with the public key $(n, e)$ can compute $c$, while inverting the map requires the modular inverse $d \equiv e^{-1} \pmod{\varphi(n)}$.

Why decryption undoes encryption -- why $(m^{e})^{d} \equiv m \pmod{n}$ -- follows from a classic result in modular arithmetic called \emph{Euler's theorem}, proved in Part~I and applied in Chapter~19. Both directions use nothing beyond modular exponentiation.

\needspace{7\baselineskip}
\begin{trapbox}
The numbers above are small enough to factor by \emph{trial division} (testing prime divisors $2, 3, 5, \dots$ one by one) in well under a second: anyone who sees $n = 3233$ can recover $p = 61$ and $q = 53$, compute $\varphi(n) = 3120$, solve for $d = 2753$, and read every message. RSA's security rests on choosing $p$ and $q$ so large that $n = pq$ has \emph{six hundred or more} decimal digits ($2048$ bits or more), putting factoring out of reach. A common beginner error is to see a scheme work on toy numbers and assume it is secure. Toy numbers check correctness, never security. Keep the two questions -- ``does it compute the right answer?'' and ``can an adversary invert it?'' -- separate in your head.
\end{trapbox}

\section*{What it takes to be an entry-level cryptographer}

People imagine the job needs a doctorate and a gift for inventing new schemes. Research roles do, while most industry cryptography roles do not. The entry-level work -- well-paid and in steady demand -- is applying cryptography accurately: choosing primitives, reading specifications, reviewing designs for recurring failure modes, and integrating vetted libraries without breaking their assumptions. That work rests on a compact set of core ideas, and building that foundation is what this book is for.

\begin{applicationbox}
What this looks like on the job: a protocol engineer deciding that a new service should use TLS~1.3 with X25519 elliptic-curve key exchange and AES-256-GCM (AES with a $256$-bit key in Galois/Counter Mode), and defending each choice. A reviewer catching that a teammate reused a GCM nonce across two messages -- a one-line bug that destroys both confidentiality and integrity. An embedded developer noticing that a smartcard's signing routine takes an \texttt{if}/\texttt{else} branch depending on secret key bits and therefore leaks the key through execution time. None of these require inventing new mathematics; all of them require understanding existing mathematics well enough to spot where it is misused.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
A fair self-test for ``ready for entry-level work,'' which doubles as a map of where you are headed. By the end you should be able to, without notes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item state Kerckhoffs's principle and explain why a public algorithm is a strength rather than a weakness;
  \item compute a small RSA encryption and decryption by hand and explain why decryption inverts encryption;
  \item explain the difference between confidentiality and integrity, and why unauthenticated encryption neither prevents tampering nor stays confidential against an active attacker who can probe decryption errors;
  \item describe a Diffie--Hellman key exchange and state what an eavesdropper does and does not learn from the wire;
  \item name three real-world attacks (a padding oracle, a nonce reuse, a timing leak) and sketch how each one works;
  \item read ``AES-256-GCM with a random 96-bit nonce'' and explain what every piece means and why each piece is there;
  \item explain why you should use a vetted constant-time library and never the toy code in this book.
\end{itemize}

If those feel far off right now, that is expected; the chapters ahead cover that ground step by step.
\end{recoverybox}

A last word on how to use the book well. \emph{Do the arithmetic.} When a worked example traces $7^{13} \bmod 23$, get a pen and follow it; when an exercise asks you to break a weakened scheme, break it. Cryptography punishes passive reading -- the instinct that spots a nonce-reuse bug in someone else's protocol comes from having reused a nonce yourself on small numbers and watched the secret fall out. Run the toy code. Trace the attacks. Work through the security proofs step by step until they read like plain common sense.

Every chapter ends with exercises -- some to compute, some to break, some to prove. Do them; much of the learning happens there. Compute problems build fluency, break problems build the adversarial reflex, and proof problems build the precision that separates a guarantee from a hope. The preface ends with eight short exercises using only the tools introduced above.

\section*{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Square-and-multiply by hand]
Compute $5^{20} \bmod 23$ using square-and-multiply, reducing modulo $23$ at every step. Write $20$ in binary first, list the squarings $5^{1}, 5^{2}, 5^{4}, 5^{8}, 5^{16}$ each reduced modulo $23$, then multiply together only the powers selected by the $1$-bits of $20$. How many squarings and how many multiplications did you use, and how does that compare to the nineteen multiplications of the naive method?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Finish the worked RSA decryption]
Using the toy key $n = 3233$, $d = 2753$ from this preface, verify that the ciphertext $c = 2790$ decrypts to $m = 65$. Use square-and-multiply on $2790^{2753} \bmod 3233$: write $2753$ in binary, build the squarings $2790^{2^{k}} \bmod 3233$ for $k = 0, 1, \dots, 11$, and combine the selected powers. (If you have a computer handy, confirm afterward with \texttt{pow(2790, 2753, 3233)} -- after doing the first several squarings by hand so you own the mechanics.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Break the toy key]
Given only the public key $(n, e) = (3233, 17)$, play the attacker: factor $n$ by trial division, recover $p$ and $q$, compute $\varphi(n)$, and reconstruct the private exponent $d$ satisfying $17 d \equiv 1 \pmod{\varphi(n)}$. (Hint: without the extended Euclidean algorithm from Part~I, note that $17 d = 3120 k + 1$ for some integer $k \in \{1, \dots, 16\}$; find the small $k$ that makes $3120 k + 1$ divisible by $17$, then divide by $17$.) Confirm you obtain $d = 2753$, and explain in one sentence why this attack fails when $n$ has six hundred decimal digits rather than four.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Scaling up modular exponentiation]
In computing $7^{13} \bmod 23$, we wrote $13 = 1101_2$ and performed $3$ squarings and $2$ multiplications. Suppose an exponent has $2048$ bits, with roughly half of its bits set to $1$. Approximately how many squarings and multiplications does square-and-multiply require? Contrast this with the naive method of multiplying the base by itself roughly $2^{2048}$ times.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Eavesdroppers versus active adversaries]
Alice sends an unencrypted email to Bob over open coffee-shop Wi-Fi. Eve captures the raw radio packets. Mallory runs a rogue Wi-Fi access point that intercepts, alters, and drops packets. For each goal below, state whether Eve alone can achieve it, only Mallory can achieve it, or both can: (a) reading the body of Alice's email, (b) changing the bank routing number inside the email, (c) preventing the email from reaching Bob altogether.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Kerckhoffs in the wild]
Find a real product or protocol whose marketing claims security comes partly from a secret or proprietary algorithm. State, in the language of this preface, which assumption its security rests on, and describe a single realistic event (a leak, reverse-engineering, or an insider disclosure) that collapses it. Contrast that cost with what happens when a single secret \emph{key} leaks in a Kerckhoffs-compliant design.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Confidentiality is not integrity]
The bitwise exclusive-OR operation $\xor$ (read ``XOR'') compares two equal-length bit strings position by position, outputting $0$ where two bits match ($0 \xor 0 = 0$, $1 \xor 1 = 0$) and $1$ where they differ ($0 \xor 1 = 1 \xor 0 = 1$). Because XORing with $0$ leaves any bit unchanged and XORing twice with the same key cancels out, $(m \xor k) \xor k = m$. Suppose Alice encrypts a one-byte vote $m$ as $c = m \xor k$ using a secret random one-byte key $k$ shared with Bob, where $m$ is either hexadecimal \texttt{0x00} ($00000000_2$, ``no'') or \texttt{0xFF} ($11111111_2$, ``yes''). Eve cannot tell from $c$ alone which vote Alice sent. Mallory, who controls the wire, wants to reverse Alice's vote -- turning ``no'' into ``yes'' and ``yes'' into ``no'' -- without knowing $k$ or which vote Alice sent. Show what byte Mallory XORs into $c$ on the wire so that Bob decrypts the opposite vote with certainty.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A timing leak you can see]
Look again at the \texttt{modexp} function in this preface, and suppose the exponent \texttt{exp} is a secret key. Argue informally why two $2048$-bit exponents with very different numbers of $1$-bits cause \texttt{modexp} to run for measurably different lengths of time, and sketch what an attacker who can time many decryptions learns about the secret exponent.
\end{exercise}

Cryptography makes a bold mathematical claim: two strangers can communicate privately across a wire the whole world is listening to, even against an adversary who knows every detail of the method and has read every word of this book. Chapter~1 sets the ground rules: what the four security goals are, which attack models govern real systems, and what ``secure'' is allowed to mean.
