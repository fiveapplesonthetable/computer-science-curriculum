\chapter{Orientation Estimation and Sensor Fusion}

In the last two chapters we met the three little sensors that live inside an inertial
measurement unit (IMU) -- the accelerometer that feels gravity and motion, the
gyroscope that feels rotation, the magnetometer that feels the Earth's field -- and we
learned to calibrate the raw numbers into trustworthy physical units. But raw numbers are
not what we ultimately want. When you tilt your wrist to bend a note, or roll your
forearm to sweep a filter, the thing that matters is not ``what is the acceleration
along the chip's $x$-axis right now'' -- it is \emph{which way is the wrist pointing}.
That single question -- the device's \emph{orientation} in space, also called its
\emph{attitude} or \emph{pose} -- is the subject of this chapter.

Orientation is a deceptively rich quantity. It is not three numbers you can simply add
up; it lives on a curved space (the set of rotations), and the naive ways of writing
it down hide nasty traps. Worse, none of our three sensors measures orientation
directly. The gyro measures the \emph{rate of change} of orientation, so to get
orientation you must integrate -- and integration quietly accumulates error until your
estimate slowly walks away from the truth. The accelerometer and magnetometer, by
contrast, give you \emph{absolute} references (down, and roughly north) but they are
noisy and, for the accelerometer, corrupted by every real motion. Neither sensor alone
is trustworthy. The art of combining them so that each covers the other's weakness is
called \emph{sensor fusion}, and it brings together
linear algebra and probability (Chapter~42): orientation as geometry,
fusion as optimal estimation under noise.

We will build the picture from the ground up. First, how do you even \emph{represent}
a rotation -- Euler angles and their gimbal-lock trap, rotation matrices, and the
quaternion that practitioners actually use? Then, why does the gyro drift and why are
the accelerometer and magnetometer ``absolute but noisy''? Then the central trick: the
\emph{complementary filter}, which is just a high-pass on the gyro plus a low-pass on
the accelerometer, fused in the frequency domain you already understand. We will derive
the \emph{Kalman filter} intuitively as the optimal version of that same idea, and meet
the \emph{Madgwick} and \emph{Mahony} filters that run on real microcontrollers. We
close, as always, with the wrist tracker: how to get a stable wrist orientation, and a
clear accounting of what orientation can and cannot tell you about the hand.

\section{What orientation is, geometrically}
\label{ch36:what-orientation-is}

Fix two coordinate frames. One is glued to the world: call it the \emph{navigation
frame} or \emph{Earth frame}, with axes pointing, say, North, East, and Down (the NED
convention). The other is glued to the sensor chip and tumbles with it: the \emph{body
frame}, with axes along the chip's own $x$, $y$, $z$. \emph{Orientation} is the answer
to a single question: how is the body frame rotated relative to the world frame? Every
way of writing down orientation is just a different bookkeeping for that one rotation.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Hold your phone flat on the table, screen up. Body frame and world frame agree --
zero rotation. Now tip the phone up to read it: you have rotated the body frame about
a horizontal axis. Spin in your chair while holding it: another rotation, about
vertical. Orientation is the \emph{total} rotation that carries the world axes onto the
body axes. The point of this chapter is that ``a rotation'' is a single geometric
object, and Euler angles, matrices, and quaternions are three languages for naming the
same object.
\end{intuitionbox}

An essential fact, easy to forget, is that rotations do \emph{not commute}. Tip then
spin lands you somewhere different from spin then tip. Try it with the phone: the final
orientations genuinely differ. This non-commutativity is the deep reason rotations are
subtle, and it is exactly what gimbal lock and quaternion algebra are wrestling with.

\subsection{Euler angles: three turns in a row}
\label{ch36:euler}

The most human way to describe an orientation is as a sequence of three simple turns
about axes: \emph{roll} $\phi$ about the forward $x$-axis, \emph{pitch} $\theta$ about
the side $y$-axis, and \emph{yaw} $\psi$ about the down $z$-axis. Aircraft people have
used these names for a century: roll dips a wingtip, pitch raises the nose, yaw swings
the nose left or right. Any orientation can be reached by some $(\phi,\theta,\psi)$.

Euler angles are wonderful for talking to humans -- ``pitch up thirty degrees'' is
instantly clear -- and they are how we will report the final answer. But three numbers
hide three problems. First, the \emph{order} matters (roll-pitch-yaw $\neq$
yaw-pitch-roll), so you must commit to a convention. Second, the angles \emph{wrap}:
$359^\circ$ and $-1^\circ$ are the same heading, so naive averaging or differencing
goes wrong near the wrap. Third, and most dangerous, is gimbal lock.

\subsection{The gimbal-lock trap}
\label{ch36:gimbal}

\begin{trapbox}
When pitch reaches $\pm 90^\circ$, the roll axis and the yaw axis line up and become
the \emph{same} physical axis. You have lost a degree of freedom: two of your three
numbers now do the same thing, and the third has become undefined. This is
\emph{gimbal lock}. Near it, tiny real motions demand huge, fast changes in the Euler
angles, so any estimator that stores orientation as Euler angles becomes numerically
violent there.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Picture a camera on a mechanical gimbal with three nested rings: outer ring yaws,
middle ring pitches, inner ring rolls. Pitch the middle ring up to $90^\circ$ so the
camera stares at the sky. Now the inner (roll) ring and the outer (yaw) ring spin about
the \emph{same} vertical line. Spinning either one just rotates the picture; you can no
longer tip the camera sideways without moving the locked middle ring. A whole direction
of motion has vanished from your control. The Apollo guidance computer had a real
gimbal-locked inertial platform; astronauts were warned to avoid certain attitudes, and
mission control joked grimly about ``going to gimbal lock.''
\end{intuitionbox}

Gimbal lock is not a bug you can patch out of Euler angles; it is a topological fact.
There is no way to cover the full space of 3-D rotations with three smooth angle
coordinates without a singularity somewhere -- the same reason every flat map of the
globe distorts the poles. The fix is to stop forcing orientation into three numbers.
We move to representations that are \emph{global} and \emph{smooth}: rotation matrices
(nine numbers, six constraints) and quaternions (four numbers, one constraint).

\subsection{Rotation matrices}
\label{ch36:rotmat}

A rotation in three dimensions is a linear map that preserves lengths and angles and
keeps the handedness of space. In linear algebra (Chapter~42), it is an
orthogonal matrix with determinant $+1$ -- an element of the group $SO(3)$. Write
$\mat{R}$ for the matrix that takes a vector's coordinates in the body frame and gives
its coordinates in the world frame:
\[
  \vec{v}_{\text{world}} = \mat{R}\,\vec{v}_{\text{body}}, \qquad
  \mat{R}^\top \mat{R} = \mat{I}, \qquad \det \mat{R} = +1 .
\]
The columns of $\mat{R}$ are the body axes expressed in world coordinates -- so $\mat R$
literally \emph{is} a picture of where the chip's axes are pointing. The single
elementary rotations are the ones you would guess; for example a rotation by $\theta$
about the $y$-axis is
\[
  \mat{R}_y(\theta) =
  \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & 0 & \sin\theta \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -\sin\theta & 0 & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix},
\]
and the full Euler orientation is a product like
$\mat{R} = \mat{R}_z(\psi)\,\mat{R}_y(\theta)\,\mat{R}_x(\phi)$. The non-commutativity
of rotations is now obvious: matrix multiplication does not commute.

\begin{whybox}
Rotation matrices never gimbal-lock and compose by plain multiplication, which makes
them excellent for \emph{computing}. Their drawback is redundancy: nine numbers for
three degrees of freedom, tied by six constraints ($\mat R^\top\mat R=\mat I$). When you
integrate a matrix forward in time with finite-precision arithmetic, rounding error
accumulates -- the columns drift out of orthonormality -- so you must periodically
\emph{re-orthonormalize}. Quaternions carry only one constraint and are cheaper to keep
clean, which is why they win in practice.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{Quaternions: the practitioner's choice}
\label{ch36:quat}

The quaternion is the representation almost every real attitude filter uses internally.
It is a four-number object $q = (q_w,\,q_x,\,q_y,\,q_z)$, often written
$q = q_w + q_x\jj_1 + q_y\jj_2 + q_z\jj_3$ with three imaginary units. The geometry
behind the algebra is clean and is the only part you must \emph{feel}:

\begin{intuitionbox}
\textbf{Euler's rotation theorem.} Any orientation, however complicated, equals a single
rotation by some angle $\alpha$ about some fixed axis $\hat{\vec n}$ (a unit vector).
A unit quaternion packs exactly that axis and angle into four numbers:
\[
  q = \Big(\cos\tfrac{\alpha}{2},\ \ \hat n_x \sin\tfrac{\alpha}{2},\ \
            \hat n_y \sin\tfrac{\alpha}{2},\ \ \hat n_z \sin\tfrac{\alpha}{2}\Big).
\]
The scalar part remembers \emph{how much} you turned; the vector part remembers
\emph{about what axis}. The half-angle is the famous quirk -- a full $360^\circ$ turn
gives $-q$ and a $720^\circ$ turn returns to the start. For our purposes you only need:
unit quaternions name rotations, and there are no singularities.
\end{intuitionbox}

Three facts let you use quaternions without drowning in algebra. (1) A \emph{unit}
quaternion, $\norm{q} = 1$, represents a rotation; you renormalize after each update to
keep it on the unit sphere -- one constraint, cheap. (2) Rotations \emph{compose} by
the quaternion product $q_2 \otimes q_1$ (do $q_1$ then $q_2$), which is just a fixed
bilinear formula. (3) To rotate a vector $\vec v$, sandwich it:
$\vec v' = q \otimes \vec v \otimes q^{-1}$. The quaternion product is non-commutative,
exactly mirroring the non-commutativity of rotations.

\begin{formalbox}
The Hamilton product of $a=(a_w,\vec a)$ and $b=(b_w,\vec b)$ (scalar part, vector part) is
\[
  a\otimes b = \big(a_w b_w - \vec a\cdot\vec b,\ \ a_w \vec b + b_w \vec a + \vec a\times\vec b\big).
\]
For a unit quaternion the inverse is the conjugate $q^{-1}=q^{*}=(q_w,-\vec q)$. The
rotation matrix equivalent to $q$ is
\[
  \mat R(q)=\begin{bmatrix}
  1-2(q_y^2+q_z^2) & 2(q_xq_y-q_wq_z) & 2(q_xq_z+q_wq_y)\\
  2(q_xq_y+q_wq_z) & 1-2(q_x^2+q_z^2) & 2(q_yq_z-q_wq_x)\\
  2(q_xq_z-q_wq_y) & 2(q_yq_z+q_wq_x) & 1-2(q_x^2+q_y^2)
  \end{bmatrix}.
\]
You convert to Euler angles only at the very end, for display, and you keep the
quaternion as the working state.
\end{formalbox}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why four dimensions? Hamilton's bridge and the vector wars}
In the 1830s, Irish mathematician William Rowan Hamilton sought to extend the two-dimensional algebra of complex numbers ($a + b\jj$, which represents rotation in a plane) to three-dimensional space. For over a decade, Hamilton struggled to construct a 3D ``triplet'' algebra $(a + b\jj_1 + c\jj_2)$ that preserved lengths under multiplication: $\|uv\| = \|u\|\|v\|$. Every attempt collapsed into contradiction: cross-terms like $\jj_1 \jj_2$ refused to reconcile in three dimensions.

On the morning of 16 October 1843, while walking along the Royal Canal in Dublin with his wife to a meeting of the Royal Irish Academy, Hamilton experienced a sudden geometric epiphany: three dimensions were not enough. A rotation in 3D required \emph{four} dimensions. In his excitement, Hamilton carved the fundamental relations into the stone of Brougham (Broom) Bridge with his penknife:
\[
  \jj_1^2 \;=\; \jj_2^2 \;=\; \jj_3^2 \;=\; \jj_1 \jj_2 \jj_3 \;=\; -1.
\]
To gain the ability to rotate 3D vectors without distortion, Hamilton sacrificed commutativity: $\jj_1 \jj_2 = -\jj_2 \jj_1 = \jj_3$.

In the late 19th century, Hamilton's disciple Peter Guthrie Tait engaged in a ferocious intellectual war with physicists Oliver Heaviside and Josiah Willard Gibbs. Heaviside mocked quaternions as ``a positive evil'' and a ``monstrosity,'' stripping quaternions apart into separate dot ($\cdot$) and cross ($\times$) products to create modern vector calculus. Vector calculus won mainstream physics for eighty years.

The revenge of the quaternion arrived with aerospace and the Apollo program. The Apollo spacecraft used physical three-axis mechanical gimbals to orient its inertial platform. During translunar coast, when spacecraft pitch approached $\pm 85^\circ$, the inner and outer gimbal rings lined up. The computer triggered a red warning light on the display console: \textbf{GIMBAL LOCK}. If the astronaut did not manually steer away, the platform lost a rotational degree of freedom and tumbled wildly, requiring hours of manual star-sighting realignment! NASA engineers had considered adding a fourth gimbal ring, but management rejected it as too heavy and mechanically complex.

When spacecraft guidance shifted from physical mechanical gimbals to strapdown software tracking in the 1970s, engineers discovered that unit quaternions are the mathematically ideal representation of 3D attitude. Unlike Euler angles, quaternions have \emph{zero singularities anywhere on the sphere}; unlike $3\times 3$ rotation matrices (which require 9 numbers and drift off orthogonality), a quaternion requires only 4 numbers, re-normalizes in four operations, and performs smooth spherical linear interpolation (SLERP). Today, every aircraft autopilot, drone, smartphone, and VR headset tracks attitude with Hamilton's quaternions.
\end{whybox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Rotation matrices are orthogonal
matrices ($\mat R^\top\mat R=\mat I$, Chapter~42); their
columns form an orthonormal basis -- the body axes. Quaternions are the unit-norm
elements of a 4-D algebra; ``rotate by sandwiching'' is a conjugation, the same pattern
as a change of basis $\mat P \mat A \mat P^{-1}$. Orientation lives on a curved manifold
($SO(3)$), which is precisely why no flat three-number chart (Euler angles) can cover it
without a singularity.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Why each sensor is not enough alone}
\label{ch36:why-not-enough}

We now have a language for orientation. Where do the numbers come from? Each sensor
offers a partial, flawed view. Fusion is worth doing only because the flaws are
\emph{complementary}.

\subsection{The gyroscope: smooth but drifting}
\label{ch36:gyro-drift}

A gyroscope measures \emph{angular rate} $\boldsymbol\omega(t)$ -- how fast the body is
rotating, in rad/s, about each body axis. To get orientation you integrate. In
quaternion form the kinematics are a clean differential equation,
\[
  \dot q = \tfrac{1}{2}\, q \otimes (0,\ \boldsymbol\omega),
\]
which in a microcontroller becomes the discrete step $q_{k} = q_{k-1} \otimes
\Delta q(\boldsymbol\omega_k,\Delta t)$, renormalized. Integration gives the gyro its
short-term smoothness and its long-term drift. Because it accumulates rate, the gyro is \emph{smooth} and
responsive over short times -- it tracks fast wrist flicks accurately. But it has no
idea where ``down'' is, and every small error in the rate accumulates.

\begin{happeningbox}
Two things make integrated gyro orientation walk away from the truth. The big one is
\emph{bias}: a real gyro reads a small nonzero rate even when perfectly still (Chapter~35). Integrating a constant bias $b$ gives an angle error that
grows \emph{linearly}, $b\cdot t$ -- a steady drift. The second is \emph{random-walk}:
broadband noise on the rate, once integrated, performs a random walk whose spread grows
like $\sqrt{t}$. Bias dominates after a few seconds. A cheap MEMS gyro might drift a
degree or more per second -- tolerable for a one-second gesture, ruinous over a minute.
\end{happeningbox}

\subsection{Accelerometer and magnetometer: noisy but absolute}
\label{ch36:absolute}

A stationary accelerometer measures the reaction to gravity: it reports an upward $1g$
specific force, which points along world ``up'' regardless of how long you have been
running. That gives you two of the three orientation angles directly -- \emph{roll} and
\emph{pitch} (the tilt of the body relative to gravity) -- as
\[
  \phi = \operatorname{atan2}(a_y,\,a_z), \qquad
  \theta = \operatorname{atan2}\!\big(-a_x,\ \sqrt{a_y^2+a_z^2}\big).
\]
These are \emph{absolute}: they do not drift, because gravity does not drift. The
magnetometer plays the same role for the remaining angle, \emph{yaw} (heading): the
Earth's field points roughly toward magnetic north, giving an absolute compass bearing
once you have tilt-compensated it using the accel-derived roll and pitch.

\begin{trapbox}
The accelerometer measures gravity \emph{plus all other acceleration}. The moment the
wrist actually moves -- and a gesture is nothing but motion -- the accel vector is
gravity contaminated by the motion you are trying to track. So the accel's ``down'' is
only trustworthy when the device is nearly still, or on average over time. Likewise the
magnetometer is wrecked by nearby steel, motors, speakers, and the very electronics on
your board (hard- and soft-iron distortion from Chapter~35). Both
absolute references are \emph{noisy and intermittently wrong}; only the gyro is reliable
moment to moment.
\end{trapbox}

The pattern is now sharp, and it is a frequency-domain pattern, which means we already
own the tools to exploit it:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The \textbf{gyro} is trustworthy at \emph{high} frequencies (fast changes) and
        untrustworthy at \emph{low} frequencies / DC (slow drift, bias).
  \item The \textbf{accel/mag} are trustworthy at \emph{low} frequencies (the long-term
        average really is down/north) and untrustworthy at \emph{high} frequencies
        (motion and noise).
\end{itemize}
Two signals that are each good in opposite halves of the spectrum -- and we know exactly
how to glue those: a high-pass on one, a low-pass on the other, added together so the
filters sum to unity. That is the complementary filter.

\begin{whybox}
Let $\theta_g$ be the orientation you get by integrating the gyro and $\theta_a$ the
orientation you get from the accelerometer (and magnetometer). Choose a high-pass
$H(s)$ and a low-pass $L(s)$ that are \emph{complementary}: $H(s)+L(s)=1$ for every
frequency. Then the fused estimate
\[
  \hat\theta = H(s)\,\theta_g + L(s)\,\theta_a
\]
keeps the gyro's fast detail (high-pass kills the gyro's low-frequency drift) and the
accel/mag's slow truth (low-pass kills their high-frequency motion noise), and because
$H+L=1$ a steady true orientation passes through with unit gain and no distortion. You
are not throwing away either sensor; you are using each only where it is reliable.
\end{whybox}

The discrete-time form is one line of code and is what runs on a real board. With a
single tuning constant $\alpha$ close to $1$,
\[
  \boxed{\ \hat\theta_k = \alpha\,\big(\hat\theta_{k-1} + \omega_k\,\Delta t\big)
           \;+\; (1-\alpha)\,\theta_{a,k}\ }
\]
Read it geometrically: \emph{predict} the new angle by stepping the previous estimate
forward with the gyro ($\hat\theta_{k-1}+\omega_k\Delta t$), then nudge that prediction
a little ($1-\alpha$ of the way) toward what the accelerometer says. The $\alpha$ term
is the high-pass on the gyro; the $(1-\alpha)$ term is the low-pass on the accel; they
sum to $1$, realizing the exact discrete-time complementary filter
(Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-compfreq}).

\begin{intuitionbox}
The cutoff between ``trust the gyro'' and ``trust the accel'' is set by $\alpha$ and the
sample time through the time constant $\tau = \dfrac{\alpha\,\Delta t}{1-\alpha}$.
Above $1/\tau$ the gyro wins; below it the accel wins. With $\Delta t = 0.01$\,s (a
$100$\,Hz loop) and $\alpha = 0.98$, $\tau \approx 0.49$\,s: trust the gyro for changes
faster than about half a second, trust the accel for anything slower. Want to lean
harder on the gyro (steadier, but drift creeps in)? Push $\alpha$ toward $1$.
\end{intuitionbox}

import numpy as np

def complementary_tilt(gyro, accel, dt, alpha=0.98):
    """gyro: (N,3) rad/s, accel: (N,3) g. Returns roll,pitch (deg)."""
    roll = pitch = 0.0
    out = []
    for w, a in zip(gyro, accel):
        # accel-only absolute tilt (good when nearly still)
        roll_a  = np.degrees(np.arctan2(a[1], a[2]))
        pitch_a = np.degrees(np.arctan2(-a[0], np.hypot(a[1], a[2])))
        # gyro integration (good for fast motion), then blend
        roll  = alpha*(roll  + np.degrees(w[0])*dt) + (1-alpha)*roll_a
        pitch = alpha*(pitch + np.degrees(w[1])*dt) + (1-alpha)*pitch_a
        out.append((roll, pitch))
    return np.array(out)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{applicationbox}
The complementary filter is everywhere because it is so cheap: self-balancing robots and
hoverboards, the early flight controllers of consumer drones (Multiwii, early
ArduPilot), camera gimbals, and countless hobby projects. On an 8-bit Arduino it is a
few multiplies per axis per loop. For two-axis tilt it is genuinely hard to beat for the
effort, and it is the right first thing to put on your wrist board.
\end{applicationbox}

Look again at $\hat\theta = H(s)\theta_g + L(s)\theta_a$ with $H+L=1$. This is exactly

\section{The Discrete Kalman Filter: Optimal State Estimation via Gaussian Precision Weighting}
\label{ch36:kalman}

In Section~\ref{ch36:complementary}, we solved the dual weaknesses of our inertial sensors by designing a complementary filter:
\[
  \hat\theta_k = \alpha(\hat\theta_{k-1} + \omega_k\,\Delta t) + (1-\alpha)\,\theta_{a,k} .
\]
We assigned fixed roles in frequency: a high-pass filter on the integrated gyro to capture rapid rotations while peeling off slow bias drift, combined with a low-pass filter on the accelerometer to provide an absolute gravitational anchor over time. We chose a constant crossover blending parameter, such as $\alpha = 0.98$.

Why does a fixed $\alpha$ fall short in an expressive wearable device? Because the physical conditions of motion change continuously:
\begin{itemize}
  \item When the user holds their arm steady between musical phrases, the accelerometer feels only the static $1g$ downward pull of gravity. The sensor reading $\theta_{a,k}$ is clean and trustworthy. During these quiet interludes, the filter should trust the accelerometer heavily to eliminate accumulated gyro drift.
  \item But when the user whips their wrist to strike a drum pad or flick a gesture, the sensor undergoes intense kinetic acceleration ($\vec{f} = \vec{a}_{\text{kin}} - \vec{g}$). The apparent gravity vector tilts wildly away from true vertical. A filter with a fixed $\alpha$ blindly incorporates this false tilt, causing the estimated pitch and roll to lurch sideways.
\end{itemize}

A fixed blending weight is an inflexible compromise. We need an estimator that computes the \emph{optimal} blending weight dynamically at every single sample, adjusting its trust in real time based on how uncertain each estimate currently is.

In 1960, Rudolf Kalman developed the mathematical framework that achieves this: the \textbf{Discrete Kalman Filter}. First implemented for real-time spacecraft navigation on the Apollo Guidance Computer (AGC) by Stanley Schmidt at NASA Ames Research Center, the Kalman filter dynamically tracks the uncertainty of both its internal physics prediction and incoming sensor observations, computing the optimal blend at every clock tick.

\subsection{The Foundation: Multiplying Two Gaussian Bell Curves Adds Their Precisions}
\label{ch36:kalman-precision}

The standard derivation of the Kalman filter often appears submerged under matrix Riccati equations and calculus of variations. Yet the entire algorithm flows directly from a single geometric picture: \textbf{multiplying two Gaussian bell curves multiplies their likelihoods, which simply adds their precisions ($1/\sigma^2$)}.

Consider the scalar problem of tracking an angle $x$. Before a sensor measurement arrives at time step $k$, our internal physics model (integrating the gyro rate) provides a prior belief about $x$. Under the assumption of linear dynamics and additive Gaussian noise, this prior belief is a Gaussian distribution centered at prediction $\hat{x}^- = \hat{x}_{k\mid k-1}$ with variance $P^- = P_{k\mid k-1}$:
\begin{equation}
  p_{\text{prior}}(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi P^-}} \exp\left( -\frac{(x - \hat{x}^-)^2}{2 P^-} \right) = \mathcal{N}(\hat{x}^-,\, P^-) .
  \label{ch36:eq-prior-gauss}
\end{equation}
The variance $P^-$ represents the spread of our uncertainty about the prior state.

Now, an accelerometer measurement $z = z_k$ arrives. The sensor does not reveal the true state directly; it yields an observation contaminated by zero-mean Gaussian measurement noise of variance $R$:
\begin{equation}
  p(z \mid x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi R}} \exp\left( -\frac{(z - x)^2}{2 R} \right) = \mathcal{N}(z,\, R) .
  \label{ch36:eq-meas-gauss}
\end{equation}

By Bayes' rule, the posterior probability density of the true angle $x$ given the measurement $z$ is proportional to the product of our prior belief and the measurement likelihood:
\begin{equation}
  p_{\text{post}}(x) \propto p_{\text{prior}}(x) \cdot p(z \mid x) \propto \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \left[ \frac{(x - \hat{x}^-)^2}{P^-} + \frac{(x - z)^2}{R} \right] \right) .
  \label{ch36:eq-bayes-product}
\end{equation}
Examine the quadratic expression inside the exponent:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{(x - \hat{x}^-)^2}{P^-} + \frac{(x - z)^2}{R} = x^2 \left( \frac{1}{P^-} + \frac{1}{R} \right) - 2x \left( \frac{\hat{x}^-}{P^-} + \frac{z}{R} \right) + \left( \frac{(\hat{x}^-)^2}{P^-} + \frac{z^2}{R} \right) .
  \label{ch36:eq-quadratic-expand}
\end{equation}
Because this expression is quadratic in $x$, the posterior distribution is \emph{itself a Gaussian distribution} $\mathcal{N}(\hat{x}, P)$:
\begin{equation}
  p_{\text{post}}(x) \propto \exp\left( -\frac{(x - \hat{x})^2}{2 P} \right) = \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \left[ \frac{x^2}{P} - 2x \frac{\hat{x}}{P} + \frac{\hat{x}^2}{P} \right] \right) .
\end{equation}
Equating coefficients of $x^2$ and $x$ between the two representations reveals the structure of Bayesian fusion:
\begin{align}
  \frac{1}{P} &= \frac{1}{P^-} + \frac{1}{R} , \label{ch36:eq-prec-sum} \\
  \frac{\hat{x}}{P} &= \frac{\hat{x}^-}{P^-} + \frac{z}{R} . \label{ch36:eq-mean-prec}
\end{align}

In probability and statistics, the reciprocal of variance $\tau = 1/\sigma^2$ is defined as the \textbf{precision} (certainty) of an estimate. Equation~\eqref{ch36:eq-prec-sum} establishes a fundamental rule:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Precision}_{\text{posterior}} = \text{Precision}_{\text{prior}} + \text{Precision}_{\text{measurement}} .
\end{equation}
When two independent Gaussian sources of information are fused, \textbf{their precisions simply add}.

Because precisions add, the posterior precision $1/P$ is strictly greater than both $1/P^-$ and $1/R$. Inverting this relationship demonstrates that the fused variance $P$ is strictly smaller than either individual source of uncertainty:
\begin{equation}
  P = \frac{1}{\frac{1}{P^-} + \frac{1}{R}} = \frac{P^- R}{P^- + R} < \min\left(P^-,\, R\right) .
  \label{ch36:eq-fused-variance}
\end{equation}
Fusing a prior prediction with a noisy measurement always contracts uncertainty. Geometrically, the posterior Gaussian bell curve sits between the prior and the measurement, and is visibly \textbf{taller and narrower} than both (Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-kalman-loop}B).

Now solve for the updated estimate $\hat{x}$ by multiplying Equation~\eqref{ch36:eq-mean-prec} by $P$:
\begin{align}
  \hat{x} &= P \left( \frac{\hat{x}^-}{P^-} + \frac{z}{R} \right) = \frac{P^- R}{P^- + R} \left( \frac{\hat{x}^-}{P^-} + \frac{z}{R} \right) \notag \\
          &= \frac{R}{P^- + R}\, \hat{x}^- + \frac{P^-}{P^- + R}\, z .
  \label{ch36:eq-mean-weighted}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{ch36:eq-mean-weighted} states that the posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of the prior and the measurement. To express this in the standard predictor-corrector form, rewrite $R = (P^- + R) - P^-$:
\begin{align}
  \hat{x} &= \frac{(P^- + R) - P^-}{P^- + R}\, \hat{x}^- + \frac{P^-}{P^- + R}\, z \notag \\
          &= \hat{x}^- + \frac{P^-}{P^- + R} \left( z - \hat{x}^- \right) .
  \label{ch36:eq-kalman-form}
\end{align}
Define the dimensionless ratio:
\begin{equation}
  K = \frac{P^-}{P^- + R} .
  \label{ch36:eq-kalman-gain-scalar}
\end{equation}
This ratio is the \textbf{Kalman Gain}. Substituting $K$ into the state and variance updates gives the celebrated 1D Kalman equations:
\begin{align}
  \hat{x} &= \hat{x}^- + K\, (z - \hat{x}^-) , \label{ch36:eq-scalar-state-update} \\
  P &= (1 - K)\, P^- . \label{ch36:eq-scalar-cov-update}
\end{align}
Notice how Equation~\eqref{ch36:eq-scalar-state-update} directly connects to the complementary filter:
\[
  \hat{x} = (1 - K)\, \hat{x}^- + K\, z .
\]
Where the complementary filter relied on a hand-tuned constant $\alpha$, the Kalman gain $K = P^- / (P^- + R)$ computes the exact balance between prior uncertainty and measurement noise.

\subsection{The Two Physical Limits of the Kalman Gain}
\label{ch36:kalman-limits}

The scalar Kalman gain $K = P^- / (P^- + R)$ is strictly confined to the interval $[0, 1]$. Examining its two asymptotic limits provides immediate physical insight into how the filter behaves under stress:

\paragraph{Limit 1: The Sensor Glitch ($R \to \infty$).}
Suppose an accelerometer axis suffers a transient spike due to severe table shock, or an optical tracking system temporarily loses line-of-sight. The measurement noise variance $R$ becomes immense ($R \to \infty$). In this regime:
\begin{equation}
  K = \lim_{R \to \infty} \frac{P^-}{P^- + R} = 0 .
\end{equation}
Substituting $K = 0$ into Equations~\eqref{ch36:eq-scalar-state-update} and~\eqref{ch36:eq-scalar-cov-update}:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{x} = \hat{x}^- + 0 \cdot (z - \hat{x}^-) = \hat{x}^- , \qquad P = (1 - 0)\, P^- = P^- .
\end{equation}
When the sensor is known to be untrustworthy, the Kalman gain drops to zero. The filter completely discounts the corrupt measurement $z$ and coasts purely on its internal physics prediction $\hat{x}^-$. Its uncertainty neither contracts nor diverges; it remains $P^-$.

\paragraph{Limit 2: Long Drift or the Perfect Sensor ($P^- \to \infty$ or $R \to 0$).}
Now consider the opposite situation: suppose the filter has dead-reckoned on gyro integration for a prolonged period without any sensor updates, so accumulated gyro drift has inflated the prior variance ($P^- \gg R$). Alternatively, suppose an ultra-precise optical calibration rig provides an absolute orientation fix with near-zero noise ($R \to 0$). In this regime:
\begin{equation}
  K = \lim_{P^- \to \infty} \frac{P^-}{P^- + R} = 1 .
\end{equation}
Substituting $K = 1$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{x} = \hat{x}^- + 1 \cdot (z - \hat{x}^-) = z , \qquad P = (1 - 1)\, P^- = 0 .
\end{equation}
When prior uncertainty is total, or the measurement is pristine, the Kalman gain becomes unity. The filter discards its past prediction entirely and snaps directly to the measurement $z$.

\begin{example}[A Concrete Kalman Step by Hand]
\label{ch36:ex-kalman}
A 1D orientation filter tracks wrist tilt angle $x$. At step $k-1$, the posterior estimate is $\hat{x}_{k-1} = 10.0^\circ$ with variance $P_{k-1} = 4.0\,\text{deg}^2$. 

During the next sample interval $\Delta t = 5\,\text{ms}$, the gyroscope measures angular velocity indicating a rotation of $u = 2.0^\circ$ with process noise variance $Q = 1.0\,\text{deg}^2$. An accelerometer measurement subsequently arrives reporting $z = 13.5^\circ$ with measurement noise variance $R = 9.0\,\text{deg}^2$.

\textbf{1. Predict (Time Update):}
\begin{align*}
  \hat{x}^- &= \hat{x}_{k-1} + u = 10.0^\circ + 2.0^\circ = 12.0^\circ , \\
  P^- &= P_{k-1} + Q = 4.0 + 1.0 = 5.0\,\text{deg}^2 .
\end{align*}
Uncertainty has expanded from $4.0$ to $5.0\,\text{deg}^2$ due to gyro process noise.

\textbf{2. Compute Kalman Gain:}
\[
  K = \frac{P^-}{P^- + R} = \frac{5.0}{5.0 + 9.0} = \frac{5.0}{14.0} \approx 0.357 .
\]
Because measurement noise $R = 9.0$ exceeds prior uncertainty $P^- = 5.0$, the gain is well below $0.5$, leaning more toward the prediction than the noisy sensor.

\textbf{3. Update (Measurement Fusion):}
\begin{align*}
  \hat{x} &= \hat{x}^- + K\, (z - \hat{x}^-) = 12.0^\circ + 0.357 \cdot (13.5^\circ - 12.0^\circ) = 12.0^\circ + 0.536^\circ = 12.54^\circ , \\
  P &= (1 - K)\, P^- = (1 - 0.357) \cdot 5.0 = 0.643 \cdot 5.0 = 3.21\,\text{deg}^2 .
\end{align*}
The state estimate moved only roughly a third of the way toward the accelerometer reading, and uncertainty contracted from $5.0\,\text{deg}^2$ down to $3.21\,\text{deg}^2$ --- tighter than both the prior prediction ($5.0$) and the sensor ($9.0$).
\end{example}

\subsection{The General Matrix Discrete Kalman Filter}
\label{ch36:kalman-matrix}

To track multi-axis orientation alongside physical states like time-varying gyroscope biases, the scalar equations expand into matrix state-space notation.

Let the state vector $\vec{x}_k \in \R^n$ evolve according to a linear discrete-time state-space model driven by a known control input $\vec{u}_k \in \R^p$ and observed by measurement vector $\vec{z}_k \in \R^m$:
\begin{align}
  \vec{x}_k &= \mat{F}\, \vec{x}_{k-1} + \mat{B}\, \vec{u}_k + \vec{w}_k , \quad &\vec{w}_k \sim \mathcal{N}(\vec{0}, \mat{Q}) , \label{ch36:eq-mat-state-model} \\
  \vec{z}_k &= \mat{H}\, \vec{x}_k + \vec{v}_k , \quad &\vec{v}_k \sim \mathcal{N}(\vec{0}, \mat{R}) , \label{ch36:eq-mat-obs-model}
\end{align}
where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mat{F} \in \R^{n \times n}$ is the state transition matrix mapping the previous state to the current state.
  \item $\mat{B} \in \R^{n \times p}$ is the control input matrix mapping control commands (such as gyro rates) into the state space.
  \item $\mat{H} \in \R^{m \times n}$ is the observation matrix mapping the internal state vector to expected sensor observations.
  \item $\mat{Q} \in \R^{n \times n}$ is the process noise covariance matrix, capturing physical disturbances and modeling approximations.
  \item $\mat{R} \in \R^{m \times m}$ is the measurement noise covariance matrix, capturing sensor electronic noise and vibrations.
\end{itemize}

The complete recursive discrete Kalman filter algorithm executes in two distinct phases at every time step $k$:

\begin{formalbox}
\textbf{Phase 1: Predict (Time Update / State Propagation)}
\begin{align}
  \hat{\vec{x}}_{k\mid k-1} &= \mat{F}\, \hat{\vec{x}}_{k-1\mid k-1} + \mat{B}\, \vec{u}_k , \label{ch36:eq-mat-pred-x} \\
  \mat{P}_{k\mid k-1} &= \mat{F}\, \mat{P}_{k-1\mid k-1}\, \mat{F}^\top + \mat{Q} . \label{ch36:eq-mat-pred-P}
\end{align}
The state estimate is propagated forward in time. Adding the positive semi-definite matrix $\mat{Q}$ expands the uncertainty covariance: $\mat{P}_{k\mid k-1} > \mat{P}_{k-1\mid k-1}$.

\textbf{Phase 2: Update (Measurement Update / Observation Fusion)}
\begin{align}
  \tilde{\vec{y}}_k &= \vec{z}_k - \mat{H}\, \hat{\vec{x}}_{k\mid k-1} \quad \text{(Innovation residual)} , \label{ch36:eq-mat-innov-y} \\
  \mat{S}_k &= \mat{H}\, \mat{P}_{k\mid k-1}\, \mat{H}^\top + \mat{R} \quad \text{(Innovation covariance)} , \label{ch36:eq-mat-innov-S} \\
  \mat{K}_k &= \mat{P}_{k\mid k-1}\, \mat{H}^\top \mat{S}_k^{-1} \notag \\
            &= \mat{P}_{k\mid k-1}\, \mat{H}^\top \left( \mat{H}\, \mat{P}_{k\mid k-1}\, \mat{H}^\top + \mat{R} \right)^{-1} \quad \text{(Kalman Gain Matrix)} , \label{ch36:eq-mat-gain-K} \\
  \hat{\vec{x}}_{k\mid k} &= \hat{\vec{x}}_{k\mid k-1} + \mat{K}_k\, \tilde{\vec{y}}_k , \label{ch36:eq-mat-update-x} \\
  \mat{P}_{k\mid k} &= \left( \mat{I} - \mat{K}_k\, \mat{H} \right) \mat{P}_{k\mid k-1} . \label{ch36:eq-mat-update-P}
\end{align}
The innovation residual $\tilde{\vec{y}}_k$ measures the discrepancy between observed reality and predicted observation. Weighting by the optimal Kalman gain matrix $\mat{K}_k$ contracts the state covariance: $\mat{P}_{k\mid k} < \mat{P}_{k\mid k-1}$.
\end{formalbox}

\begin{historybox}
In late 1959, Hungarian-American mathematician Rudolf E.~K\'alm\'an published his landmark paper, \emph{``A New Approach to Linear Filtering and Prediction Problems''}. Up to that point, signal processing and control theory lived in Norbert Wiener's frequency-domain framework ($H(s)$, transfer functions, and spectral factorization). Wiener's filter assumed infinite past history, stationary signals, and continuous-time signals; it could not handle time-varying noise, discrete sampled data, or transient initial conditions.

When K\'alm\'an visited NASA Ames Research Center in Mountain View in the autumn of 1960, aerospace engineers were grappling with a seemingly impossible challenge: how to steer the Apollo spacecraft to the Moon and back. Earth-based radar tracking and on-board optical sextant sightings of stars and lunar landmarks arrived at irregular intervals, corrupted by measurement noise. The spacecraft was constantly disturbed by venting thrusters and solar radiation pressure. 

Worse, the on-board Apollo Guidance Computer (AGC) was severely constrained: it possessed a total working RAM of just $2{,}048$ sixteen-bit words ($4$ kilobytes) and a cycle time of $12\,\mu\text{s}$. Solving a batch least-squares problem across thousands of historical tracking observations would require megabytes of memory and minutes of CPU inversion time.

Stanley F.~Schmidt at NASA Ames saw what classical engineers missed: K\'alm\'an's recursive state-space formulation did not require storing historical data! It maintained only the \emph{current state estimate} $\hat{\vec{x}}$ and its \emph{covariance matrix} $\mat{P}$. When a new star sighting arrived, the estimate updated in place via a single matrix gain multiplication. Schmidt extended Kalman's linear equations to non-linear orbital dynamics via Taylor-series linearization, creating the \emph{Extended Kalman Filter} (EKF). Schmidt's filter flew on the Apollo 11 Lunar Module and Command Module, guiding Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin to the lunar surface. Today, the Kalman filter runs inside every smartphone GPS, aircraft flight computer, and missile guidance system on Earth.

Half a century later, in 2011, while researching wearable biomedical sensors at the University of Bristol, Sebastian Madgwick recognized that wearable orientation tracking did not require full Riccati covariance propagation because the sensor noise characteristics of a given IMU are largely static. Madgwick reformulated attitude fusion as an analytical single-step gradient descent on the quaternion error surface. Rather than propagating an $n\times n$ covariance matrix, Madgwick's filter computes the direction in quaternion space that fastest minimizes the discrepancy between measured gravity/magnetic vectors and their predicted orientations, taking one gradient step per sample scaled by a single parameter $\beta$ representing the gyro drift rate. This collapsed the computational cost by two orders of magnitude---requiring fewer than $300$ basic floating-point operations per update with zero matrix inversions---so an 8-bit Arduino could execute it in under $1\,\text{ms}$. Alongside Robert Mahony's non-linear PI complementary filter (which adds an integral term to continuously estimate gyro bias), Madgwick's algorithm liberated orientation tracking from costly matrix algebra, making smooth, low-latency 3D tracking standard on wearable hardware.
\end{historybox}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The Fatal Naive Pitfall: Why the Full EKF Fails on Low-Power Microcontrollers}
If the Extended Kalman Filter is the mathematically optimal estimator, why doesn't every hobbyist and wearable project run an EKF?

The barrier is \textbf{computational complexity and numerical fragility}. A 9-DOF orientation EKF tracking attitude quaternion (4 states) and 3-axis gyro bias (3 states) has a $7$-dimensional state vector ($n=7$). At every single sensor sample ($200\,\text{Hz}$ to $500\,\text{Hz}$):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item It must evaluate non-linear trigonometric Jacobians $\mat{F}_k$ and $\mat{H}_k$.
  \item It must perform matrix multiplications $\mat{F}\mat{P}\mat{F}^\top + \mat{Q}$ ($O(n^3)$ operations).
  \item It must invert an innovation covariance matrix $\mat{S}_k = \mat{H}\mat{P}^-\mat{H}^\top + \mat{R}$ of size $6\times 6$ ($O(m^3)$ floating-point operations).
\end{enumerate}
On an 8-bit ATmega or low-power ARM Cortex-M0/M3 running at 16--48 MHz without a hardware floating-point unit (FPU), a single 7-state EKF step takes \textbf{15 to 30 milliseconds} of CPU time. At $200\,\text{Hz}$, the available time per sample is only $5\,\text{ms}$! The MCU locks up, missing ADC deadlines. In addition, roundoff error in single-precision floating point can destroy the positive-definiteness of the covariance matrix $\mat{P}$, causing the filter to compute negative variances ($\sigma^2 < 0$) and diverge into numerical garbage.
\end{trapbox}

\section{The Madgwick and Mahony filters}
\label{ch36:madgwick-mahony}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Both filters share one clean idea grounded in multivariable calculus (Chapter~42): \emph{gradient descent}. Integrate the gyro to predict the quaternion.
Now ask: given my predicted orientation, where \emph{should} the accelerometer point if
the only force were gravity? Compare that predicted gravity direction to the measured
accel. The mismatch is an \emph{error}, and you can compute which way to nudge the
quaternion to shrink that error fastest -- the gradient. Take a small step downhill.
That small correction, blended with the gyro integration, is the whole filter: gyro for
speed, a gradient nudge toward gravity (and north, with a magnetometer) for the absolute
anchor.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{whybox}
Madgwick frames fusion as an optimization: find the quaternion $q$ that best aligns the
\emph{predicted} field directions (gravity, and magnetic north) with the
\emph{measured} accel and mag vectors. That is a least-squares problem,
\[
  \min_q \ \norm{\,f(q,\ \text{measured fields})\,}^2 ,
\]
and one step of gradient descent on it is cheap to write in closed form. The single knob
$\beta$ sets the descent step size -- the analog of the complementary $\alpha$ and the
Kalman $K$. Small $\beta$: trust the gyro more (smooth, slight drift). Large $\beta$:
pull harder toward accel/mag (drift-free, more noise). Mahony instead drives the same
mismatch through a PI controller and, as a bonus, its integral term \emph{estimates and
removes the gyro bias} -- a real practical win.
\end{whybox}

\begin{focusbox}
The unifying picture (Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-loop}): \emph{every} attitude filter in this
chapter is the same skeleton.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Predict} orientation by integrating the gyro (fast, drifts).
  \item Measure how far the prediction's gravity/north disagree with the accel/mag.
  \item \textbf{Correct} by nudging the prediction toward the absolute references.
\end{enumerate}
They differ only in \emph{how} they choose the correction strength:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Complementary: fixed weight $\alpha$.
  \item Kalman/EKF: optimal time-varying weight $K$ from the noise statistics.
  \item Madgwick: gradient-descent step of size $\beta$.
  \item Mahony: PI feedback with gain pair, plus online bias estimation.
\end{itemize}
\end{focusbox}

\section{Getting a stable wrist orientation}
\label{ch36:wrist}

\begin{goalbox}
For the wrist controller, here is the recipe, in increasing order of effort.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Tilt mapping (start here).} Run a complementary filter on the 6-axis IMU
        for roll and pitch only. Map wrist tilt to two continuous MIDI controls -- e.g.
        pitch up/down to a pitch-bend, roll to a mod wheel. This is rock-solid, needs no
        magnetometer, and never drifts because gravity anchors it.
  \item \textbf{Full orientation.} Add the magnetometer and run Madgwick or Mahony to get
        a drift-resistant quaternion, hence heading too. Now ``which way the wrist
        points'' becomes a usable control, e.g. left/right pointing selects an
        instrument.
  \item \textbf{Bias and stillness.} Use Mahony's bias estimate (or a startup
        gyro-bias calibration while the arm rests) so the heading does not creep when the
        wrist is held still between notes.
\end{enumerate}
\end{goalbox}

\begin{buildbox}
A workable loop on an ESP32 or Teensy with an MPU-9250 / ICM-20948:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Rate.} Run the filter at $100$--$200$\,Hz. Gyro integration error grows
        with $\Delta t$, so a steady, fast loop matters more than raw sensor quality.
  \item \textbf{Calibrate first.} Apply the gyro-bias, accel, and (especially)
        magnetometer hard/soft-iron calibration from the previous chapter \emph{before}
        fusion. The strongest magnetic distortion is your own board -- mount the IMU away
        from the battery, motors, and high-current traces.
  \item \textbf{Startup.} Hold the arm still for one second at boot; average the gyro to
        seed the bias and the accel to seed the initial quaternion. Tune $\beta$ (or
        $\alpha$) to taste: too low feels sluggish and drifts, too high feels twitchy.
  \item \textbf{Report.} Keep the quaternion as the working state; convert to Euler only
        at the moment you emit a MIDI value, and watch the $\pm 90^\circ$ pitch
        singularity when you do.
\end{itemize}
\end{buildbox}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C,caption={Mahony-style correction core (per loop, quaternion q[4]).}]
// gx,gy,gz in rad/s; ax,ay,az normalized accel. Kp,Ki tuning gains.
// estimated direction of gravity from current quaternion:
float vx = 2*(q[1]*q[3] - q[0]*q[2]);
float vy = 2*(q[0]*q[1] + q[2]*q[3]);
float vz = q[0]*q[0] - q[1]*q[1] - q[2]*q[2] + q[3]*q[3];
// error = measured accel x estimated gravity (cross product):
float ex = (ay*vz - az*vy);
float ey = (az*vx - ax*vz);
float ez = (ax*vy - ay*vx);
ix += Ki*ex*dt; iy += Ki*ey*dt; iz += Ki*ez*dt;   // integral -> bias removal
gx += Kp*ex + ix;  gy += Kp*ey + iy;  gz += Kp*ez + iz;  // corrected rates
// integrate quaternion with corrected rates, then normalize q (omitted)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{What wrist orientation does, and where the fingers come from.} A wrist- or
forearm-worn IMU recovers the \emph{orientation of the forearm/wrist segment} -- tilt and
heading -- and discrete whole-hand gestures (a flick, a twist, a tap shock seen as an
accel spike). That alone gives the instrument several expressive axes. What the IMU
\emph{cannot} give you is which \emph{finger} moved: the IMU is bolted to the wrist, and
finger motion barely jostles it. But the fingers are far from out of reach. Each finger
is driven by tendons that run under the wrist and by forearm muscles whose electrical
activity reaches the skin, so finger movement is genuinely detectable from a band on the
wrist or forearm -- through \emph{force myography} (FMG, the tendon bulge under a band of
force sensors) or \emph{surface EMG} (the muscle's electrical pulses). With a short
per-finger calibration for each user, those signatures separate cleanly enough to drive
notes. That is the subject of the next chapter and the capstone. The one thing that
stays out of reach from the wrist is the \emph{continuous joint angle} of each finger --
how far each knuckle is bent at every instant. That information is not present in wrist
bulk motion and needs sensors \emph{on the fingers} (instrumented rings); see the
upgrade path in the capstone. Everything our instrument actually needs -- which finger,
how hard, when -- is on the doable side.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Summary}
\label{ch36:summary}

Orientation is a single geometric object -- a rotation carrying the world frame onto the
body frame -- and choosing how to write it down matters. Euler angles are intuitive but
suffer gimbal lock; rotation matrices and quaternions are global and smooth, and the
unit quaternion (axis-and-half-angle in four numbers) is what real filters carry. No
single sensor gives orientation: the gyro is smooth but drifts (bias integrates to a
linear error), while the accelerometer and magnetometer are absolute but noisy and
motion-corrupted. Their flaws are complementary in frequency, so we fuse them. The
complementary filter is a high-pass on the gyro plus a low-pass on the accel with a fixed
weight; the Kalman filter makes that weight optimal and time-varying via inverse-variance
(Bayesian) weighting; Madgwick and Mahony reach the same goal with a cheap gradient or PI
correction toward gravity and north, Mahony also estimating gyro bias. For the wrist
controller, start with complementary tilt, graduate to Madgwick/Mahony for full
orientation -- and remember that the wrist IMU sees forearm pose and whole-hand gestures.
Which finger moved is recovered separately, from tendon (FMG) or muscle (EMG) sensing
with per-user calibration; only the continuous per-joint finger angle stays out of reach
of a wrist band.

\section{Sensor Fusion in Practice: Boundaries, Real-World Traps, and the Hand}
\label{ch36:capabilities-limits}

\subsection{What Sensor Fusion Unlocked}
Sensor fusion resolves the fundamental paradox of inertial navigation: combining high-frequency velocity integration (which drifts over time) with low-frequency physical field observation (which is noisy and corruptible):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Drift-free, low-latency 3D tracking:} Fusing gyro angular rates with gravitational tilt cancels gyro bias drift completely in roll and pitch, giving responsive ($<2\,\text{ms}$) orientation tracking that stays rock-solid indefinitely.
  \item \emph{Singularity-free quaternion navigation:} Quaternions liberate algorithms from mechanical and mathematical gimbal lock, enabling aerobatic drones, spacecraft, smartphone camera stabilization, and wearable motion instruments to tumble freely without coordinate breakdown.
  \item \emph{Edge-executable fusion:} Algorithms like Madgwick and Mahony bring optimal multi-sensor fusion down to tiny, battery-powered microcontrollers without requiring matrix inversions or heavy floating-point coprocessors.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Honest Downsides and Inherent Limitations}
Even optimal fusion filters face unyielding physical boundaries:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{The Yaw Observability Wall:} Gravity points purely along the vertical axis. Accelerometers provide zero information about rotation around the gravity vector (yaw). Without an external reference (a clean magnetometer, optical cameras, or radio beacons), heading/yaw is \textbf{unobservable} and \emph{must drift} over time at the rate of gyro bias instability.
  \item \emph{Centrifugal and Linear Acceleration Corruption:} When the wrist moves vigorously, the specific force measured by the accelerometer is $\vec{f} = \vec{a}_{\text{kin}} - \vec{g}$. A naive filter interprets kinetic acceleration as a change in gravity, temporarily tilting the perceived vertical until motion ceases.
  \item \emph{The Hand vs.\ Arm Boundary:} Orientation tracking tells you the exact spatial pose of the forearm in 3D space. It tells you \emph{nothing} about what the fingers are doing inside the hand.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Road Ahead: From Arm Kinematics to Muscle Sensing}
We have mastered the rigid-body kinematics of the forearm. But a musician playing an expressive controller does not merely rotate their arm --- they curl their index finger, pinch their thumb, tap rhythms, and clench fists. Because the wrist bone's motion reveals none of these finger actions, \textbf{Chapter 37} moves from mechanics to physiology: exploring \textbf{Electromyography (EMG)}, \textbf{Force Myography (FMG)}, and \textbf{Bioacoustics} to sense the tendons and muscle bellies of the forearm directly.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}
Explain in your own words why three Euler angles cannot smoothly cover all orientations
without a singularity, and identify the orientation(s) at which the roll-pitch-yaw
convention gimbal-locks. What physical degree of freedom is lost there?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
A gyro has a constant bias of $0.5^\circ$/s and otherwise perfect output. Starting from a
known orientation, compute the heading error after $1$, $10$, and $60$ seconds of pure
integration. Now suppose the bias is zero but there is white rate noise; explain why the
error grows like $\sqrt{t}$ instead of $t$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Show that the discrete complementary filter
$\hat\theta_k = \alpha(\hat\theta_{k-1}+\omega_k\Delta t) + (1-\alpha)\theta_{a,k}$ is a
first-order high-pass on the gyro plus a first-order low-pass on the accel. Find the
crossover time constant $\tau$ and evaluate it for $\alpha=0.95$, $\Delta t=0.005$\,s.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Why is the accelerometer trustworthy for orientation only when the device is nearly
still or averaged over time? Write the measured accel as gravity plus motion and explain
which part the low-pass branch of the complementary filter is trying to keep.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Repeat Example~\ref{ch36:ex-kalman} but with the device held still, so you receive three
accel measurements $z = 13.5, 13.4, 13.6$ with no gyro motion ($u=0$). Run three
predict/update cycles and report how $\hat x$, $P$, and $K$ evolve. What happens to $K$
as $P$ shrinks, and what does that mean physically?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Derive the Kalman gain $K=P^-/(P^-+R)$ from the product-of-two-Gaussians fact: if the
prediction is $\mathcal N(\hat x^-, P^-)$ and the measurement is $\mathcal N(z,R)$, show
the posterior mean is the inverse-variance-weighted average and rewrite it in the form
$\hat x^- + K(z-\hat x^-)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Convert the unit quaternion $q=(\cos 30^\circ, 0, 0, \sin 30^\circ)$ to a rotation
matrix using the formula in Section~\ref{ch36:quat}. What axis and angle of rotation does
it represent? Verify the matrix is orthogonal with determinant $+1$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Both Madgwick and Mahony correct the gyro prediction toward gravity. In the C sketch, the
error is computed as the \emph{cross product} of measured accel and estimated gravity.
Explain geometrically why a cross product is the right error signal -- what its direction
and magnitude tell you about the misalignment, and why it vanishes when the two vectors
agree.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Tune by experiment: implement the complementary filter and test $\alpha \in
\{0.90,\allowbreak 0.98,\allowbreak 0.999\}$ on the same recorded wrist motion. Describe qualitatively the
trade-off you see between responsiveness, noise, and drift, and state which you would
pick for (a) a fast percussive trigger and (b) a slow expressive filter sweep.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Kinematic limits and centrifugal corruption. Consider a wrist-worn 9-axis IMU during an expressive musical gesture:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item A performer pivots their forearm around the elbow at angular velocity $\omega = 4\,\text{rad/s}$ with the wrist sensor located at distance $r = 0.35\,\text{m}$. Compute the centrifugal acceleration $a_c = \omega^2 r$ experienced by the accelerometer. What percentage of Earth's gravitational acceleration $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ does this false tilt represent?
  \item Explain why an uncompensated complementary filter tilts dramatically during this fast swing, and how acceleration-norm gating ($\|\vec{a}\| \approx 1g$) prevents kinetic acceleration from corrupting the gravitational vertical.
  \item When the performer wiggles individual fingers while keeping the wrist perfectly stationary, explain from rigid-body kinematics why the IMU registers no angular velocity or linear acceleration, and identify which bio-sensing modalities (developed in Chapter 37) can be integrated into the wrist strap to detect tendon activation without placing sensors on the fingers.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
