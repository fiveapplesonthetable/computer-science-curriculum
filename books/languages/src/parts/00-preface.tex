\chapter*{Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Preface}

Most books about programming languages fall into one of two camps. Neither camp quite helps a working engineer jump from an older object-oriented language to modern systems and type-driven software.

The first camp is the syntax tour: here is a loop in Rust, a class in Kotlin, a goroutine in Go. After fifty pages you can transcribe small programs. Then the Rust borrow checker rejects an ordinary listener callback, or TypeScript narrows a union type, or a Go interface holding a \texttt{nil} pointer evaluates \texttt{err != nil} to \texttt{true}, and you hit a wall. Syntax was never the hard part. The mental model underneath the syntax was.

The second camp is pure language theory: operational semantics, lambda calculus reductions, and inference rules stacked like fractions before you ever inspect a byte in memory. Theory matters. Algebraic data types, parametric polymorphism, variance, and linear ownership are clean mathematical ideas. Yet studying them apart from stack frames, cache lines, vtable pointers, and real production failures hides the engineering pressure that forced language designers to invent them.

This book works in both layers at once. One eye stays on the hardware: stack frames, 64-byte cache lines, object headers, fat pointers, and state-machine layouts. The other stays on the type system and mathematical structure that rule out whole classes of bugs at compile time. Our running example is a concrete production service: a double-entry financial ledger in Rust with Axum, SQLx, and PostgreSQL.

\section*{Who this book is for, and where we start}

We assume a very specific, realistic starting point:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{You know Java at roughly the Java~6 level.} You are comfortable with classes, interfaces, inheritance (\texttt{extends}, \texttt{implements}), \texttt{ArrayList} and \texttt{HashMap} generics, \texttt{null}, checked and unchecked \texttt{try}/\texttt{catch}/\texttt{finally} exceptions, \texttt{synchronized} blocks, OS threads, and garbage collection. You do \emph{not} need Java~8 streams, records, or sealed classes --- when modern JVM ideas appear, we build them cleanly from Java~6 upward.
  \item \textbf{You know basic C and basic C++.} You have written \texttt{for} loops, \texttt{if}/\texttt{else} branches, functions, \texttt{struct} declarations, arrays, \texttt{malloc}/\texttt{free}, and raw pointers (\texttt{T*} and \texttt{\&x}). You do \emph{not} need modern C++11--C++20 move semantics, smart pointers, templates, or RAII --- Chapter~2 builds all of those from scratch.
\end{itemize}

Because you already know loops, conditionals, and functions, we spend zero pages re-explaining basic control flow. Every page tackles the questions that rewire how you read and write software:

\begin{itemize}
  \item When you declare a variable in Java~6, C++, Rust, Go, Kotlin, or TypeScript, \emph{what exact bytes sit on the stack and on the heap?} How many pointer hops stand between the CPU and your integer, and who frees those bytes when?
  \item Why did Rust drop class inheritance, \texttt{null}, exceptions, and garbage collection? How do ownership, borrowing (\texttt{\&T} versus \texttt{\&mut T}), lifetimes (\texttt{'a}), algebraic data types (\texttt{struct} and \texttt{enum}), and traits fit into one coherent picture?
  \item How do functional programmers in \textbf{OCaml} and \textbf{Haskell} think? Without academic jargon, how do immutability, algebraic variants, pattern matching, higher-order pipelines, typeclasses, functors, and monads show up inside everyday Rust?
  \item How do \textbf{TypeScript}, \textbf{Kotlin}, and \textbf{Go} each solve the pain points of Java~6? We compare TypeScript's erased structural types, Kotlin's null-safe hierarchies and JVM coroutines, and Go's flat structs, fat-pointer interfaces, and goroutine scheduler.
  \item How do these machine-level and type-level tools pay off when real money is on the line? In Part~V we build an asynchronous \textbf{Rust + Axum + SQLx + PostgreSQL} ledger where negative transfers, currency mismatches, lost updates, and unbalanced entries are prevented by construction.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Why a financial ledger is our north star}

Toy examples flatter weak abstractions. Almost any language looks pleasant when you are printing \texttt{Animal.speak()} for a \texttt{Dog} and a \texttt{Cat}. Financial systems are unforgiving in a much more instructive way:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Representation precision matters.} Binary floating-point (\texttt{double} / \texttt{f64}) turns $0.10 + 0.20$ into $0.30000000000000004$. Silent integer overflow turns a large credit into a negative balance. Mixing USD cents with EUR cents or swapping an \texttt{AccountId} with a \texttt{JournalId} corrupts books silently unless distinct types separate them at zero runtime cost.
  \item \textbf{Illegal states must be unrepresentable.} Suppose a Java~6 \texttt{PaymentOrder} class has eight nullable fields and three boolean flags (\texttt{isVerified}, \texttt{isSettled}, \texttt{isFailed}). Eight flag combinations exist even when only three make business sense, so every method starts with defensive \texttt{null} checks. Sum types (\texttt{enum}s) and typestate rule out the invalid combinations at compile time.
  \item \textbf{Concurrency and partial failure are adversarial.} Suppose two HTTP requests withdraw $\$80$ at once from a $\$100$ balance, or a client drops its TCP connection mid-transfer. What happens to the lock, the memory, and the uncommitted PostgreSQL transaction? Answering that requires understanding \texttt{Future} state machines, RAII drop guards, and database isolation together.
\end{enumerate}

Even in Parts~I through~IV, you will meet orders, accounts, minor-unit amounts, and ledger postings alongside buffers, parsers, and schedulers. By the time we reach Part~V, the capstone service builds entirely on ideas you already own.

\section*{How to read this book, and what the boxes mean}

Every concept follows a four-step rhythm. First comes the concrete bug or hardware cost. Second comes the byte-level picture. Third comes the language or type-system rule, and fourth comes idiomatic code side by side across languages.

Throughout the chapters, recurring colored boxes tell you what kind of thinking a passage asks for:

\begin{intuitionbox}
The core mental picture --- the physical or geometric way of seeing a rule so you never have to memorize it by rote. If you revisit a chapter months later, reading the intuition boxes reconstructs the whole arc.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{focusbox}
A central invariant or guiding principle that organizes an entire section or language design choice --- for instance, Rust's \emph{Aliasing XOR Mutability} rule or TypeScript's \emph{types as sets of values}.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{formalbox}

\end{formalbox}

\begin{whybox}
The engineering and mathematical reason \emph{why} a rule takes the shape it does. For instance, why can C++ move constructors not skip the moved-from destructor, and why must mutable references in Rust be invariant?
\end{whybox}

\end{trapbox}

\end{labbox}

\begin{applicationbox}

\section*{A ten-minute preview: one transfer, three mental models}

To taste the difference between reading syntax and reading through the machine-and-types lens, look at the same miniature operation --- debiting an account balance --- written in Java~6, basic C, and Rust.

First, a familiar Java~6 method:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Java]
// Java 6: heap reference, implicit null, exception control flow
public class Account {
    private Long balanceCents; // boxed heap object!

    public void debit(Long amountCents) throws InsufficientFundsException {
        if (amountCents <= 0) {
            throw new IllegalArgumentException("amount must be positive");
        }
        if (this.balanceCents < amountCents) {
            throw new InsufficientFundsException(this.balanceCents, amountCents);
        }
        this.balanceCents = this.balanceCents - amountCents;
    }
}
\end{lstlisting}

Look past the clean surface and ask what the JVM actually executes, and what the type signature hides:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Hidden \texttt{null}s.} Both \texttt{balanceCents} and \texttt{amountCents} are boxed \texttt{java.lang.Long} references. If a caller passes \texttt{null}, the check \texttt{amountCents <= 0} calls \texttt{amountCents.\allowbreak longValue()} under the hood and throws \texttt{NullPointerException} --- an exit path mentioned nowhere in the signature.
  \item \textbf{Pointer chasing and allocation.} Reading \texttt{this.balanceCents} follows a pointer to the \texttt{Account} object and its 16-byte JVM header. Then it follows a second pointer to a separate \texttt{Long} object on the heap: another 16-byte header plus 8 payload bytes, or 24 bytes just to hold one integer. Subtracting \texttt{amountCents} allocates a fresh \texttt{Long} unless the result falls inside \texttt{Long.valueOf}'s tiny $[-128, 127]$ cache.
  \item \textbf{Unrestricted aliasing.} Any number of threads, lists, and caches may hold references to this same \texttt{Account} simultaneously while \texttt{debit} mutates it in place. Nothing in the type tells you whether you hold the only handle or one of fifty.
\end{enumerate}

Now swing to the opposite extreme in C:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C]
/* C: flat bytes in memory, zero GC, zero compile-time guardrails */
#include <stdint.h>

typedef struct {
    uint64_t id;
    int64_t  balance_cents;
} Account;

int account_debit(Account *acct, int64_t amount_cents) {
    if (acct == NULL || amount_cents <= 0) return -1;
    if (acct->balance_cents < amount_cents) return -2;
    acct->balance_cents -= amount_cents;
    return 0;
}
\end{lstlisting}

The hardware picture is now plain: \texttt{Account} is a flat 16-byte value (\texttt{id} at offset 0, \texttt{balance\_cents} at offset 8), with zero object headers and zero heap allocations during subtraction. Yet notice what we gave up to get that flatness:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{acct} might still be \texttt{NULL}, or worse, a \emph{dangling pointer} to a stack frame that already returned or a heap block already passed to \texttt{free()}.
  \item The error codes \texttt{-1} and \texttt{-2} sit in an ordinary \texttt{int}. A caller who writes \texttt{account\_debit(\&a, 5000);} and forgets to check the return value continues executing as if the debit succeeded.
  \item Nothing stops two pointers from aliasing the same \texttt{Account} across threads or across function arguments while mutating it.
\end{itemize}

Now look at the idiomatic Rust version we will be able to read down to the register level by the end of Part~II:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rust]
// Rust: flat C-like memory layout + compile-time ownership & algebraic types
#[derive(Debug, Clone, Copy, PartialEq, Eq)]
pub struct PositiveCents(i64); // invariant: always > 0

#[derive(Debug, PartialEq, Eq)]
pub enum DebitError {
    InsufficientFunds { available: i64, requested: i64 },
    ArithmeticOverflow,
}

pub struct Account {
    pub id: u64,
    balance_cents: i64,
}

impl Account {
    pub fn debit(&mut self, amount: PositiveCents) -> Result<(), DebitError> {
        if self.balance_cents < amount.0 {
            return Err(DebitError::InsufficientFunds {
                available: self.balance_cents,
                requested: amount.0,
            });
        }
        self.balance_cents = self.balance_cents
            .checked_sub(amount.0)
            .ok_or(DebitError::ArithmeticOverflow)?;
        Ok(())
    }
}
\end{lstlisting}

Pause and count how many runtime checks and heap allocations disappeared \emph{at the same time}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Exact C memory layout.} \texttt{Account} occupies the exact same 16 flat bytes as the C \texttt{struct}, and \texttt{PositiveCents(i64)} compiles down to a bare 64-bit register value with zero wrapper header.
  \item \textbf{No \texttt{null}, no dangling pointer, no aliasing race.} The parameter \texttt{\&mut self} proves at compile time that \texttt{self} points to a live, aligned \texttt{Account}. It also proves that no other active reference anywhere in the program can read or write this \texttt{Account} while \texttt{debit} runs.
  \item \textbf{Invariants baked into types.} The inner \texttt{i64} inside \texttt{PositiveCents} is private to its module. Callers can build one only through a smart constructor that checks \texttt{val > 0} once at the boundary, so \texttt{debit} needs no \texttt{amount <= 0} check.
  \item \textbf{Errors as flat values.} \texttt{Result<(), DebitError>} is a stack-allocated tagged union, not a heap-allocated exception unwinding stack frames. And if a caller ignores a \texttt{Result}, the Rust compiler immediately warns that a \texttt{\#[must\_use]} failure value was dropped.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Roadmap of the five parts}

The fourteen chapters of this book are grouped into five progressive parts:

\textbf{Part~I --- Leaving Java~6: Memory, Values, and the Machine (Chapters~1--2).}
Chapter~1 strips away the JVM illusion and inspects what Java~6 actually puts in RAM. We trace stack frames, 16-byte object headers, pointer chasing across 64-byte cache lines, GC pauses, aliasing bugs, \texttt{null}, and exceptions against flat value arrays. Chapter~2 builds \textbf{Modern C++ (C++11--C++20)} from basic C/C++. You will learn deterministic RAII destructors, move semantics, smart pointers, non-owning views (\texttt{string\_view}, \texttt{span}), monomorphized templates with C++20 Concepts, and lambdas. It closes with the four C++ footguns --- dangling views, vector reallocation, use-after-move, and data races --- that Rust eliminates at compile time.

\textbf{Part~II --- Seeing Through the Rust Lens (Chapters~3--6).}
Chapter~3 derives Rust's core invariant, \emph{Aliasing XOR Mutability}. It maps the stack and heap bytes of \texttt{String}, \texttt{\&str}, \texttt{Vec<T>}, \texttt{\&[T]}, \texttt{Box<T>}, \texttt{Rc<T>}, \texttt{Arc<T>}, \texttt{Cell<T>}, \texttt{RefCell<T>}, and \texttt{Mutex<T>}. Chapter~4 demystifies lifetimes (\texttt{'a}), Non-Lexical Lifetimes, elision, \texttt{'static}, variance, and the \texttt{unsafe} proof boundary. Chapter~5 develops product types (\texttt{struct}) and sum types (\texttt{enum}), niche optimizations where \texttt{Option<\&T>} takes 8 bytes, \texttt{Option}/\texttt{Result}/\texttt{?}, and exhaustive matching. Chapter~6 covers traits, generics, monomorphized static dispatch versus 16-byte \texttt{(data\_ptr, vtable\_ptr)} fat pointers, and coherence rules.

\textbf{Part~III --- Thinking Functionally and Writing Idiomatic Rust (Chapters~7--8).}
Chapter~7 visits Rust's functional ancestors, \textbf{OCaml} and \textbf{Haskell}, to replace imperative loop-and-flag habits. From OCaml we take expression-oriented code, Hindley--Milner inference, variants, structural sharing, and pipelines; from Haskell we connect purity, lazy iterators, typeclasses, \texttt{.map()}, and \texttt{.and\_then()}/\texttt{?} to Rust's zero-allocation closures and iterators. Chapter~8 shows how experienced engineers structure Rust systems in practice. We replace cyclic OOP object graphs with flat \texttt{Vec} arenas and typed handles, apply \emph{Parse, Don't Validate}, and build newtypes, typestate (\texttt{PhantomData}), view APIs, and clean error hierarchies.

\textbf{Part~IV --- Modern Type Systems and Concurrency: TypeScript, Kotlin, and Go (Chapters~9--11).}
With both the hardware lens and the Rust/FP lens in hand, other modern languages snap into focus quickly. Chapter~9 builds \textbf{TypeScript} from set theory and structural subtyping: \texttt{never} ($\emptyset$), \texttt{unknown} ($U$), unions, intersections, narrowing, conditional types (\texttt{infer}), branded types, and \texttt{Zod} boundary parsing. Chapter~10 bridges Java~6 to \textbf{Kotlin} down to JVM bytecode, covering null-safe types, \texttt{value class}, \texttt{sealed interface}, \texttt{reified} generics, and Continuation-Passing Style (\texttt{suspend}) coroutines. Chapter~11 dissects \textbf{Go} --- value structs, escape analysis, fat-pointer interfaces, the typed-\texttt{nil} trap, and the \texttt{G-M-P} goroutine scheduler --- before comparing all eight languages side by side.

\textbf{Part~V --- Capstone: A Financial Backend in Rust with Axum, SQLx, and PostgreSQL (Chapters~12--14).}
Chapter~12 opens up \textbf{Async Rust and Tokio}. We trace how \texttt{async fn} compiles into a flat \texttt{enum} state machine, why self-borrows across \texttt{.await} need \texttt{Pin<\&mut Self>}, work-stealing tasks, backpressure, and cancellation safety. Chapter~13 models \textbf{money and double-entry ledgers} in types: minor-unit integers, checked arithmetic, banker's rounding with remainder allocation, currency tags, and balanced-entry proof tokens (\texttt{BalancedEntry}). Chapter~14 wires up the complete \textbf{Axum + Tower + SQLx + PostgreSQL} service. It combines schema constraints, compile-time checked SQL (\texttt{sqlx::query!}), ordered \texttt{SELECT ... FOR UPDATE} row locks, idempotency keys, a transactional outbox, graceful shutdown, and integration tests.

\section*{How to get the most out of every chapter}

Do not read passively. When a diagram shows a 24-byte \texttt{Vec<T>} header on the stack, grab scrap paper and trace every word across a reallocation or a move. When a chapter contrasts Java~6, C++, and Rust, ask three questions. Which bugs failed at runtime in Java~6? Which became undefined behavior in C++? And which exact rule rejected them at compile time in Rust?

Every chapter closes with hands-on exercises. You will calculate byte sizes and alignments, spot aliasing or concurrency bugs, predict compiler errors, and refactor imperative code into type-checked state machines. Working through them is where fluency sticks. Try these four short warm-ups using only what we just previewed.

\section*{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Counting hidden exit paths in Java~6]
Go back to the ten-line Java~6 \texttt{Account.debit} method in this preface. List every distinct way a call \texttt{acct.debit(amt)} can terminate \emph{without} returning normally --- including what happens if \texttt{acct} itself is \texttt{null}, if \texttt{amt} is \texttt{null}, or if \texttt{balanceCents} was left \texttt{null} by an incomplete constructor. Which of those exit paths are visible in the method's type signature?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Silent overflow with signed 64-bit cents]
Suppose the Java~6 \texttt{Account} class used primitive \texttt{long} instead of \texttt{Long}, and a separate \texttt{credit(long amount)} method updated \texttt{balanceCents += amount} without a range check. In Java (and two's-complement hardware), what happens if \texttt{balanceCents} equals \texttt{Long.MAX\_VALUE} ($9{,}223{,}372{,}036{,}854{,}775{,}807$) and \texttt{credit(100)} executes? Contrast this silent wraparound with \texttt{checked\_add} returning \texttt{Option<i64>} in Rust.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The self-transfer aliasing bug]
Consider this short C transfer function that reads both balances before writing:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=C]
void transfer(Account *from, Account *to, int64_t cents) {
    int64_t f = from->balance_cents;
    int64_t t = to->balance_cents;
    if (f >= cents) {
        from->balance_cents = f - cents;
        to->balance_cents   = t + cents;
    }
}
\end{lstlisting}
Trace line by line what happens when a caller invokes \texttt{transfer(\&alice, \&alice, 5000)} with \texttt{alice}'s starting balance equal to \texttt{10000}. What is \texttt{alice}'s final balance, and where did the extra $\$50.00$ come from? Why does a Rust signature taking \texttt{from: \&mut Account} and \texttt{to: \&mut Account} reject \texttt{transfer(\&mut alice, \&mut alice, ...)} at compile time?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Floating-point money on a napkin]
In IEEE-754 double-precision binary floating point (\texttt{double} in Java/C, \texttt{f64} in Rust), the decimal fraction $0.1 = \frac{1}{10}$ has the infinite repeating binary expansion $0.0001100110011\dots_2$, just as $\frac{1}{3} = 0.3333\dots_{10}$ repeats forever in base $10$. Suppose a payment service adds ten thousand micro-fees of $\$0.01$ stored as \texttt{double} and then compares \texttt{total == 100.00} with \texttt{==}. Explain in two sentences why binary floating point rounds $\frac{1}{100}$, and why storing integer minor units (\texttt{1} cent $= \texttt{1\_i64}$) makes addition exact.
\end{exercise}
