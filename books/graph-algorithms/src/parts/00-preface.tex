\chapter*{Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Preface}

Graphs are a language for modeling problems. You stop asking how to run standard algorithms like breadth-first search (BFS) and start asking how to represent your problem. A scheduling constraint becomes a directed edge (a one-way link); a bottleneck becomes a cut (a partition of the vertices into two sets); a measure of similarity becomes a weight (a value on an edge); a combinatorial question (such as finding the best way to group items) becomes an eigenvalue problem (a specific type of equation from linear algebra). This book shows you how to make that shift, prove that the algorithms work, and analyze exactly how fast they run.

We start with breadth-first search on a small graph, and work up step by step to algorithms that solve massive network flow problems in near-linear time (meaning the running time is almost proportional to the size of the graph). We justify each step along the way.

\section*{Who this book is for}

This is the \emph{advanced companion} to a friendly introductory algorithms book.
We assume you have seen the basics before. You have seen BFS and depth-first search (DFS), you know what a stack (last-in, first-out) and a queue (first-in, first-out) are, and you can read a recursive function (a function that calls itself). You have also heard the terms ``Dijkstra'' (for shortest paths) and ``minimum spanning tree'' (for the cheapest way to connect everything). We assume you are comfortable with big-$\Oh$ notation, which bounds the running time from above as the input size grows; its partners $\Om$ (a lower bound) and $\Th$ (upper and lower bound together) are recapped in Section~\ref{ch42:asymptotics}. You will be comfortable when we write $\Th(m + n\log n)$ (where $m$ is the number of edges and $n$ is the number of vertices).

We do not assume you remember the proofs, or have seen advanced material. We re-derive the foundations precisely so that the later algorithms -- such as finding matchings in complex networks, dynamic data structures, and algebraic graph theory -- rest on proofs you have seen. If you can follow a proof by induction and draw pictures, you are ready.

\begin{intuitionbox}
A useful self-test. Take a connected undirected graph (a network with two-way links where every vertex can reach every other vertex) and remove one edge. When does the graph fall into two pieces? If the phrase ``that edge was a bridge'' came to mind, you have the right background. If it did not, you will after the chapter on bridges and biconnectivity (see Figure~\ref{fig:bridge_example}).
\end{intuitionbox}

\section*{What ``from the ground up'' actually means here}

For every major algorithm, the chapter follows a consistent sequence:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A picture and an intuition.} Before writing pseudocode, we draw a small graph and ask what should happen to build a mental model.
  \item \textbf{The algorithm, in pseudocode.} Every major algorithm is stated precisely, with data structures named explicitly.
  \item \textbf{A correctness argument.} We prove the algorithm works and name the proof technique (such as a loop invariant or an exchange argument).
  \item \textbf{A tight complexity analysis.} We provide the exact running time bound and identify the bottleneck operation.
  \item \textbf{A trace by hand.} We run the algorithm on a small graph, showing the data-structure state at each step.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{whybox}
Why insist on intuition \emph{and} full rigor? Pure intuition produces algorithms that seem plausible but fail on edge cases, like disconnected components. Pure formalism produces proofs you can verify but cannot reconstruct or modify. Combining both allows you to re-derive an algorithm from scratch and ensure it works on all valid inputs.
\end{whybox}

\section*{How the three books fit together}

This volume is one of three related books.

\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{The intro algorithms book} is the prequel. It introduces pseudocode, big-$\Oh$ notation, recursion, core data structures, and basic graph algorithms. Where that book introduces an algorithm, this book proves it rigorously and explores advanced variations.

\textbf{The linear algebra book} supplies the mathematical language for the second half of this volume. When we write down the Laplacian matrix (a matrix representing the graph's connections),
\[ \Lap = \mathbf{D} - \Adjm \]
in the spectral chapters, we are doing spectral theory: eigenvalues, eigenvectors, the Rayleigh quotient, positive semidefiniteness, and the quadratic form
\[ x^\top \Lap x = \sum_{(u,v)\in E}(x_u - x_v)^2 \]
The linear algebra book builds these concepts from scratch. The whole spectral arc -- which studies graphs using matrices -- is an extended application of that book. A graph becomes a matrix, a cut becomes a vector, a bottleneck becomes the second-smallest eigenvalue, and solving a network becomes solving a linear system
\[ \Lap x = b \]
\end{connectionbox}

This book is self-contained, and the appendix gathers the necessary data-structure and linear-algebra facts. The Connection boxes link to the companion books to provide a unified picture.

\section*{The shape of the book: from BFS to Laplacian solvers}

The book is structured into five broad territories:

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Territory I --- Foundations and traversal.} Representations, BFS, DFS, topological order, strongly connected components, minimum spanning trees, and shortest paths. We redo this foundation rigorously.

\textbf{Territory II --- Cuts, flows, and matchings.} We cover bridges and biconnectivity, global minimum cut, maximum flow, min-cost flow, matching algorithms, and the art of modeling a problem as a flow.

\textbf{Territory III --- Structure on trees and sparse graphs.} We cover tree decompositions, lowest common ancestors, planarity, graph coloring, independent sets, and perfect graphs. By exploiting the structure of sparse graphs, intractable problems become easier to solve.

\textbf{Territory IV --- The spectral world.} We introduce the Laplacian matrix, spectral graph theory, expanders, random walks, electrical networks, and Markov chains. This is where graphs become linear algebra.

\textbf{Territory V --- Hardness, scale, and the frontier.} We cover NP-hard graph problems, approximation algorithms, dynamic graphs, massive graphs, and near-linear-time solvers for electrical flows.
\end{focusbox}

One quantity links the first chapters to the last: connectivity. In the BFS chapter, connectivity asks whether you can reach vertex $t$ from vertex $s$. In the maximum-flow chapter, it asks \emph{how much} can flow from $s$ to $t$. In the spectral chapters, it asks how well-connected the graph is \emph{as a whole}, measured by the second eigenvalue $\lambda_2$ of the Laplacian matrix. In the random-walks chapter, connectivity is the physical conductance of a resistor network. The figure below summarizes these four stages:

\section*{A worked trace, so you know what they look like}

The single habit this book encourages most is tracing an algorithm by hand on a small graph. Let us trace breadth-first search (BFS) to demonstrate.

Consider this undirected graph on six vertices. We will run BFS from vertex $s$ and ask which vertices it reaches, in what order, and at what distance.

\begin{intuitionbox}
BFS explores outward in rings. It visits everything at distance $0$ (just $s$), then everything at distance $1$ (the neighbors of $s$), then distance $2$, and so on. A first-in, first-out (FIFO) queue naturally enforces this order: distance-$k$ vertices are enqueued and dequeued before any distance-$(k+1)$ vertex. This ensures every vertex is reached via the shortest path.
\end{intuitionbox}

Here is the BFS algorithm.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Breadth-First Search from a source}
\label{ch00:alg-bfs}
\SetKwInOut{Input}{Input}\SetKwInOut{Output}{Output}
\Input{Graph $\Gph=(V,E)$ as adjacency lists; source $s \in V$.}
\Output{$\dist[v]$ = number of edges on a shortest $s$--$v$ path, or $\infty$.}
\ForEach{$v \in V$}{$\dist[v] \gets \infty$}
$\dist[s] \gets 0$\;
$Q \gets$ empty FIFO queue; enqueue $s$\;
\While{$Q$ is not empty}{
  $u \gets$ dequeue($Q$)\;
  \ForEach{neighbor $w$ of $u$}{
    \If{$\dist[w] = \infty$}{
      $\dist[w] \gets \dist[u] + 1$\;
      enqueue $w$ into $Q$\;
    }
  }
}
\Return $\dist$\;
\end{algorithm}

Let us trace the algorithm. Assume neighbors are visited in alphabetical order. The table shows the queue (front on the left) and the distances discovered after each step.

The final distances are $\dist[s]=0$, $\dist[a]=\dist[b]=1$, $\dist[c]=\dist[d]=2$, and $\dist[e]=3$. The rings correspond to the items in the queue: $s$ alone, then $\{a,b\}$, then $\{c,d\}$, then $\{e\}$. The edges $a$--$b$ and $d$--$e$ discovered no new vertices because they connect vertices in the same ring or an already-finished ring. Distinguishing between tree and non-tree edges is essential for algorithms like cycle detection (finding paths that loop back to their start).

\begin{whybox}
\emph{Why the distances are correct.} BFS explores outward in concentric rings. Because the queue is first-in, first-out, all vertices at distance $k$ are explored before any at distance $k+1$. When a new vertex $w$ is first encountered from a vertex $u$, the path through $u$ has length $\dist[u] + 1$, which is the shortest possible path.
\end{whybox}

\begin{complexitybox}
Each vertex is enqueued and dequeued at most once, so the \textbf{while} loop runs $\Oh(n)$ times. Across all iterations, the inner \textbf{foreach} loop scans each adjacency list exactly once. The total number of edge inspections in an undirected graph is
\[ \sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) = 2m \]
where $\deg(v)$ is the number of edges connected to vertex $v$. With adjacency lists and an array-backed queue, every individual operation is $\Oh(1)$. The total time is $\Th(n+m)$ and the extra space is $\Th(n)$. This is optimal because, in the worst case, reachability requires examining every vertex and edge.
\end{complexitybox}

\begin{trapbox}
The most common BFS bug is marking a vertex as visited \emph{when you dequeue it} instead of \emph{when you enqueue it}. If marked at dequeue, a vertex can be enqueued multiple times by different neighbors, causing the queue to swell to $\Th(m)$ and degrading performance. Always mark vertices as visited (or update their distance) before enqueueing them.
\end{trapbox}

Every chapter follows this pattern: picture, pseudocode, trace, correctness, cost, and common traps.

\section*{How to read this book}

Read with a pen. Try tracing the algorithms yourself before reading our traces. Drawing small graphs often reveals their underlying structure.

\begin{marginnotebox}
There are two ways to read this book. \textbf{Linear:} Read straight through. \textbf{Targeted:} Jump to a specific tool (like max-flow) and follow the backward Connection boxes to fill gaps.
\end{marginnotebox}

Do not let the proofs block you. On a first pass, you can read the \emph{Why it works} box and trace the algorithm. Understanding what is true and why it works is enough to use an algorithm. Later, read the full proof to understand how to modify the algorithm for new problems.

\begin{historybox}
The order of chapters roughly follows the history of the field because problems typically preceded their solutions. BFS, DFS, and shortest paths came early, followed by maximum flows. Later, spectral methods connected graph structure to eigenvalues, and modern solvers synthesized these ideas. We introduce concepts in this order.
\end{historybox}

\section*{Exercises}

These are warm-up exercises to get you started.

\begin{exercise}[Trace it cold]
Redraw the undirected six-vertex graph from the worked trace, then add one edge:
$c$--$d$. Re-run BFS from $s$ by hand, writing the queue and the discovered
distances after each dequeue exactly as we did. Which distances change, and which
do not? Identify, for the new edge, whether it is discovered as a tree edge or
found pointing at an already-seen vertex.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Changing the starting point]
On the undirected graph from the worked trace, run the search starting from vertex
$c$ instead of $s$. In what order are the vertices discovered, and what is the
maximum distance from $c$ to any reachable vertex?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Break the algorithm on purpose]
Implement the dequeue-time-marking bug described in the \emph{Common trap} box:
move the ``mark as visited'' step from enqueue time to dequeue time. On the
undirected graph, exhibit a vertex that enters the queue twice. Then construct, or
describe, a family of graphs on $n$ vertices where this bug pushes the queue to
size $\Th(n^2)$ even though the correct algorithm keeps it at $\Oh(n)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Draw your own graph]
Draw an undirected connected graph with 6 vertices where every vertex has degree at
least 2, but the shortest-path distance between some pair of vertices is at least 3.
Trace a search from one endpoint of that diameter to the other.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A counting sanity check]
The \emph{Cost summary} box asserts
\[ \sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) = 2m \]
for an undirected graph. Count the degrees of all six vertices in the worked undirected graph, sum them, and confirm the total is twice the number of edges you drew. Explain in your own words why every undirected edge contributes exactly 2 to the sum of degrees.
\end{exercise}
