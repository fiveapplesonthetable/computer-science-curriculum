\chapter{Random Walks and Electrical Networks}

A token sits on a vertex of a graph. At each step it chooses one of the edges touching its current vertex, each with equal probability, and slides to the other end. How many steps, on average, until the token reaches a target vertex? How long until it visits every vertex? And where do bottlenecks trap it? These random-walk questions can be answered in three ways: by tracking one random step at a time, by multiplying a transition matrix repeatedly, or by replacing every edge with a resistor and studying how electric current flows through the resulting circuit.

In that circuit, vertex voltages equal probabilities of reaching one terminal before another, and pushing a unit of current from $s$ to $t$ requires a voltage drop proportional to the average round-trip walk time between $s$ and $t$. Questions that look difficult to analyze by summing over infinitely many random paths reduce to solving a single linear system from Ohm's and Kirchhoff's circuit laws.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Replace each edge of an $m$-edge graph by a $1$-ohm resistor and wire a $1$-volt battery across two vertices $s$ and $t$, holding $s$ at $1\,\text{V}$ and $t$ at $0\,\text{V}$. Current flows from $s$ to $t$, splitting at junctions. Two identities turn this physical circuit into a tool for random walks:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The \emph{voltage} at any vertex equals the probability that a random walker starting there reaches $s$ before $t$. At every vertex other than $s$ and $t$ (an \emph{interior} vertex), the voltage equals the average of its neighbors' voltages --- a property called \emph{harmonicity}, which matches the one-step averaging of the walk.
  \item The \emph{effective resistance} $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)$ (the voltage drop required to drive one unit of current from $s$ to $t$) equals $\frac{1}{2m}$ times the \emph{expected} (average) number of steps for the walk to travel from $s$ to $t$ and back, called the \emph{commute time}.
\end{itemize}
Physics (low effective resistance) and probability (short expected round trip) give the same quantitative measure of how well connected $s$ and $t$ are.
\end{intuitionbox}

\section{The simple random walk and its transition matrix}

Fix a connected undirected graph $\Gph=(V,E)$ with $n=\abs{V}$ vertices and $m=\abs{E}$ edges. For most of the chapter we take edges unweighted; positive edge weights $w_{ij}>0$ generalize every formula by replacing vertex degrees with weighted degrees. Write $\deg(i)$ for the degree of vertex $i$, $\Adjm$ for the adjacency matrix, $\mathbf{D}=\operatorname{diag}(\deg(1),\dots,\deg(n))$ for the diagonal degree matrix, $\mathbf{I}$ for the $n\times n$ identity matrix, and $\Lap=\mathbf{D}-\Adjm$ for the graph Laplacian from Chapter~31.

\begin{definition}[Simple random walk]\label{ch35:def-srw}
A \emph{Markov chain} on $V$ is a sequence of random vertices $(X_0,X_1,X_2,\dots)$ in which the distribution of the next vertex $X_{t+1}$ depends only on the current vertex $X_t$, not on earlier steps. The \emph{simple random walk} on $\Gph$ is the Markov chain that, from vertex $i$, moves to a neighbor chosen uniformly at random (each neighbor with equal probability $1/\deg(i)$):
\[
  \Pr[X_{t+1}=j \mid X_t=i] \;=\;
  \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{\deg(i)} & \{i,j\}\in E,\\[6pt] 0 & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
\]
where $\Pr[X_{t+1}=j \mid X_t=i]$ denotes the conditional probability of stepping to $j$ given $X_t=i$. The \emph{transition matrix} is $\mathbf{P} \in \R^{n\times n}$ with $(\mathbf{P})_{ij}=\Pr[X_{t+1}=j\mid X_t=i]$, or compactly $\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{D}^{-1}\Adjm$.
\end{definition}

Each row of $\mathbf{P}$ has nonnegative entries that sum to $1$ (the walker must move to some neighbor): a matrix with this property is called \emph{row-stochastic}. If $p_t \in \R^{n}$ is the row vector of vertex probabilities $(p_t)_i = \Pr[X_t=i]$ at step $t$, then one step of the walk is a vector--matrix product:
\[
  p_{t+1} \;=\; p_t \,\mathbf{P}, \qquad\text{and by induction}\qquad p_t \;=\; p_0\,\mathbf{P}^{\,t}.
\]
Multi-step walk probabilities are governed by the matrix powers $\mathbf{P}^{\,t}$, which ties the long-run behavior of the walk to the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{P}$ and of the related Laplacian $\Lap = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})$.

\subsection{The stationary distribution}

\begin{definition}[Stationary distribution]\label{ch35:def-stationary}
A probability row vector $\pi$ (nonnegative entries summing to $1$) is \emph{stationary} for the walk if $\pi\,\mathbf{P}=\pi$. If the initial state $X_0$ is drawn from $\pi$, then $p_t = \pi\,\mathbf{P}^{\,t} = \pi$ at every step $t \ge 0$.
\end{definition}

On an \emph{irregular} graph (one whose vertices do not all have the same degree), the stationary probability of a vertex is proportional to its degree rather than uniform. High-degree hubs are visited more often because more edges lead into them.

\begin{theorem}[Degree-proportional stationarity]\label{ch35:thm-stationary}
On a connected undirected graph, the vector $\pi \in \R^n$ with entries
\[
  \pi_i \;=\; \frac{\deg(i)}{2m}
\]
is the unique stationary distribution of the simple random walk.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
First verify $\pi_i \ge 0$ and $\sum_{i=1}^n \pi_i = \frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^n \deg(i) = \frac{2m}{2m} = 1$. Now compute the $j$-th entry of $\pi\mathbf{P}$:
\begin{align*}
  (\pi\mathbf{P})_j &= \sum_{i\in V}\pi_i (\mathbf{P})_{ij} \\
  &= \sum_{i:\{i,j\}\in E}\frac{\deg(i)}{2m}\cdot\frac{1}{\deg(i)} \\
  &= \sum_{i:\{i,j\}\in E}\frac{1}{2m} \\
  &= \frac{\deg(j)}{2m} \\
  &= \pi_j.
\end{align*}
The two $\deg(i)$ factors cancel, leaving $\frac{1}{2m}$ for each of the $\deg(j)$ neighbors of $j$, so $\pi\mathbf{P}=\pi$.

For uniqueness, suppose $\sigma\mathbf{P}=\sigma$ is any stationary distribution, and set $v_i = \sigma_i/\deg(i)$. Then $\sigma_j = \sum_{i:\{i,j\}\in E} \sigma_i/\deg(i)$ rewrites as $v_j = \frac{1}{\deg(j)}\sum_{i:\{i,j\}\in E} v_i$: every vertex's $v$-value is the average of its neighbors' $v$-values. Pick a vertex $i^\star$ where $v$ attains its maximum $M$. For the average of $i^\star$'s neighbors to equal $M$, every neighbor of $i^\star$ must also have $v=M$. Repeating along paths in the connected graph $\Gph$ shows $v_i = M$ for all $i\in V$, so $\sigma_i = M\deg(i)$. Since $\sum_i \sigma_i = 1$ and $\sum_i \deg(i) = 2m$, we must have $M = \frac{1}{2m}$ and $\sigma = \pi$.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuitionbox}
\textbf{Detailed balance and time-reversibility.}
A Markov chain is \emph{irreducible} if every state can reach every other state in one or more steps; on an undirected graph, irreducibility is equivalent to connectivity. For general directed chains, uniqueness of $\pi$ follows from the \emph{Perron--Frobenius theorem} (Chapter~36), which guarantees a unique positive eigenvector for any irreducible stochastic matrix.

On an undirected graph something stronger holds. For every edge $\{i,j\}\in E$,
\begin{align*}
  \pi_i (\mathbf{P})_{ij} &= \frac{\deg(i)}{2m} \cdot \frac{1}{\deg(i)} \;=\; \frac{1}{2m}
  \;=\; \frac{\deg(j)}{2m} \cdot \frac{1}{\deg(j)} \;=\; \pi_j (\mathbf{P})_{ji}.
\end{align*}
This identity, $\pi_i (\mathbf{P})_{ij} = \pi_j (\mathbf{P})_{ji}$, is called \emph{detailed balance}, and a Markov chain that obeys it is called \emph{reversible}: at stationarity, the probability flux crossing edge $\{i,j\}$ from $i$ to $j$ equals the reverse flux from $j$ to $i$ (namely $\frac{1}{2m}$ in each direction on every edge, as Figure~\ref{fig:ch35-detailed-balance} illustrates). Reversibility is the exact reason undirected random walks correspond to two-way resistor networks, as Section~\ref{ch35:electrical} makes precise.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{trapbox}
Two conditions are needed for $p_t$ to converge to $\pi_i=\deg(i)/(2m)$ from every starting vertex:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Disconnected graph}: stationarity is not unique, because each connected component traps the walk and supports its own stationary distribution.
  \item \emph{Bipartite graph}: a state has \emph{period} $k$ if every return to it takes a multiple of $k$ steps, and is \emph{aperiodic} when $k=1$. On a bipartite graph every cycle has even length, so every vertex has period $2$: $\pi$ is still the unique stationary distribution, but $p_t$ alternates between the two sides of the bipartition and never settles. Any odd cycle, such as the triangle in $\Gph$, makes the walk aperiodic and restores convergence. To avoid periodicity on arbitrary graphs, replace $\mathbf{P}$ by the \emph{lazy random walk} $\mathbf{P}'=\tfrac12(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{P})$, which flips a fair coin at each step and stays put on heads; $\mathbf{P}'$ is aperiodic on every graph and shares the same stationary distribution $\pi$.
\end{itemize}
\end{trapbox}

\section{Hitting times and cover times}

\begin{definition}[Hitting and commute times]\label{ch35:def-hitting}
For vertices $a,b \in V$, let $T_b=\min\{t\ge 0: X_t=b\}$ be the first step at which the walk visits $b$. The \emph{hitting time} is the expected (average) number of steps until that first visit when starting from $a$:
\[
  H(a,b)\;=\;\mathbb{E}\bigl[T_b \mid X_0=a\bigr].
\]
The \emph{commute time} is the expected round-trip time, $C(a,b)=H(a,b)+H(b,a)$. The \emph{cover time} from $a$ is $\mathbb{E}[\max_{v\in V}T_v\mid X_0=a]$, the expected number of steps until \emph{every} vertex has been visited at least once; the cover time of $\Gph$ is the worst case over starting vertices,
\[
  \operatorname{cov}(\Gph)\;=\;\max_{a\in V}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\max_{v\in V}T_v \;\Big|\; X_0=a\Bigr].
\]
\end{definition}

Hitting time is generally \emph{not} symmetric: $H(a,b)\ne H(b,a)$. In our running graph, stepping from the leaf $5$ toward the triangle is quick, whereas wandering out to the leaf $5$ from inside the triangle is slow because the walk keeps cycling among $\{1,2,3\}$. The commute time adds both directions, so $C(a,b)=C(b,a)$ is always symmetric.

\subsection{Hitting times by first-step analysis}

To compute $H(a,b)$, we use \emph{first-step analysis}: break the walk from $a\ne b$ into its first step (which takes $1$ unit of time and lands at each neighbor $c$ of $a$ with probability $1/\deg(a)$) plus the remaining expected steps $H(c,b)$ from that neighbor:
\begin{equation}\label{ch35:eq-firststep}
  H(a,b) \;=\; 1+\frac{1}{\deg(a)}\sum_{c:\{a,c\}\in E} H(c,b),
  \qquad H(b,b)=0.
\end{equation}
For a fixed target $b$, \eqref{ch35:eq-firststep} is a system of $n-1$ linear equations in the $n-1$ unknowns $\{H(a,b):a\ne b\}$. Writing $h$ for this vector of hitting times and deleting row and column $b$ from $\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}$, \eqref{ch35:eq-firststep} takes the matrix form $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\,h=\mathbf{1}$ on the non-$b$ coordinates, where $\mathbf{1}$ is the all-ones column vector.

\begin{example}[Hitting times on the tail of $\Gph$]\label{ch35:ex-hitting}
Compute the hitting times $h_a = H(a,5)$ to the leaf vertex $5$ on $\Gph$. Because swapping vertices $1$ and $2$ preserves the graph and fixes vertex $5$, symmetry gives $h_1=h_2$. With $h_5=0$, the equations \eqref{ch35:eq-firststep} for $a=1,2,3,4$ are:
\begin{align*}
  h_1 &= 1+\tfrac12 h_2+\tfrac12 h_3,\\
  h_2 &= 1+\tfrac12 h_1+\tfrac12 h_3,\\
  h_3 &= 1+\tfrac13 h_1+\tfrac13 h_2+\tfrac13 h_4,\\
  h_4 &= 1+\tfrac12 h_3+\tfrac12\cdot 0.
\end{align*}
Substitute $h_2 = h_1$ into the first equation and eliminate downward one variable at a time:
\begin{align*}
  h_1 = 1 + \tfrac12 h_1 + \tfrac12 h_3 &\;\implies\; h_1 = 2 + h_3, \\
  h_3 = 1 + \tfrac23(2 + h_3) + \tfrac13 h_4 &\;\implies\; \tfrac13 h_3 = \tfrac73 + \tfrac13 h_4 \;\implies\; h_3 = 7 + h_4, \\
  h_4 = 1 + \tfrac12(7 + h_4) &\;\implies\; \tfrac12 h_4 = \tfrac92 \;\implies\; h_4 = 9.
\end{align*}
Back-substituting gives $h_3 = 7 + 9 = 16$ and $h_1 = h_2 = 2 + 16 = 18$. Starting at vertex $1$ or $2$, the walk takes $18$ steps on average to reach the leaf $5$ (Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-hitting}). Example~\ref{ch35:ex-commute} recovers $H(4,5)=9$ directly from effective resistance.

\subsection{Bounds on hitting and cover times}

How large can hitting and cover times grow as functions of $n$ and $m$? Commute time and effective resistance (Section~\ref{ch35:commute}) yield a cubic worst-case bound.

\begin{theorem}[Polynomial hitting time]\label{ch35:thm-hitbound}
On any connected graph with $n$ vertices and $m$ edges,
\[
  H(a,b) \;\le\; 2m(n-1) \;<\; n^3
\]
for all $a,b\in V$. This cubic exponent is tight: on the $n$-vertex \emph{lollipop graph} (a complete graph on $\floor{n/2}$ vertices joined at one vertex to a path on the remaining vertices), $H(u,w)=\Th(n^3)$ from the attachment vertex $u$ to the path endpoint $w$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Theorem~\ref{ch35:thm-commute} proves $C(a,b)=2m\,R_{\mathrm{eff}}(a,b)$, and the effective resistance between $a$ and $b$ is at most the shortest-path length $\dist(a,b) \le n-1$ (unit resistors in series along that path). Since $m \le \binom{n}{2} = n(n-1)/2$,
\begin{align*}
  H(a,b) \;\le\; C(a,b) &= 2m\,R_{\mathrm{eff}}(a,b) \\
  &\le 2m(n-1) \\
  &\le n(n-1)^2 \;<\; n^3.
\end{align*}
On the lollipop graph (Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-lollipop}), the clique $K_{\floor{n/2}}$ contributes $m=\Th(n^2)$ edges while the stem path of length $\ceil{n/2}$ has effective resistance $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(u,w)=\Th(n)$, so $C(u,w)=2m\,R_{\mathrm{eff}}(u,w)=\Th(n^3)$. Walking from the path tip $w$ back to $u$ never enters the clique and takes $H(w,u)=\Th(n^2)$ steps, leaving $H(u,w)=C(u,w)-H(w,u)=\Th(n^3)$: almost every excursion from $u$ gets pulled back into the dense clique before reaching $w$.
\end{proof}

The cover time pays at most an additional logarithmic factor above the maximum pairwise hitting time.

\begin{theorem}[Matthews bound]\label{ch35:thm-matthews}
Let $H_{\max}=\max_{a,b\in V}H(a,b)$. The cover time satisfies
\[
  \operatorname{cov}(\Gph)\;\le\; H_{\max}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k}\;=\;H_{\max}\,\Oh(\log n).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Idea]
Pick a uniformly random ordering $v_1,v_2,\dots,v_n$ of $V$, independent of the walk, and let $L_k = \max_{1\le j\le k} T_{v_j}$ be the step when all of $\{v_1,\dots,v_k\}$ have been visited. Because the ordering is uniformly random, each of $\{v_1,\dots,v_k\}$ is equally likely to be the last of those $k$ vertices visited, so $\Pr[L_k > L_{k-1}] = \Pr[T_{v_k} = L_k] \le \frac{1}{k}$ (the same symmetry behind the \emph{coupon-collector problem} of collecting all $n$ distinct prizes from random boxes). Whenever $v_k$ has not yet been visited at time $L_{k-1}$, the expected additional wait $L_k - L_{k-1}$ from the walk's position at $L_{k-1}$ is at most $H_{\max}$. Summing $\mathbb{E}[L_k - L_{k-1}] \le \frac{1}{k}H_{\max}$ from $k=1$ to $n$ produces the $n$-th \emph{harmonic number} $\sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{k} \le 1 + \ln n = \Oh(\log n)$. Matthews also proved a matching lower bound: for any subset $S\subseteq V$, $\operatorname{cov}(\Gph) \ge \bigl(\min_{a,b\in S,\,a\ne b} H(a,b)\bigr)\sum_{k=1}^{\abs{S}-1}\frac{1}{k}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{complexitybox}
For an $n$-vertex, $m$-edge connected graph: hitting time satisfies $H(a,b)=\Oh(mn)=\Oh(n^3)$, tight on the lollipop graph. Cover time satisfies $\operatorname{cov}(\Gph)=\Oh(mn\log n)$; it is $\Th(n\log n)$ on the complete graph $K_n$ and $\Th(n^2)$ on the path $P_n$ and cycle $C_n$. Computing exact hitting times to a target vertex requires solving one Laplacian linear system: $\Oh(n^3)$ time by Gaussian elimination, or $\tilde\Oh(m)$ time (where $\tilde\Oh$ hides polylogarithmic factors in $n$ and accuracy $\log(1/\eps)$) using the nearly linear Laplacian solvers of Chapter~41.
\end{complexitybox}

\section{The electrical-network analogy}\label{ch35:electrical}

Turn the graph $\Gph$ into an electrical circuit: replace each edge $\{i,j\}\in E$ by a resistor of resistance $r_{ij}>0$ (measured in ohms, $\Omega$, quantifying opposition to current), or equivalently of \emph{conductance} $c_{ij}=1/r_{ij}$ (measuring ease of current flow; set $c_{ij}=0$ when $\{i,j\}\notin E$). For the unweighted simple random walk, every edge is a unit resistor ($r_{ij}=1\,\Omega$, $c_{ij}=1$). On a weighted graph with positive edge weights $w_{ij}$, set $c_{ij}=w_{ij}$ so the walk transitions from $i$ to $j$ with probability $(\mathbf{P})_{ij}=c_{ij}/c_i$, where $c_i=\sum_{j\in V} c_{ij}$ is the total conductance at vertex $i$ ($\deg(i)$ in the unit case).

\subsection{Voltages are harmonic functions}

Assign an electric potential, or \emph{voltage}, $v(i)\in\R$ (measured in volts, $\text{V}$) to each vertex $i$. \emph{Ohm's law} states that the current (in amperes, $\text{A}$) flowing along edge $\{i,j\}$ from $i$ to $j$ is $i_{ij}=c_{ij}\bigl(v(i)-v(j)\bigr)$. \emph{Kirchhoff's current law} states that at every vertex $i$ where no external current is injected or extracted, the net current leaving $i$ sums to zero:
\[
  \sum_{j:\{i,j\}\in E} c_{ij}\bigl(v(i)-v(j)\bigr) \;=\; 0.
\]
Solving this equation for $v(i)$ gives
\begin{align*}
  v(i) &= \frac{\sum_{j\in V} c_{ij}\,v(j)}{\sum_{j\in V} c_{ij}} \\
  &= \sum_{j\in V} (\mathbf{P})_{ij}\,v(j).
\end{align*}
A function $v$ obeying this averaging rule at $i$ is called \emph{harmonic} at $i$: its value equals the conductance-weighted average of its neighbors' values, which is also $(\mathbf{P}v)(i)$, one step of the walk's transition operator applied to $v$.

\begin{whybox}
Harmonicity at vertex $i$, Kirchhoff's current law at $i$, and $v(i)=(\mathbf{P}v)(i)$ are three ways of writing the same equation. The weighted graph Laplacian $\Lap$ (with $\Lap_{ii}=c_i$ and $\Lap_{ij}=-c_{ij}$ for $i\ne j$) combines all three: $(\Lap v)(i)=\sum_{j} c_{ij}\bigl(v(i)-v(j)\bigr)$ is the net current leaving vertex $i$, so $v$ is harmonic at $i$ if and only if $(\Lap v)(i)=0$.
\end{whybox}

Now connect an external current source that injects $f$ amperes at vertex $s$ and extracts $f$ amperes at vertex $t\ne s$. Let $\mathbf{1}_s \in \R^n$ denote the indicator vector with $1$ in entry $s$ and $0$ elsewhere. Kirchhoff's law at all $n$ vertices becomes the Laplacian linear system
\[
  \Lap v \;=\; f\,(\mathbf{1}_s-\mathbf{1}_t).
\]
Because the all-ones vector $\mathbf{1}$ lies in the null space (kernel) of $\Lap$, adding any constant to every entry of $v$ leaves all voltage differences and currents unchanged; fixing one vertex's voltage to $0$ (called \emph{grounding} that vertex) pins down the additive constant.

Given a boundary set $B\subset V$ with prescribed voltages, finding a function $v$ that matches those values on $B$ and is harmonic on the \emph{interior} $V\setminus B$ is called the \emph{Dirichlet problem}. Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-dirichlet-analogy} compares the electrical and random-walk solutions on our running graph for $B=\{1,5\}$.

\begin{theorem}[Voltages solve the Dirichlet problem]\label{ch35:thm-dirichlet}
Fix distinct vertices $s\ne t$ in a connected graph and boundary values $v(s)=1$, $v(t)=0$. There is a unique function $v:V\to\R$ satisfying those boundary values that is harmonic at every interior vertex $a\in V\setminus\{s,t\}$. For every vertex $a\in V$, $v(a)$ equals the probability that the random walk started at $a$ reaches $s$ before $t$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Existence and uniqueness.} Harmonicity on $I=V\setminus\{s,t\}$ with $v(s)=1,v(t)=0$ is a square linear system $\Lap_{II}v_I = b$ in the interior unknowns $v_I$, where $\Lap_{II}$ deletes rows and columns $s$ and $t$ from $\Lap$. Each diagonal entry of $\Lap_{II}$ equals the full vertex conductance $c_a$, which is at least the sum of absolute values of off-diagonal entries in its row (a property called \emph{diagonal dominance}), and strictly larger in every row adjacent to $s$ or $t$. On a connected graph $\Lap_{II}$ is invertible (Chapter~31, Theorem~31.5), so the solution $v$ exists and is unique.

\emph{Probabilistic identity.} Let $p(a)=\Pr[\text{reach }s\text{ before }t\mid X_0=a]$. By definition, $p(s)=1$ and $p(t)=0$. For any interior vertex $a\notin\{s,t\}$, first-step conditioning gives $p(a)=\sum_{j\in V}(\mathbf{P})_{aj}\,p(j)$, so $p$ is harmonic on $I$ with the same boundary values as $v$. Uniqueness forces $p=v$.
\end{proof}

Harmonicity also enforces the \emph{maximum principle}: a harmonic function attains its maximum and minimum on the boundary, because no interior vertex can strictly exceed all of its neighbors when its value equals their average. Consequently $0\le v(a)\le 1$ at every vertex, consistent with $v(a)$ being a probability.

\subsection{Effective resistance and energy}

\begin{definition}[Effective resistance]\label{ch35:def-reff}
The \emph{effective resistance} $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)$ between $s$ and $t$ is the voltage drop $v(s)-v(t)$ required to drive one unit of current ($f=1$) from $s$ to $t$:
\[
  R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t) \;=\; v(s)-v(t), \qquad \text{where } \Lap v=\mathbf{1}_s-\mathbf{1}_t.
\]
\end{definition}

Effective resistance summarizes the entire circuit between $s$ and $t$ as a single equivalent resistor. Two circuit rules simplify series--parallel subnetworks: resistors in \emph{series} add ($R=r_1+r_2$), and resistors in \emph{parallel} (written $r_1\parallel r_2$) add reciprocally ($\frac1R=\frac1{r_1}+\frac1{r_2}$).

Effective resistance can also be written as a minimum-energy problem. Recall from Chapter~31 the Laplacian quadratic form $f^\top\Lap f=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}c_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2$, which equals the total electrical power dissipated (in watts, $\text{W}$). Orient each edge $e=(a,b)$ arbitrarily; a signed flow $i_e\in\R$ is positive along that orientation and negative against it, and its \emph{net outflow} at vertex $x$ is $\sum_{e=(x,b)}i_e - \sum_{e=(a,x)}i_e$.

\begin{theorem}[Thomson's principle]\label{ch35:thm-thomson}
Among all unit $s$--$t$ flows (functions $i$ on oriented edges with net outflow $1$ at $s$, $-1$ at $t$, and $0$ at every other vertex), the electrical current $i^\star$ is the unique minimizer of the dissipated energy
\[
  \mathcal{E}(i) \;=\; \sum_{e\in E} r_e\,i_e^2,
\]
and the minimum energy equals $\mathcal{E}(i^\star) = R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $i^\star$ be the electrical current driven by vertex voltages $v$, so Ohm's law gives $r_e i^\star_e=v(a)-v(b)$ on each oriented edge $e=(a,b)$. Any other unit $s$--$t$ flow takes the form $i=i^\star+\Delta$, where $\Delta = i - i^\star$ is a \emph{circulation} (a flow whose net outflow is $0$ at every vertex, including $s$ and $t$). Expanding $\mathcal{E}(i^\star+\Delta)$ gives
\[
  \mathcal{E}(i) \;=\; \mathcal{E}(i^\star)+2\sum_{e\in E} r_e\, i^\star_e\,\Delta_e+\mathcal{E}(\Delta).
\]
Substitute $r_e i^\star_e = v(a)-v(b)$ into the middle sum and regroup terms by vertex:
\[
  \sum_{e=(a,b)}\bigl(v(a)-v(b)\bigr)\Delta_e
  \;=\; \sum_{x\in V} v(x)\cdot(\text{net outflow of }\Delta\text{ at }x) \;=\; 0.
\]
Hence $\mathcal{E}(i)=\mathcal{E}(i^\star)+\mathcal{E}(\Delta)\ge\mathcal{E}(i^\star)$, with equality if and only if $\Delta=0$ because every $r_e>0$. Regrouping the same sum for $i^\star$ itself evaluates the minimum:
\begin{align*}
  \mathcal{E}(i^\star) &= \sum_{e=(a,b)} i^\star_e\bigl(v(a)-v(b)\bigr) \\
  &= \sum_{x\in V} v(x)\cdot(\text{net outflow of }i^\star\text{ at }x) \\
  &= v(s)\cdot 1 + v(t)\cdot(-1) \;=\; R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t). \qedhere
\end{align*}
\end{proof}

Thomson's principle pairs with \emph{Dirichlet's principle}: among all vertex functions $f$ with $f(s)=1$ and $f(t)=0$, the harmonic voltage $v$ minimizes $f^\top\Lap f = \sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}c_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2$, and the minimum equals the effective conductance $1/R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)$. Together they yield \emph{Rayleigh's monotonicity law}: deleting an edge restricts the set of feasible flows in Thomson's minimization and can only \emph{increase} $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)$, while \emph{shorting} two vertices together (merging them with a zero-resistance wire so they must share the same voltage) restricts the feasible potentials in Dirichlet's minimization and can only \emph{decrease} $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)$. Figure~\ref{fig:ch35-thomson} illustrates both Thomson's energy minimum and Rayleigh monotonicity on the triangle $\{1,2,3\}$.

  \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad

\subsection{Resistance via the Laplacian pseudoinverse}

Networks that are not series--parallel (such as a Wheatstone bridge or a grid) cannot be reduced by those two rules alone, so we express effective resistance directly through $\Lap$. Let $\Lap^{+}$ be the Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse of $\Lap$ from Chapter~31, which inverts $\Lap$ on the subspace $\mathbf{1}^\perp = \{x\in\R^n : \mathbf{1}^\top x = 0\}$ of vectors perpendicular to $\mathbf{1}$ and sends $\mathbf{1}$ to $0$. Solving $\Lap v=\mathbf{1}_s-\mathbf{1}_t$ on $\mathbf{1}^\perp$ gives $v=\Lap^{+}(\mathbf{1}_s-\mathbf{1}_t)$, and taking $v(s)-v(t)$ yields a closed-form expression.

\begin{theorem}[Resistance formula]\label{ch35:thm-reffform}
For any vertices $s,t\in V$ in a connected graph,
\[
  R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)
  \;=\;(\mathbf{1}_s-\mathbf{1}_t)^\top \Lap^{+}(\mathbf{1}_s-\mathbf{1}_t)
  \;=\;\Lap^{+}_{ss}+\Lap^{+}_{tt}-2\Lap^{+}_{st}.
\]
In terms of the Laplacian eigenvalues $0=\lambda_1<\lambda_2\le\cdots\le\lambda_n$ and an orthonormal eigenbasis $u_1,\dots,u_n$,
\[
  R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t) \;=\; \sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{1}{\lambda_k}\bigl(u_k(s)-u_k(t)\bigr)^2.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Set $\chi=\mathbf{1}_s-\mathbf{1}_t$. Its entries sum to $0$, so $\chi\in\mathbf{1}^\perp$ and $\Lap\Lap^+\chi=\chi$. Thus $v=\Lap^+\chi$ solves $\Lap v=\chi$, giving
\[
  R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t) \;=\; v(s)-v(t) \;=\; \chi^\top v \;=\; \chi^\top\Lap^+\chi \;=\; \Lap^{+}_{ss}+\Lap^{+}_{tt}-2\Lap^{+}_{st}.
\]
Substituting the spectral decomposition $\Lap^+=\sum_{k=2}^{n}\lambda_k^{-1}u_ku_k^\top$ and $\chi^\top u_k=u_k(s)-u_k(t)$ gives the eigenvalue sum.
\end{proof}

Because each term in the eigenvalue sum is weighted by $1/\lambda_k$, a small second eigenvalue $\lambda_2$ (indicating a sparse bottleneck cut by Cheeger's inequality, Chapter~32) creates large effective resistances across that bottleneck.

\begin{remark}[Resistance and Euclidean metrics]\label{ch35:rem-metric}
A \emph{metric} on $V$ is a distance function $d:V\times V\to\R_{\ge 0}$ that is zero if and only if its two arguments coincide, is symmetric ($d(s,t)=d(t,s)$), and obeys the triangle inequality $d(s,t)\le d(s,u)+d(u,t)$. Define the matrix square root $(\Lap^+)^{1/2} = \sum_{k=2}^n \lambda_k^{-1/2}u_k u_k^\top$, and map each vertex $s\in V$ to the vector $\psi(s)=(\Lap^+)^{1/2}\mathbf{1}_s\in\R^n$. Then
\[
  \norm{\psi(s)-\psi(t)}_2^2 \;=\; (\mathbf{1}_s-\mathbf{1}_t)^\top \Lap^+(\mathbf{1}_s-\mathbf{1}_t) \;=\; R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t),
\]
so $\sqrt{R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)}$ is the ordinary Euclidean distance between $\psi(s)$ and $\psi(t)$ (called the \emph{resistive embedding}) and is therefore a metric on $V$. In fact, $R_{\mathrm{eff}}$ itself also obeys the triangle inequality: Section~\ref{ch35:commute} shows $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)=C(s,t)/(2m)$, and a round trip between $s$ and $t$ takes no longer in expectation than a round trip forced to detour through $u$.
\end{remark}

\section{Commute time equals \texorpdfstring{$2m$}{2m} times resistance}\label{ch35:commute}

The next theorem connects the probabilistic round-trip time $C(s,t)$ to the electrical effective resistance $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)$ through a factor depending only on the edge count $m$.

\begin{theorem}[Chandra--Raghavan--Ruzzo--Smolensky--Tiwari]\label{ch35:thm-commute}
For every pair of vertices $s,t$ in a connected unweighted graph,
\[
  C(s,t)\;=\;H(s,t)+H(t,s)\;=\;2m\cdot R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t).
\]
For a weighted graph with conductances $c_{ij}$ and total vertex conductance $2W=\sum_{i\in V}c_i$, the constant $2m$ is replaced by $2W$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We superpose two electrical experiments (Figure~\ref{fig:ch35-superposition}). Fix a target $t$ and write $\phi_a=H(a,t)$ for the hitting time to $t$ from $a$. Since $\sum_j(\mathbf{P})_{aj}=1$, first-step analysis \eqref{ch35:eq-firststep} rewrites, for every $a\ne t$, as
\begin{align*}
  \phi_a = 1+\sum_{j\in V} (\mathbf{P})_{aj}\phi_j
  &\;\Longleftrightarrow\; \sum_{j\in V} (\mathbf{P})_{aj}(\phi_a-\phi_j) = 1 \\
  &\;\Longleftrightarrow\; \frac{1}{\deg(a)}\sum_{j:\{a,j\}\in E}(\phi_a-\phi_j) = 1.
\end{align*}
Multiplying by $\deg(a)$ gives $(\Lap\phi)_a = \sum_{j:\{a,j\}\in E}(\phi_a-\phi_j)=\deg(a)$ for every $a\ne t$, together with $\phi_t=0$. Thus $\phi$ solves $\Lap\phi=\mathbf{d}-\alpha\,\mathbf{1}_t$, where $\mathbf{d}=(\deg(1),\dots,\deg(n))^\top$ is the degree vector and $\alpha$ is the net outflow at $t$. Because every column of $\Lap$ sums to $0$, the entries of $\Lap\phi$ sum to $0$, forcing $\alpha=\sum_{a\in V}\deg(a)=2m$:
\[
  \Lap\,\phi \;=\; \mathbf{d}-2m\,\mathbf{1}_t,
  \qquad \phi_a=H(a,t).
\]
In electrical terms, $\phi$ is the voltage vector when $\deg(a)$ amperes are injected into every vertex $a$ and all $2m$ amperes are extracted at $t$. Repeating the same derivation for target $s$, the vector $\psi_a=H(a,s)$ solves $\Lap\psi=\mathbf{d}-2m\,\mathbf{1}_s$: inject $\deg(a)$ everywhere and extract $2m$ at $s$.

Subtract the second system from the first:
\[
  \Lap(\phi-\psi) \;=\; (\mathbf{d}-2m\,\mathbf{1}_t) - (\mathbf{d}-2m\,\mathbf{1}_s) \;=\; 2m\,(\mathbf{1}_s-\mathbf{1}_t).
\]
Every $\deg(a)$ injection cancels out, leaving a single $2m$-ampere current injected at $s$ and extracted at $t$. By linearity and Definition~\ref{ch35:def-reff}, the voltage drop of $\phi-\psi$ from $s$ to $t$ equals $2m\,R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)$. Evaluating that voltage drop directly from $\phi_a=H(a,t)$ and $\psi_a=H(a,s)$ gives
\begin{align*}
  (\phi-\psi)_s-(\phi-\psi)_t
  &= \bigl(\underbrace{H(s,t)}_{\phi_s}-\underbrace{H(s,s)}_{\psi_s=0}\bigr)
   -\bigl(\underbrace{H(t,t)}_{\phi_t=0}-\underbrace{H(t,s)}_{\psi_t}\bigr) \\
  &= H(s,t)+H(t,s) \\
  &= C(s,t),
\end{align*}
so $C(s,t)=2m\,R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Foster's theorem: a global resistance identity}

Summing effective resistances over all edges of a connected graph produces $n-1$, regardless of how many edges the graph has or how they are arranged.

\begin{theorem}[Foster]\label{ch35:thm-foster}
In any connected unweighted graph on $n$ vertices,
\[
  \sum_{\{i,j\}\in E} R_{\mathrm{eff}}(i,j)\;=\;n-1.
\]
More generally, for positive edge conductances $c_e$, $\sum_{e\in E} c_e\,R_{\mathrm{eff}}(e) = n-1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Orient each edge $e=(i,j)$ arbitrarily and recall from Chapter~31 the $n\times m$ signed vertex--edge incidence matrix $\mathbf{B}$, whose $e$-th column is $\mathbf{1}_i-\mathbf{1}_j$ and which factors the unweighted Laplacian as $\Lap=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^\top$. By Theorem~\ref{ch35:thm-reffform}, $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(e)=(\mathbf{1}_i-\mathbf{1}_j)^\top\Lap^+(\mathbf{1}_i-\mathbf{1}_j)=\bigl(\mathbf{B}^\top\Lap^+\mathbf{B}\bigr)_{ee}$, the $e$-th diagonal entry of $\mathbf{B}^\top\Lap^+\mathbf{B}$. Summing over all $m$ edges gives the matrix trace $\operatorname{tr}$ (the sum of diagonal entries). Using the cyclic trace identity $\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y})=\sum_{p,q}X_{pq}Y_{qp}=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X})$,
\begin{align*}
  \sum_{e\in E} R_{\mathrm{eff}}(e) &= \operatorname{tr}\bigl(\mathbf{B}^\top\Lap^+\mathbf{B}\bigr) \\
  &= \operatorname{tr}\bigl(\Lap^+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^\top\bigr) && \text{(cyclic property of trace)} \\
  &= \operatorname{tr}(\Lap^+\Lap) && \text{($\Lap=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^\top$)} \\
  &= \rank(\Lap) \\
  &= n-1,
\end{align*}
because on a connected graph $\Lap^+\Lap$ is the orthogonal projection onto the $(n-1)$-dimensional subspace $\mathbf{1}^\perp$ (it has one eigenvalue $0$ along $\mathbf{1}$ and $n-1$ eigenvalues equal to $1$). For weighted edges with $\mathbf{W}=\operatorname{diag}(c_e)$, replacing $\mathbf{B}$ by $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{W}^{1/2}$ gives $\sum_e c_e R_{\mathrm{eff}}(e)=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\mathbf{B}^\top\Lap^+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{W}^{1/2})=n-1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Foster's theorem and spanning trees on $\Gph$]\label{ch35:ex-foster}

\[
  \frac23 + \frac23 + \frac23 + 1 + 1 \;=\; 2 + 2 \;=\; 4 \;=\; n-1.
\]

Foster's theorem underlies \emph{spectral sparsification}. Spielman and Srivastava showed that sampling $\Oh(n\log n/\eps^2)$ edges with probability proportional to $w_e R_{\mathrm{eff}}(e)$ (normalized by their sum $\sum_e w_e R_{\mathrm{eff}}(e)=n-1$ from Foster's theorem) and rescaling weights produces a sparse graph whose Laplacian quadratic form matches the original within $1\pm\eps$ on every vector. Those effective-resistance sparsifiers drive the nearly linear Laplacian solvers of Chapter~41. The companion \emph{Probability} volume develops first-step analysis on general Markov chains; here reversibility turns those systems into symmetric Laplacian equations.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Computing the quantities: an algorithm}

Every quantity introduced so far reduces to solving Laplacian linear systems. Algorithm~1 collects the pipeline for effective resistance, commute time, and single-target hitting times.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\DontPrintSemicolon
\caption{Random-walk quantities via Laplacian solves}
\KwIn{Connected graph $\Gph=(V,E)$ with conductances $c_{ij}>0$; query vertices $s,t$.}
\KwOut{$R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)$, $C(s,t)$, and hitting times $H(\cdot,t)$.}
Assemble $\Lap=\mathbf{D}-\Adjm$ with $(\Adjm)_{ij}=c_{ij}$ and $\mathbf{D}=\operatorname{diag}(c_1,\dots,c_n)$\;
$2W \leftarrow \sum_{i\in V} c_i$ \tcp*{$=2m$ in the unit-resistor case}
\tcp{Effective resistance: one Laplacian solve}
Solve $\Lap\,v=\mathbf{1}_s-\mathbf{1}_t$ (fix the constant shift via $v_t=0$ or $v\perp\mathbf{1}$)\;
$R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)\leftarrow v(s)-v(t)$\;
$C(s,t)\leftarrow 2W\cdot R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)$\;
\tcp{Hitting times to fixed target $t$: one Laplacian solve}
Solve $\Lap\,\phi=\mathbf{d}-2W\,\mathbf{1}_t$ with $\phi_t=0$ \tcp*{$\mathbf{d}=(c_1,\dots,c_n)^\top$}
\For{each $a\in V$}{$H(a,t)\leftarrow \phi_a$\;}
$H(s,t)\leftarrow \phi_s$;\quad $H(t,s)\leftarrow C(s,t)-H(s,t)$\;
\Return $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t),\,C(s,t),\,\phi$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{whybox}
Why does solving $\Lap\phi=\mathbf{d}-2W\mathbf{1}_t$ give \emph{all} $n$ hitting times to $t$ at once? Because Theorem~\ref{ch35:thm-commute} showed that $H(\cdot,t)$ is the voltage vector of the ``inject $c_a$ everywhere, drain $2W$ at $t$'' circuit. For \emph{all-pairs} commute times, one computes the full pseudoinverse $\Lap^+$ ($\Oh(n^3)$ time or $n$ solves) and reads off $C(s,t)=2W\bigl(\Lap^+_{ss}+\Lap^+_{tt}-2\Lap^+_{st}\bigr)$ in $\Oh(1)$ time per pair.
\end{whybox}

\begin{complexitybox}
\textbf{Cost.} One Laplacian solve takes $\Oh(n^3)$ time by dense Gaussian elimination, roughly $\Oh(m^{1.5})$ by conjugate gradient with a \emph{preconditioner} (an easily inverted approximate matrix that reduces the iteration count), or $\tilde\Oh(m)=\Oh\bigl(m\log^{c}n\,\log(1/\eps)\bigr)$ by the Spielman--Teng and Koutis--Miller--Peng nearly linear solvers (Chapter~41). A single $(s,t)$ query uses two solves, $\tilde\Oh(m)$. Approximating \emph{all} pairwise resistances within $1\pm\eps$ avoids computing $\Lap^+$: projecting the $m$-dimensional edge-resistance vectors onto $\Oh(\log n/\eps^2)$ random directions (the \emph{Johnson--Lindenstrauss} random-projection method, which preserves pairwise Euclidean distances within $1\pm\eps$) needs only $\Oh(\log n/\eps^2)$ Laplacian solves, or $\tilde\Oh(m/\eps^2)$ time total.
\end{complexitybox}

\section{Mixing time and the spectral gap}\label{ch35:mixing}

How many steps does it take for $p_t$ to approach the stationary distribution $\pi$? We measure the discrepancy between two probability distributions $p$ and $\pi$ on $V$ by their \emph{total variation distance}:
\[
  \norm{p-\pi}_{\mathrm{TV}} \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\norm{p-\pi}_1 \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in V}\abs{p_i-\pi_i} \;=\; \max_{S\subseteq V}\abs[\Big]{\sum_{i\in S} p_i - \sum_{i\in S}\pi_i},
\]
the largest difference in probability that $p$ and $\pi$ assign to any subset of vertices $S\subseteq V$. For a target tolerance $\eps>0$, the \emph{mixing time} $t_{\mathrm{mix}}(\eps)=\min\set{t\ge 0 : \max_{X_0}\norm{p_t-\pi}_{\mathrm{TV}}\le\eps}$ is the step count needed from the worst starting vertex to get within $\eps$ of stationarity.

For a reversible walk, convergence is controlled by the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{D}^{-1}\Adjm$. Writing $\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}=\operatorname{diag}(\deg(1)^{-1/2},\dots,\deg(n)^{-1/2})$, the similarity transform $\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}=\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\Adjm\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}=\mathbf{I}-\mathcal{L}$ relates $\mathbf{P}$ to the symmetric normalized Laplacian $\mathcal{L}$ of Chapter~32, so $\mathbf{P}$ has $n$ real eigenvalues in $[-1,1]$.

\begin{theorem}[Spectral mixing bound]\label{ch35:thm-mixing}
Let $\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{D}^{-1}\Adjm$ on a connected graph have eigenvalues $1=\mu_1>\mu_2\ge\cdots\ge\mu_n\ge-1$. Set $\mu_\star=\max(\mu_2,\abs{\mu_n})$ (the second-largest eigenvalue in absolute value) and $\pi_{\min}=\min_{i\in V}\pi_i$. For every starting vertex and every $t\ge 0$,
\[
  \norm{p_t-\pi}_{\mathrm{TV}}\;\le\;\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{1-\pi_{\min}}{\pi_{\min}}}\;\mu_\star^{\,t},
  \qquad
  t_{\mathrm{mix}}(\eps)\;=\;\Oh\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{1-\mu_\star}\log\frac{1}{\pi_{\min}\eps}\Bigr).
\]
On a bipartite graph $\mu_n=-1$, so $\mu_\star=1$ and the non-lazy walk fails to mix. Running the lazy walk $\mathbf{P}'=\tfrac12(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{P})$ shifts every eigenvalue to $\tfrac12(1+\mu_k)\in[0,1]$, replacing $\mu_\star$ by $\tfrac12(1+\mu_2)$ and giving
\[
  t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\mathrm{lazy}}(\eps) \;=\; \Oh\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{1-\mu_2}\log\frac{1}{\pi_{\min}\eps}\Bigr).
\]
The quantity $1-\mu_2$ is the \emph{spectral gap}; a larger gap produces faster mixing.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Idea]
Because $\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}$ is symmetric, it admits an orthonormal eigenbasis. Expanding the degree-rescaled start vector in that basis separates the stationary component (eigenvalue $\mu_1=1$, unchanged at every step) from the $n-1$ orthogonal components, each of which shrinks by $\abs{\mu_k}\le\mu_\star$ per step. Converting the weighted $\ell_2$ norm to $\frac{1}{2}\norm{\cdot}_1=\norm{\cdot}_{\mathrm{TV}}$ via the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality introduces the factor $\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(1-\pi_{\min})/\pi_{\min}}$. Under the lazy walk $\mathbf{P}'=\tfrac12(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{P})$, negative eigenvalues near $-1$ map near $0$, so the slowest decaying non-stationary mode has eigenvalue $\tfrac12(1+\mu_2)=1-\tfrac12(1-\mu_2)$, halving the spectral gap while eliminating bipartite oscillation (Figure~\ref{fig:ch35-mixing}).
\end{proof}

\section{Historical Context and Controversies}

\begin{historybox}
The connection between random walks and electrical networks has a long history in applied mathematics. In 1847, Gustav Kirchhoff formulated his laws of electrical circuits alongside the matrix--tree theorem (which counts spanning trees via determinants of Laplacian submatrices), showing that the distribution of current through a network of wires can be expressed in terms of spanning trees. Thirty years later, Lord Rayleigh established his monotonicity principle in acoustics and electricity: pinching a wire or adding parallel pathways can never increase the effective resistance between two terminals.

The probabilistic half developed in parallel. In 1921, George P\'olya asked whether a particle walking at random on the $d$-dimensional integer grid $\Z^d$ (where each point has $2d$ nearest neighbors) returns to its starting point. He proved that random walks on a line ($d=1$) or plane ($d=2$) return with probability $1$, whereas in dimensions $d\ge 3$ the walker escapes to infinity with positive probability. In 1944, Shizuo Kakutani linked Brownian motion (the continuous-time limit of random walks) to continuous Dirichlet problems, and in 1984 Peter Doyle and J.~Laurie Snell made the circuit proof of P\'olya's theorem elementary: recurrence holds in dimensions $d \le 2$ because the infinite $d$-dimensional unit-resistor grid has infinite effective resistance to infinity for $d\le 2$, and finite resistance for $d \ge 3$.

Early algorithmic work ran into two obstacles. First, computing hitting times by summing geometric series over cycles becomes intractable on general graphs; solving the single linear system $\Lap \phi = \mathbf{d}-2m\,\mathbf{1}_t$ avoids path enumeration altogether. Second, hitting times violate symmetry: on an $n$-vertex lollipop graph, walking down the stem into the clique takes $\Theta(n^2)$ steps, whereas escaping the clique out to the stem tip takes $\Theta(n^3)$ steps. In 1989, Ashok Chandra, Prabhakar Raghavan, Walter Ruzzo, Roman Smolensky, and Prasoon Tiwari proved the commute-time identity $C(s,t) = 2m\,R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)$, showing that summing both directions restores the symmetry of physical resistance and bounds the time needed to test undirected connectivity in randomized logarithmic space ($\Oh(\log n)$ bits of memory). An active research problem is local estimation of hitting and commute times on massive directed networks where detailed balance breaks down.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{What We Can Now Do and Its Limitations}
\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{What these tools give us}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Exact hitting times, escape probabilities, and commute times on any connected undirected graph reduce to solving linear systems in the Laplacian matrix $\Lap$.
  \item Foster's theorem gives a structural invariant: edge effective resistances across any connected $n$-vertex graph sum to $n-1$, so edge resistances cannot all be large simultaneously.
  \item Effective resistance induces a Euclidean geometry: $\sqrt{R_{\mathrm{eff}}(u,v)}$ satisfies the triangle inequality and embeds vertices into $\R^n$ so that distance reflects multi-path connectivity.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Limitations}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Direct solver cost}: Computing exact resistances by dense Gaussian elimination on $\Lap v = \mathbf{1}_s - \mathbf{1}_t$ costs $\Oh(n^3)$ operations, which is infeasible on graphs with millions of vertices (approximate solvers in Chapter~41 reduce single-pair queries to $\tilde\Oh(m)$ time).
  \item \emph{Failure of reversibility on directed graphs}: The resistor-network dictionary requires detailed balance, $\pi_i (\mathbf{P})_{ij} = \pi_j (\mathbf{P})_{ji}$. On directed graphs, one-way transitions create non-zero probability circulations at stationarity; ordinary passive resistors cannot represent one-way flow without active or directional components such as diodes (one-way circuit elements), so voltages no longer match hitting probabilities.
  \item \emph{Global sensitivity}: Effective resistance depends on the whole graph. Adding a single low-resistance shortcut edge between two distant regions can change $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)$ for vertex pairs far from the new edge.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Where We Go Next}:
The electrical tools of this chapter rely on two-way edges and detailed balance. In \textbf{Chapter~36 (PageRank and Markov Chains)}, we move to directed, non-reversible networks: to rank web pages connected by one-way hyperlinks, we combine Markov chains with random teleportation, the Perron--Frobenius theorem, and power iteration. Later, Chapter~41 returns to undirected resistor networks to solve Laplacian systems in nearly linear time.
\end{connectionbox}

\section*{Exercises}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Stationary by hand]
Take the path graph $P_4$ on vertices $1\!-\!2\!-\!3\!-\!4$. Write $\mathbf{P}$, find $\pi$ from $\deg/(2m)$, and verify $\pi\mathbf{P}=\pi$ directly. Why does the non-lazy walk fail to converge from $X_0=1$? Identify the period.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Hitting times on a cycle]
For the $n$-cycle $C_n$, prove by symmetry and first-step analysis that $H(i,j)=d(n-d)$ where $d$ is the shortest-path distance from $i$ to $j$. Conclude $C(i,j)=2H(i,j)=2d(n-d)$ (reaching $n^2/2$ for even $n$ when $i$ and $j$ are \emph{antipodal}, meaning diametrically opposite at distance $d=n/2$), and check that this matches $2m\,R_{\mathrm{eff}}(i,j)$ with $m=n$ and $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(i,j)=\frac{d(n-d)}{n}$ (two arcs in parallel).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The lollipop is worst]
The lollipop $L_n$ is a clique $K_{n/2}$ with a path of length $n/2$ attached at one clique vertex $u$, ending at $w$ (assume $n$ even). Show $H(w,u)=\Th(n^2)$ along the path, whereas $H(u,w)=\Th(n^3)$. Conclude the $\Th(n^3)$ worst-case hitting time and the $\Th(n)$ ratio between the two directions.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Resistance is a metric]
Using $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)=\chi^\top\Lap^+\chi$ with $\chi=\mathbf{1}_s-\mathbf{1}_t$, prove $\sqrt{R_{\mathrm{eff}}}$ is a metric: show $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)=\norm{(\Lap^+)^{1/2}\mathbf{1}_s-(\Lap^+)^{1/2}\mathbf{1}_t}_2^2$ and apply the Euclidean triangle inequality. Where is connectivity of $\Gph$ used?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Rayleigh monotonicity]
Prove from Thomson's principle that adding an edge to $\Gph$ cannot increase $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)$ for any $s,t$, and that increasing any edge's resistance cannot decrease $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)$ (minimizing over a larger set of flows, or with smaller edge resistances, cannot raise the minimum). Deduce that for any \emph{spanning subgraph} $H\subseteq\Gph$ (a subgraph on the same vertex set $V$ keeping a subset of edges), $R^{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)\ge R^{\Gph}_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Foster, twice]
Verify Foster's theorem on (a) the complete graph $K_4$ and (b) the star $K_{1,3}$ (one central vertex joined to three leaves). For $K_n$, show every edge has $R_{\mathrm{eff}}=2/n$ and check $\binom n2\cdot\frac2n=n-1$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Commute time from the pseudoinverse]
Compute $\Lap^+$ explicitly for the triangle $K_3$, where $\Lap = 3\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{J}$ and $\mathbf{J}$ is the $3\times 3$ all-ones matrix (a \emph{circulant} matrix, in which each row shifts the previous row one position to the right). Read off $C(1,2)=2m(\Lap^+_{11}+\Lap^+_{22}-2\Lap^+_{12})$ and check against $2m\,R_{\mathrm{eff}}(1,2)$ with $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(1,2)=2/3$ and $m=3$; both give $C(1,2)=4$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Harmonic = hitting probability, coded]
Implement the Dirichlet solve: given a connected graph and two vertices $s\ne t$, set $v(s)=1,v(t)=0$ and solve $(\Lap v)(a)=0$ at interior vertices $a$. Then estimate the same hitting probabilities by \emph{Monte Carlo simulation} (averaging the fraction of walks reaching $s$ before $t$ over many independent random trials). Plot solver values against empirical frequencies and verify they agree within sampling error.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Spectral gap and a barbell]
The barbell graph consists of two cliques $K_{n/2}$ joined by a single bridge edge. Argue that $\mu_2$ is close to $1$, so mixing is slow and the commute time across the bridge is $\Th(n^2)$. Relate this to Cheeger's inequality: the single bridge edge gives conductance $\Phi=\Th(1/n^2)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Weighted walks]
Generalize Theorem~\ref{ch35:thm-commute} to a weighted graph with conductances $c_{ij}>0$: define the walk with $\Pr[X_{t+1}=j\mid X_t=i]=c_{ij}/c_i$, set $2W=\sum_i c_i$, and prove $C(s,t)=2W\,R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)$ by repeating the two-experiment subtraction. Identify where reversibility (detailed balance) is used.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Cover time of the path]
Show the cover time of the path $P_n$ is $\Th(n^2)$ (reaching both endpoints from any start takes $\Th(n^2)$ steps). Compare this with the Matthews upper bound $\Oh(n^2\log n)$ obtained from $H_{\max}=\Th(n^2)$, and explain why the $\log n$ factor is slack on $P_n$ whereas on $K_n$ ($\operatorname{cov}(K_n)=\Th(n\log n)$) it is tight.
\end{exercise}
