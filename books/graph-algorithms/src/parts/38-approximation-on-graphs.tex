\chapter{Approximation Algorithms on Graphs}
\label{ch:approximation}

Vertex cover, the traveling salesman problem, max-cut, and Steiner tree are all \textsf{NP}-hard
(Chapter~37), so unless $\textsf{P}=\textsf{NP}$ no polynomial-time algorithm always finds an optimal
solution. Giving up exactness, however, does not mean giving up guarantees. For many \textsf{NP}-hard
problems, polynomial-time algorithms return a solution provably within a small factor of optimal: a
vertex cover at most twice the minimum, a tour at most $3/2$ the shortest, or a cut capturing at least
$87.8\%$ of the maximum crossing edges. Such procedures are \emph{approximation algorithms}, and their
guarantees are worst-case theorems rather than empirical observations.

How can we prove our answer is close to an optimum we cannot compute? Every algorithm in this chapter
solves that puzzle the same way: it constructs a benchmark we \emph{can} compute in polynomial time ---
a lower bound on the minimum cost, or an upper bound on the maximum value --- and bounds the gap
between that benchmark and the algorithm's output. As the problems grow harder, the benchmarks progress
from combinatorial objects (matchings and spanning trees) to continuous relaxations (linear and vector
programs).

\section{What an approximation guarantee means}

\begin{definition}[Approximation ratio]\label{ch38:def-ratio}
Let $\Pi$ be an optimization problem and let $\mathrm{OPT}(I) > 0$ denote the optimal objective value on
instance $I$. A polynomial-time algorithm $\mathcal{A}$ is an \emph{$\alpha$-approximation} if for
every instance $I$ it returns a feasible solution (one satisfying all constraints of $\Pi$) of value $\mathcal{A}(I)$ with
\[
  \frac{\mathcal{A}(I)}{\mathrm{OPT}(I)} \le \alpha \quad\text{(minimization, } \alpha\ge 1\text{)},
  \qquad
  \frac{\mathcal{A}(I)}{\mathrm{OPT}(I)} \ge \alpha \quad\text{(maximization, } \alpha\le 1\text{)}.
\]
We call $\alpha$ the \emph{approximation ratio}. A family of algorithms parameterized by an error tolerance $\eps>0$
giving ratio $1+\eps$ (or $1-\eps$ for maximization) in time polynomial in the input size $n$ for each fixed $\eps$ is a
\emph{polynomial-time approximation scheme} (PTAS), such as $\Oh(n^{1/\eps})$; if the running time is also polynomial in
$1/\eps$, such as $\Oh(n^2/\eps^2)$, it is a \emph{fully polynomial-time approximation scheme} (FPTAS).
\end{definition}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Because $\mathrm{OPT}(I)$ is \textsf{NP}-hard to compute, we cannot compare $\mathcal{A}(I)$ to it directly.
For a minimization problem, we instead identify a computable \emph{lower bound} $L(I) \le \mathrm{OPT}(I)$
and sandwich the unknown optimum inside the interval $[L(I),\, \alpha L(I)]$:
\[
  L(I) \;\le\; \mathrm{OPT}(I) \;\le\; \mathcal{A}(I) \;\le\; \alpha\cdot L(I).
\]
Proving $L(I) \le \mathrm{OPT}(I)$ and $\mathcal{A}(I) \le \alpha L(I)$ immediately yields
$\mathcal{A}(I) \le \alpha\,\mathrm{OPT}(I)$. Symmetrically, a maximization algorithm uses a computable
\emph{upper bound} $U(I) \ge \mathrm{OPT}(I)$ and proves $\mathcal{A}(I) \ge \alpha\, U(I)$, as shown
in Figure~\ref{ch38:fig-sandwich}. Choosing the right benchmark $L$ or $U$ --- the size of a matching,
the weight of a spanning tree, an eigenvalue, or the value of a continuous relaxation --- is the main
task in designing an approximation algorithm.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{marginnotebox}
Conventions for maximization ratios vary across the literature: some authors write ratios greater than $1$ for both minimization and maximization by taking $\mathrm{OPT}(I)/\mathcal{A}(I) \le \alpha$, calling a half-optimal cut a ``$2$-approximation.'' We keep $\mathcal{A}(I)/\mathrm{OPT}(I)$ throughout, so minimization ratios satisfy $\alpha \ge 1$ (smaller is better) and maximization ratios satisfy $\alpha \le 1$ (closer to $1$ is better).
\end{marginnotebox}

\section{Vertex cover: a 2-approximation three ways}
\label{ch38:sec-vc}

Recall the problem. Given an undirected graph $\Gph=(V,E)$, a \emph{vertex cover} is a set
$S\subseteq V$ touching every edge: for each $\{u,v\}\in E$, at least one of $u,v$ lies in $S$. We
want the smallest such $S$. Deciding whether a cover of size $\le k$ exists is \textsf{NP}-complete.

\subsection{The maximal-matching algorithm}

The simplest $2$-approximation makes no direct reference to the optimum. Take \emph{any} maximal
matching --- a set of edges sharing no endpoints that cannot be extended by adding another edge of $E$
(unlike a \emph{maximum} matching, which has the largest possible size) --- and output both endpoints
of every matched edge.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{VertexCover-Matching}$(\Gph=(V,E))$}\label{ch38:alg-vc-match}
\DontPrintSemicolon
$M \gets \emptyset$;\quad $S \gets \emptyset$\;
\ForEach{edge $\{u,v\}\in E$ in arbitrary order}{
  \If{$u\notin S$ \textbf{and} $v\notin S$}{
    $M \gets M\cup\{\{u,v\}\}$\;
    $S \gets S\cup\{u,v\}$\;
  }
}
\Return $S$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Feasibility.} Suppose some edge $\{x,y\}$ were uncovered, meaning $x\notin S$ and $y\notin S$.
When the loop reached $\{x,y\}$, neither endpoint belonged to $S$, so the algorithm would have added
$\{x,y\}$ to $M$ and placed both $x$ and $y$ into $S$, a contradiction. Thus $S$ covers every edge.

\textbf{The lower bound.} The matching $M$ consists of vertex-disjoint edges. Every vertex cover must
contain at least one endpoint from each matched edge (otherwise that edge goes uncovered), and because
the edges of $M$ share no vertices, those chosen endpoints are all distinct. Hence
\[
  \mathrm{OPT} \;\ge\; \abs{M}.
\]
Setting $L=\abs{M}$ gives our computable lower bound. Since each edge in $M$ contributes two new
vertices to $S$, we have $\abs{S}=2\abs{M}$, and therefore
\[
  \abs{S} \;=\; 2\abs{M} \;\le\; 2\,\mathrm{OPT}.
\]
\end{whybox}

\begin{theorem}\label{ch38:thm-vc2}
Algorithm~\ref{ch38:alg-vc-match} runs in $\Oh(\abs V+\abs E)$ time and returns a vertex cover of size
at most $2\,\mathrm{OPT}$.
\end{theorem}

The running time is one pass over the edges with $\Oh(1)$ membership tests using a boolean array indexed by $V$.

\begin{trapbox}
A tempting heuristic is to pick a \emph{highest-degree} vertex, add it to the cover, delete it and its
incident edges, and repeat. Despite looking smarter than blind matching, greedy-by-degree is \emph{not}
a constant-factor approximation: on bipartite graphs of $n$ vertices it can return a cover of size
$\Th(\log n)\cdot\mathrm{OPT}$ (Figure~\ref{ch38:fig-vc-greedy-trap}). Maximal matching succeeds because
the disjoint edges certify $\mathrm{OPT} \ge \abs{M}$; picking high-degree vertices supplies no lower bound.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{The LP relaxation and rounding}

The second route writes the lower bound as a \emph{linear program} (LP) --- optimizing a linear objective
subject to linear inequalities over continuous variables --- and extends to weighted vertex cover, where
each vertex $v$ has cost $w_v\ge 0$ and we minimize total cost $\sum_{v\in S} w_v$. Introduce a $0/1$
decision variable $x_v$ indicating whether $v$ is in the cover to obtain an \emph{integer program} (IP):
\[
  \textbf{(IP)}\quad \min \sum_{v\in V} w_v x_v
  \quad\text{subject to}\quad x_u + x_v \ge 1 \text{ for all } \{u,v\}\in E,\quad x_v\in\{0,1\}.
\]
Replacing the discrete constraint $x_v\in\{0,1\}$ with the continuous interval $0 \le x_v \le 1$ produces
the \emph{LP relaxation}. Because every $0/1$ solution also lies in $[0,1]^V$, the LP minimizes over a
larger feasible set, so its optimum satisfies $\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{LP}}\le\mathrm{OPT}$. Solve the LP
in polynomial time to get an optimal fractional vector $x$, then \emph{round} at threshold $\tfrac12$:
put $v$ into $S$ if and only if $x_v\ge \tfrac12$ (Figure~\ref{ch38:fig-vc-lp-round}).

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Feasibility.} For every edge $\{u,v\}\in E$, the constraint $x_u+x_v\ge 1$ forces
$\max(x_u,x_v)\ge \tfrac12$, so at least one endpoint is placed in $S$. Every edge is covered.

\textbf{Ratio.} Every selected vertex $v\in S$ satisfies $x_v\ge\tfrac12$, which implies $1 \le 2x_v$.
Summing weights over $S$ gives
\begin{align*}
  \sum_{v\in S} w_v &\le \sum_{v\in S} w_v\,(2x_v) && \text{(since $x_v \ge \tfrac12$ for all $v\in S$)} \\
  &\le 2\sum_{v\in V} w_v x_v && \text{(since $w_v x_v \ge 0$ for all $v\notin S$)} \\
  &= 2\,\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{LP}} \;\le\; 2\,\mathrm{OPT}. && \text{(LP relaxation lower-bounds IP)} \qquad\blacksquare
\end{align*}
\end{whybox}

This \emph{relax-and-round} pattern recurs throughout approximation: solve a tractable continuous
relaxation, then round the fractional vector to a discrete solution while bounding the cost increase
(here at most the reciprocal $1/\tfrac12 = 2$ of the rounding threshold). The worst-case ratio
$\mathrm{OPT}/\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{LP}}$ across all instances is called the \emph{integrality gap} of
the relaxation. On a unit-weight clique $K_n$, setting every $x_v = \tfrac12$ costs $n/2$ while any
integer cover needs $n-1$ vertices, so this LP has integrality gap $2 - 2/n \to 2$: no rounding scheme
using this LP alone can prove a constant smaller than $2$.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Half-integrality (Nemhauser--Trotter).} As proved in Chapter~29, the vertex-cover LP always has
an optimal solution with every $x_v\in\{0,\tfrac12,1\}$ (even with nonnegative vertex weights). Vertices
with $x_v=1$ belong to some optimal integral cover and vertices with $x_v=0$ can be discarded, leaving
only the $\tfrac12$-valued odd-cycle core; this reduction forms the Nemhauser--Trotter preprocessing
kernel from Chapter~25 (shrinking an instance in polynomial time to an equivalent smaller core).
\end{focusbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Every minimization LP has a companion maximization LP called its \emph{dual}, whose feasible values
all lower-bound the primal optimum and match it at optimality. For weighted vertex cover, the dual
assigns a nonnegative variable $y_e\ge 0$ to each edge $e\in E$ to maximize $\sum_{e\in E} y_e$ subject to
$\sum_{e\ni v} y_e\le w_v$ for every vertex $v$ (summing over all edges $e$ incident to $v$). On unit
weights ($w_v=1$), this is \emph{fractional matching}, and on bipartite graphs both LPs are integral
(K\H{o}nig's theorem, Chapter~15), making vertex cover solvable exactly.

This dual also yields the \emph{third} $2$-approximation --- the \emph{primal-dual method}, which avoids
solving an LP at all. Start with $y_e=0$ and $S=\emptyset$; while some edge $e=\{u,v\}$ is uncovered,
raise $y_e$ until at least one endpoint's budget goes \emph{tight} ($\sum_{e'\ni v} y_{e'} = w_v$), and
add every tight endpoint to $S$. Every chosen vertex pays $w_v = \sum_{e\ni v} y_e$, and each edge $e$ has
two endpoints, so $\sum_{v\in S} w_v \le 2\sum_{e\in E} y_e \le 2\,\mathrm{OPT}$ (Exercise~\ref{ch38:ex-primal-dual-vc}).
For unit weights, raising $y_e$ from $0$ to $1$ on uncovered edges reduces directly to Algorithm~\ref{ch38:alg-vc-match}.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{marginnotebox}
Whether any polynomial-time algorithm beats ratio $2-\eps$ for vertex cover is open. Under the
\emph{Unique Games Conjecture} (UGC) --- a complexity hypothesis proposed by Subhash Khot in 2002 stating
that it is \textsf{NP}-hard to distinguish nearly satisfiable from almost completely unsatisfiable
systems of two-variable permutation constraints --- Khot and Regev (2008) proved vertex cover is
\textsf{NP}-hard to approximate within $2-\eps$ for every $\eps>0$. Unconditionally (assuming only
$\textsf{P}\neq\textsf{NP}$), Dinur and Safra (2005) proved $1.3606$-hardness, later improved to
$\sqrt{2}-\eps$ by Khot, Minzer, and Safra (2018).
\end{marginnotebox}

\section{Set cover and the greedy $\ln n$ bound}

Vertex cover is a special case of \emph{set cover}. Given a ground set (universe) $U$ of $n = \abs{U}$
elements and a collection $\mathcal{S}=\{S_1,\dots,S_m\}$ of subsets of $U$ with positive costs $c_i > 0$
(or unit costs $c_i = 1$) whose union equals $U$, we want a subcollection covering all of $U$ at minimum
total cost. Taking $U = E$ and letting $S_v$ be the set of edges incident to vertex $v$ recovers vertex cover.

\begin{intuitionbox}
The greedy rule repeatedly buys the set that covers the most still-uncovered elements per unit of cost,
stopping once every element is covered. To compare greedy against an unknown optimal cover, we use a
\emph{charging argument}: divide each chosen set's cost $c_i$ equally among the newly covered elements
it pays for, then bound the total price paid inside each set of an optimal cover by the \emph{harmonic
series} $H_k = 1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \dots + \tfrac1k \le \ln k + 1$ (Figure~\ref{ch38:fig-setcover-harmonic}).
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{GreedySetCover}$(U,\mathcal{S},c)$}\label{ch38:alg-setcover}
\DontPrintSemicolon
$C \gets \emptyset$;\quad $R \gets U$ \tcp*{$R$ = uncovered elements}
\While{$R \neq \emptyset$}{
  pick $S_i$ maximizing the coverage rate $\dfrac{\abs{S_i\cap R}}{c_i}$\;
  \ForEach{$e \in S_i\cap R$}{ $\operatorname{price}(e) \gets \dfrac{c_i}{\abs{S_i\cap R}}$ }
  $C \gets C\cup\{i\}$;\quad $R \gets R\setminus S_i$\;
}
\Return $C$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}\label{ch38:thm-greedy-setcover}
\textup{\textsc{GreedySetCover}} returns a cover of cost at most $H_n\cdot\mathrm{OPT}$, where
$H_n=\sum_{k=1}^n \tfrac1k \le \ln n + 1$ is the $n$th harmonic number.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
Each time greedy picks a set $S_i$, its cost $c_i$ is distributed evenly across the $\abs{S_i\cap R}$
elements covered for the first time, so the algorithm's total cost equals $\sum_{e\in U}\operatorname{price}(e)$.
Writing $c(S)$ for the cost of a set $S\in\mathcal{S}$, fix an optimal cover $\mathcal{O}$ of total cost
$\sum_{S\in\mathcal{O}} c(S) = \mathrm{OPT}$.

Take any set $S\in\mathcal{O}$ of size $k=\abs{S}$, and list its elements $e_1,e_2,\dots,e_k$ in the
order greedy covers them (breaking ties in the order they are charged). Right before $e_j$ is covered,
all $k-j+1$ elements $e_j,e_{j+1},\dots,e_k$ still lie in the uncovered set $R$. Hence $S$ itself is a
candidate covering $\abs{S\cap R} \ge k-j+1$ uncovered elements at cost per element at most $c(S)/(k-j+1)$.
Because greedy picks the set with minimum cost per newly covered element,
\[
  \operatorname{price}(e_j) \;\le\; \frac{c(S)}{k-j+1}.
\]
Summing over $j=1,\dots,k$ gives
\[
  \sum_{e\in S}\operatorname{price}(e) \;\le\; c(S)\left(\frac{1}{k} + \frac{1}{k-1} + \dots + 1\right) \;=\; c(S)\,H_k.
\]
Comparing the rectangles of width $1$ and heights $\tfrac12,\tfrac13,\dots,\tfrac1k$ to the area under $1/x$ on $[1,k]$ shows $H_k \le 1 + \int_1^k \tfrac{1}{x}\,dx = \ln k + 1$. Finally, every element $e\in U$ belongs to at least one set $S\in\mathcal{O}$ and $\operatorname{price}(e)\ge 0$, so
\begin{align*}
  \sum_{i\in C} c_i &= \sum_{e\in U}\operatorname{price}(e) \\
  &\le \sum_{S\in\mathcal{O}}\sum_{e\in S}\operatorname{price}(e) && \text{(each $e\in U$ appears at least once)} \\
  &\le \sum_{S\in\mathcal{O}} H_{\abs{S}}\, c(S) \;\le\; H_n \sum_{S\in\mathcal{O}} c(S) \;=\; H_n\cdot\mathrm{OPT}. && \text{(since $\abs{S}\le n \implies H_{\abs{S}}\le H_n$)} \qquad\blacksquare
\end{align*}
\end{whybox}

Maintaining each set's current uncovered count $\abs{S_i\cap R}$ in a priority queue gives running time

\section{Metric TSP, the double-tree bound, and Christofides}

In the \emph{traveling salesman problem} (TSP), we are given a complete undirected graph $\Gph$ on $n = \abs{V}$
vertices with nonnegative edge weights $w(u,v) = w(v,u) \ge 0$, and we seek a minimum-weight Hamiltonian cycle
(a tour visiting every vertex once and returning to its start). Without restrictions on $w$, TSP cannot be
approximated within any constant --- or even within $2^{\poly(n)}$, where $\poly(n)$ is any polynomial in $n$ ---
unless $\textsf{P}=\textsf{NP}$ (Exercise~\ref{ch38:ex-tsp-nonmetric}). Approximation becomes possible under
the \emph{metric} assumption: weights obey the triangle inequality
\[
  w(u,z) \;\le\; w(u,v) + w(v,z) \qquad\text{for all } u,v,z\in V.
\]
Geometric distances, road travel times, and shortest-path distances in weighted graphs all satisfy this inequality.

\subsection{The MST lower bound and double-tree}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Deleting any single edge from an optimal tour leaves a Hamiltonian path, which is a spanning tree of $\Gph$.
Writing $\mathrm{MST}$ for the total weight $w(T)$ of a minimum spanning tree $T$, every tour weighs at least $\mathrm{MST}$:
\[
  \mathrm{MST} \;\le\; \mathrm{OPT}.
\]
To turn $T$ back into a tour, duplicate each edge of $T$ to form a multigraph $T'$ in which every vertex has
even degree, trace an \emph{Eulerian circuit} (a closed walk using every edge of $T'$ once, which exists by
Chapter~5 because $T'$ is connected and all degrees are even), and \emph{shortcut} past already-visited
vertices (Figure~\ref{ch38:fig-doubletree}). The Eulerian walk costs $2\,\mathrm{MST}$, and by the triangle
inequality shortcutting never increases weight, yielding a $2$-approximation.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{DoubleTreeTSP}$(\Gph,w)$ -- metric}\label{ch38:alg-doubletree}
\DontPrintSemicolon
$T \gets$ minimum spanning tree of $\Gph$\;
$T' \gets$ multigraph doubling every edge of $T$ \tcp*{all degrees even}
$W \gets$ Eulerian circuit of $T'$ \tcp*{exists: connected, even degrees}
walk $W$, output vertices in first-visit order, close the cycle\;
\Return the resulting Hamiltonian tour\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{whybox}
Doubling each edge of $T$ makes every vertex degree even ($2\deg_T(v)$) while keeping $T'$ connected, so an
Eulerian circuit $W$ exists and has total weight $2\,w(T) = 2\,\mathrm{MST}$. Listing vertices in order of first
visit along $W$ replaces each revisited multi-step detour $u \to v_1 \to \dots \to v_k \to z$ by the single
edge $\{u,z\}$. Repeatedly applying the triangle inequality gives $w(u,z) \le w(u,v_1) + \dots + w(v_k,z)$,
so the resulting Hamiltonian tour weighs at most $2\,\mathrm{MST} \le 2\,\mathrm{OPT}$.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{Christofides: fixing the parity instead of doubling}

Doubling the entire tree $T$ is wasteful because only the odd-degree vertices of $T$ prevent an Eulerian
circuit. Christofides repairs parity at half the extra cost by adding a minimum-weight perfect matching on
those odd-degree vertices alone (Figure~\ref{ch38:fig-christofides-steps}).

\begin{focusbox}
Let $O$ be the set of vertices of odd degree in the MST $T$. By the handshake lemma (Chapter~1: vertex
degrees sum to $2\abs{E(T)}$, an even number), $\abs{O}$ is even. Compute a \emph{minimum-weight perfect
matching} $M$ on $O$ --- a set of $\abs{O}/2$ vertex-disjoint edges pairing all of $O$ at minimum total metric
weight, solvable in polynomial time via Edmonds' blossom algorithm (Chapter~17). Adding $M$ to $T$ increases
the degree of every vertex in $O$ by $1$ (flipping odd to even) and leaves all other degrees even, so the
multigraph $T\cup M$ has an Eulerian circuit.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{Christofides}$(\Gph,w)$ -- metric}\label{ch38:alg-christofides}
\DontPrintSemicolon
$T \gets$ minimum spanning tree\;
$O \gets$ vertices of odd degree in $T$ \tcp*{$\abs O$ even}
$M \gets$ minimum-weight perfect matching on $O$ (metric weights)\;
$H \gets T \cup M$ \tcp*{multigraph; all degrees even, connected}
$W \gets$ Eulerian circuit of $H$\;
shortcut $W$ to a Hamiltonian tour and return it\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}\label{ch38:thm-christofides}
\textup{\textsc{Christofides}} is a $\tfrac32$-approximation for metric TSP.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
The Eulerian circuit of $H = T\cup M$ has weight $w(T)+w(M)$, and shortcutting never increases weight by
the triangle inequality, so the returned tour weighs at most $w(T)+w(M)$. We bound each term against $\mathrm{OPT}$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tree:} $w(T) = \mathrm{MST} \le \mathrm{OPT}$, since deleting one edge of an optimal tour gives a spanning tree.
  \item \textbf{Matching:} Walk an optimal tour of weight $\mathrm{OPT}$ and list the vertices of $O$ in the cyclic order $o_1,o_2,\dots,o_{2t},o_1$ in which the tour visits them (Figure~\ref{ch38:fig-christofides-matching-bound}). Shortcutting the optimal tour directly between consecutive vertices of $O$ produces an even cycle $\mathcal{C}$ on $O$ with $w(\mathcal{C})\le\mathrm{OPT}$. This $2t$-cycle decomposes into two disjoint perfect matchings on $O$: the odd-indexed edges $M_1=\{\{o_1,o_2\},\{o_3,o_4\},\dots\}$ and the even-indexed edges $M_2=\{\{o_2,o_3\},\{o_4,o_5\},\dots\}$. Because $w(M_1)+w(M_2)=w(\mathcal{C})\le\mathrm{OPT}$, the cheaper of $M_1,M_2$ weighs at most $\tfrac12\mathrm{OPT}$, and the minimum-weight perfect matching $M$ on $O$ is no heavier:
  \[
    w(M) \;\le\; \min\bigl(w(M_1),\, w(M_2)\bigr) \;\le\; \tfrac12\,\mathrm{OPT}.
  \]
\end{itemize}
Combining the two bounds gives
\[
  \text{tour weight} \;\le\; w(T) + w(M) \;\le\; \mathrm{OPT} + \tfrac12\,\mathrm{OPT} \;=\; \tfrac32\,\mathrm{OPT}. \qquad\blacksquare
\]
\end{whybox}

\section{Steiner tree: the metric closure and double-spanning-tree}

\emph{Steiner tree} generalizes MST. Given a weighted graph $\Gph=(V,E,w)$ and a subset $R\subseteq V$ of
\emph{terminals}, we seek a minimum-weight connected subgraph containing all of $R$; the subgraph may also
include non-terminal \emph{Steiner} vertices in $V\setminus R$ whenever routing through them lowers total
cost. Deciding which Steiner vertices to include makes the problem \textsf{NP}-hard. A $2$-approximation
applies the MST lower bound to the \emph{metric closure} $\bar\Gph$ on $R$ --- the complete graph on $R$
whose edge weight $\bar w(s,t)=\dist_\Gph(s,t)$ is the shortest-path distance between $s$ and $t$ in $\Gph$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Ignore the Steiner vertices temporarily by passing to the metric closure $\bar\Gph$ on the terminals $R$,
compute an MST $\bar T$ of $\bar\Gph$, and expand each chosen metric edge $\{s,t\}\in\bar T$ back into a
shortest $s$--$t$ path in $\Gph$ (deleting any cycles created when expanded paths overlap). Steiner
vertices appear automatically whenever a shortest path between two terminals passes through them, and
doubling an optimal Steiner tree bounds $\bar w(\bar T)$ by twice $\mathrm{OPT}$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{SteinerMST}$(\Gph,w,R)$}\label{ch38:alg-steiner}
\DontPrintSemicolon
compute the metric closure $\bar\Gph$ on $R$: $\bar w(s,t)=\dist_\Gph(s,t)$\;
$\bar T \gets$ minimum spanning tree of $\bar\Gph$\;
replace each edge $\{s,t\}\in\bar T$ by a shortest $s$--$t$ path in $\Gph$\;
remove cycles/redundant edges to get a tree; \Return it\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}\label{ch38:thm-steiner2}
\textup{\textsc{SteinerMST}} returns a Steiner tree of weight at most $2(1-\tfrac1{\abs R})\,\mathrm{OPT}<2\,\mathrm{OPT}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
Let $T^\ast$ be an optimal Steiner tree of weight $\mathrm{OPT}$. Duplicate every edge of $T^\ast$ and
trace an Eulerian circuit $W$ of weight $2\,\mathrm{OPT}$. Walk $W$ and record the terminals of $R$ in
order of first visit; joining consecutive terminals in that order gives a Hamiltonian cycle $\mathcal{H}$
on $R$ in the metric closure $\bar\Gph$. Each edge $\{s,t\}$ of $\mathcal{H}$ weighs $\dist_\Gph(s,t)$,
which is at most the weight of the segment of $W$ between $s$ and $t$, so $\bar w(\mathcal{H})\le 2\,\mathrm{OPT}$.
Deleting the heaviest of the $\abs{R}$ edges in $\mathcal{H}$ (which weighs at least the average
$\bar w(\mathcal{H})/\abs{R}$) leaves a spanning path of $R$ in $\bar\Gph$ of weight at most
\[
  \left(1 - \frac{1}{\abs{R}}\right)\bar w(\mathcal{H}) \;\le\; 2\left(1 - \frac{1}{\abs{R}}\right)\mathrm{OPT}.
\]
Because the MST $\bar T$ of $\bar\Gph$ weighs no more than that spanning path, and expanding metric edges
into shortest paths in $\Gph$ (then dropping redundant edges) never increases total weight, the returned
Steiner tree costs at most $2(1-\tfrac{1}{\abs{R}})\,\mathrm{OPT}$. $\blacksquare$
\end{whybox}

\begin{labbox}
Build a wheel graph $\Gph$ with a central Steiner vertex $c$ connected to four terminals $R=\{N,E,S,W\}$
by spokes of weight $1$, plus four rim edges $\{N,E\},\{E,S\},\{S,W\},\{W,N\}$ of weight $2$
(Figure~\ref{ch38:fig-steiner-closure}). Every pair of terminals has shortest-path distance $2$ in $\Gph$,
so every edge of the metric closure $\bar\Gph$ on $R$ has weight $2$, and any MST $\bar T$ of $\bar\Gph$
has weight $3\cdot 2 = 6$. Choosing $\bar T$ as the rim path $N\!-\!E\!-\!S\!-\!W$ and expanding each edge
along the rim of $\Gph$ produces a tree of weight $6$ that never uses $c$. (Had the rim edges been absent,
every shortest path would pass through $c$, and deduplicating the shared spokes in Step~4 would recover
the star.) Meanwhile, the optimal Steiner tree $T^\ast$ is the four-spoke star through $c$ of weight $4$,
so the algorithm pays $6 = 2(1-\tfrac{1}{4})\cdot 4 = \tfrac{3}{2}\,\mathrm{OPT}$, attaining the bound of
Theorem~\ref{ch38:thm-steiner2}.
\end{labbox}

\section{Max-cut: greedy, local search, and SDP rounding}

We now turn to a maximization problem. Given an undirected graph $\Gph=(V,E)$ (with unit edge weights
for clarity; nonnegative edge weights work identically), partition $V$ into two disjoint sets $(A,B)$
to maximize the number of \emph{cut} edges --- edges with one endpoint in $A$ and one in $B$. Max-cut is
\textsf{NP}-hard (Chapter~37), whereas minimum cut runs in polynomial time (Chapter~11).

\subsection{Greedy and local search: factor one half}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Independent fair coin flips already give a $\tfrac12$-approximation in expectation. Place each vertex in
$A$ or $B$ independently with probability $\tfrac12$. Any edge $\{u,v\}$ lands on one of four equally
likely side pairs $(A,A),(A,B),(B,A),(B,B)$, two of which cut $\{u,v\}$, so each edge is cut with
probability $\tfrac12$. By \emph{linearity of expectation} --- the expected sum of random variables
equals the sum of their expectations, even when edges share endpoints --- the expected cut size is
$\tfrac12\abs{E}$. Because no cut can exceed $\mathrm{OPT}\le\abs{E}$, the expected value is at least
$\tfrac12\,\mathrm{OPT}$.

Local search \emph{derandomizes} this guarantee (replaces random choices with a deterministic procedure):
start from any partition $(A,B)$ and repeatedly move any vertex that has more neighbors on its own side
than across the cut. Each move increases the cut by at least $1$, and when no move remains every vertex
has at least half its incident edges cut.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{LocalMaxCut}$(\Gph)$}\label{ch38:alg-localcut}
\DontPrintSemicolon
$(A,B)\gets$ arbitrary partition\;
\While{some $v$ has more neighbors on its own side than the other}{
  move $v$ to the other side\;
}
\Return $(A,B)$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{whybox}
Let $d(v)$ be the degree of $v$ and $c(v)$ be the number of edges incident to $v$ that cross $(A,B)$.
When the loop terminates, $c(v)\ge \tfrac12 d(v)$ for every $v\in V$ (otherwise moving $v$ would strictly
increase the cut). Summing over all vertices and counting edge endpoints gives
\[
  2\,\abs{E(A,B)} \;=\; \sum_{v\in V} c(v) \;\ge\; \frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V} d(v) \;=\; \abs{E},
\]
so $\abs{E(A,B)} \ge \tfrac12\abs{E} \ge \tfrac12\,\mathrm{OPT}$. Because the cut size is an integer in
$[0,\abs{E}]$ that increases by at least $1$ per move, at most $\abs{E}$ moves occur, taking
$\Oh(\abs{V}\abs{E})$ total time. $\blacksquare$
\end{whybox}

\subsection{Goemans--Williamson: the $0.878$ SDP rounding}

For twenty years no polynomial-time algorithm beat ratio $\tfrac12$; natural LP relaxations have an
integrality gap of $\tfrac12$ too. In 1995, Michel Goemans and David Williamson broke the barrier using
a \emph{semidefinite program} (SDP). An exact partition assigns each vertex $v\in V$ (with $n=\abs{V}$)
a scalar sign $y_v \in \{-1,+1\}$, placing $+1$ in $A$ and $-1$ in $B$. Goemans and Williamson relaxed
each scalar sign $y_v \in \{-1,+1\}$ to a unit vector $x_v \in \R^n$ of Euclidean length
$\norm{x_v}_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n x_{v,i}^2} = 1$ on the $n$-dimensional unit sphere (Figure~\ref{ch38:fig-gw-sphere-curve}).

\begin{intuitionbox}
For signs $y_v\in\{-1,+1\}$, the product $y_u y_v$ equals $+1$ when $u,v$ share a side and $-1$ when
they are separated, so $\tfrac12(1-y_u y_v)$ equals $1$ on cut edges and $0$ otherwise. Replacing $y_u y_v$
with the Euclidean inner product (dot product) $\langle x_u,x_v\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^n x_{u,i}x_{v,i} = \cos\theta_{uv}$,
where $\theta_{uv}\in[0,\pi]$ is the angle between unit vectors $x_u$ and $x_v$, produces the vector relaxation
\[
  \max \sum_{\{u,v\}\in E} \frac{1 - \langle x_u, x_v\rangle}{2}
  \quad\text{subject to}\quad \norm{x_v}_2 = 1 \text{ for all } v\in V,\quad x_v\in\R^n.
\]
Setting $x_v = (y_v, 0, \dots, 0)$ shows every $\pm 1$ cut is feasible, so the optimal SDP value
satisfies $\mathrm{SDP} \ge \mathrm{OPT}$ (an upper bound $U = \mathrm{SDP}$ on $\mathrm{OPT}$).

Why can we solve this continuous problem in polynomial time? Collect the pairwise inner products into the
$n\times n$ \emph{Gram matrix} $\mathbf{X}$ with entries $X_{uv} = \langle x_u, x_v\rangle$. A symmetric
matrix $\mathbf{X}$ is the Gram matrix of vectors $x_1,\dots,x_n\in\R^n$ if and only if $\mathbf{X}$ is
\emph{positive semidefinite} (written $\mathbf{X}\succeq 0$), meaning $z^\top\mathbf{X}z \ge 0$ for every
vector $z\in\R^n$ (equivalently, all eigenvalues of $\mathbf{X}$ are nonnegative). Under this change of
variables, the unit-length constraint becomes the linear equation $X_{vv} = 1$, the objective
$\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}\tfrac12(1-X_{uv})$ is linear in $\mathbf{X}$, and the feasible set
$\{\mathbf{X}\succeq 0 : X_{vv}=1\}$ is convex (closed under weighted averages). Interior-point methods ---
polynomial-time convex optimization algorithms that step through the interior of the feasible set ---
compute $\mathbf{X}^\ast$ to any precision $\eps>0$, and Cholesky factorization ($\mathbf{X}^\ast = \mathbf{V}^\top\mathbf{V}$)
recovers the vectors $x_v$ as the columns of $\mathbf{V}$.
\end{intuitionbox}

To round the unit vectors $x_v$ back into a partition $(A,B)$, slice the sphere with a \emph{random
hyperplane} through the origin: draw a vector $r = (r_1,\dots,r_n)\in\R^n$ whose coordinates are
independent \emph{standard Gaussian} (standard normal) random variables with density
$\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-t^2/2}$. Because the joint density $(2\pi)^{-n/2}e^{-\norm{r}_2^2/2}$ depends
only on the length $\norm{r}_2$ and not on direction, $r/\norm{r}_2$ is distributed uniformly on the unit
sphere. The orthogonal hyperplane $r^\perp = \{x\in\R^n : \langle x,r\rangle = 0\}$ splits the sphere
into two hemispheres, and we set $A = \{v : \langle x_v, r\rangle \ge 0\}$ and $B = V\setminus A$.

What is the probability that $r^\perp$ separates $x_u$ and $x_v$? Only the two-dimensional plane spanned
by $x_u$ and $x_v$ matters: the orthogonal projection of $r$ onto that plane is a two-dimensional standard
Gaussian vector, so its direction is uniform over the $2\pi$ radians of the circle. Looking down onto that
plane (left of Figure~\ref{ch38:fig-gw-sphere-curve}), $\langle x_u,r\rangle$ and $\langle x_v,r\rangle$
have opposite signs when the line $r^\perp$ passes between $x_u$ and $x_v$, which occurs across two
opposite arcs of angle $\theta_{uv}$ each --- probability $2\theta_{uv}/(2\pi) = \theta_{uv}/\pi$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{GW-MaxCut}$(\Gph)$}\label{ch38:alg-gw}
\DontPrintSemicolon
solve the SDP: $\max \sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}\dfrac{1-\langle x_u,x_v\rangle}{2}$ over unit vectors $x_v\in\R^n$\;
draw a standard Gaussian vector $r\in\R^n$\;
$A \gets \{v: \langle x_v, r\rangle \ge 0\}$,\quad $B \gets V\setminus A$\;
\Return $(A,B)$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[Goemans--Williamson, 1995]\label{ch38:thm-gw}
\textup{\textsc{GW-MaxCut}} returns a cut of expected value at least $\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}\cdot\mathrm{OPT}$,
where
\[
  \alpha_{\mathrm{GW}} \;=\; \min_{0<\theta\le\pi}\frac{\theta/\pi}{(1-\cos\theta)/2}
  \;\approx\; 0.87856.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
For each edge $\{u,v\}\in E$ with vector angle $\theta_{uv}=\arccos\langle x_u,x_v\rangle\in[0,\pi]$,
the hyperplane cuts $\{u,v\}$ with probability $\theta_{uv}/\pi$, while the edge contributes
$(1-\cos\theta_{uv})/2$ to the SDP objective. By definition of $\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}$, every angle
$\theta_{uv}\in[0,\pi]$ satisfies $\theta_{uv}/\pi \ge \alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}\cdot(1-\cos\theta_{uv})/2$
(trivially $0 \ge 0$ when $\theta_{uv}=0$). Applying linearity of expectation across all edges gives
\begin{align*}
  \mathbb{E}\bigl[\abs{E(A,B)}\bigr] &= \sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}\frac{\theta_{uv}}{\pi} && \text{(linearity of expectation)} \\
  &\ge \alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}\frac{1-\cos\theta_{uv}}{2} && \text{(edge-by-edge ratio $\ge \alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}$)} \\
  &= \alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}\cdot\mathrm{SDP} \;\ge\; \alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}\cdot\mathrm{OPT}. && \text{(since $\mathrm{SDP}\ge\mathrm{OPT}$)}
\end{align*}
Differentiating $\frac{2\theta}{\pi(1-\cos\theta)}$ gives $1 - \cos\theta = \theta\sin\theta$ (equivalently $\tan(\theta/2)=\theta$), solved
near $\theta^\ast\approx 2.331\text{ rad}$ ($133.6^\circ$), where the ratio equals $\approx 0.87856$. $\blacksquare$
\end{whybox}

On the triangle $K_3$, the maximum cut is $2$. In the SDP, three unit vectors $120^\circ$ apart in a plane
satisfy $\langle x_u,x_v\rangle = \cos(2\pi/3) = -\tfrac12$, so each edge contributes
$\tfrac12(1+\tfrac12)=\tfrac34$ and $\mathrm{SDP} = 3\cdot\tfrac34 = \tfrac94$. Random hyperplane rounding
cuts each edge with probability $\tfrac{2\pi/3}{\pi}=\tfrac23$, giving expected cut
$3\cdot\tfrac23 = 2 = \tfrac89\,\mathrm{SDP}$, which equals $\mathrm{OPT}$.

\begin{trapbox}
The minimum ratio $\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}$ does \emph{not} occur at an endpoint of $(0,\pi]$ (right panel of
Figure~\ref{ch38:fig-gw-sphere-curve}). At $\theta=\pi$ (antipodal vectors, $x_v=-x_u$), the ratio is
$\frac{1}{(1-(-1))/2}=1$; as $\theta\to 0^+$, $1-\cos\theta \approx \theta^2/2$ shrinks quadratically, so
\[
  f(\theta) \;=\; \frac{2\theta}{\pi(1-\cos\theta)} \;\sim\; \frac{4}{\pi\theta} \;\longrightarrow\; +\infty.
\]
Checking only endpoints misses the interior minimum at $\theta^\ast\approx 134^\circ$ completely.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{marginnotebox}
Khot, Kindler, Mossel, and O'Donnell (2007) proved that under the Unique Games Conjecture, approximating
Max-Cut within $\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}+\eps$ is \textsf{NP}-hard for every $\eps>0$ --- the transcendental
trigonometric constant from sphere geometry matches the hardness threshold. Unconditionally (assuming only
$\textsf{P}\neq\textsf{NP}$), H\aa stad (2001) proved $\tfrac{16}{17}\approx 0.941$-hardness.
\end{marginnotebox}

\begin{complexitybox}
The Max-Cut SDP can be solved to additive error $\delta$ in $\poly(n,\log(1/\delta))$ time via interior-point
solvers or faster first-order matrix-exponentiation methods, and evaluating $\langle x_v,r\rangle$ for one
random hyperplane takes $\Oh(n^2)$ time. Sampling $\Oh(\log n)$ independent hyperplanes and keeping the
largest cut achieves at least $(\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}-\eps)\,\mathrm{OPT}$ with high probability.
\end{complexitybox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Writing the discrete Max-Cut objective in terms of the graph Laplacian $\Lap$ (Chapter~31) gives
$\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}\tfrac12(1-y_u y_v) = \tfrac14\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}(y_u-y_v)^2 = \tfrac14 y^\top\Lap y$
for $y\in\{-1,+1\}^n$. Dropping the per-vertex constraint $y_v^2=1$ down to the single norm constraint
$\norm{y}_2^2 = n$ yields the looser eigenvalue upper bound $\tfrac{n}{4}\lambda_{\max}(\Lap)$, whereas
keeping $X_{vv}=1$ at every vertex yields the tighter Goemans--Williamson SDP. Max-Cut probes the
\emph{top} of the Laplacian spectrum; sparsest cut below probes the \emph{bottom}.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Sparsest cut, conductance, and the Leighton--Rao and ARV bounds}

The \emph{sparsest cut} problem connects approximation to Laplacian spectra, metric embeddings, and
electrical flows. Unlike minimum cut, which ignores the sizes of the two sides, sparsest cut asks for a
partition $(S,\bar S)$ that cuts few edges \emph{relative} to how evenly it splits the graph.

\begin{definition}[Sparsest cut / conductance]\label{ch38:def-sparsest}
Let $\Gph=(V,E)$ be an undirected graph on $n=\abs{V}$ vertices. For any subset $S\subseteq V$ with
$0<\abs S\le n/2$, write $\bar S = V\setminus S$ for its complement and $E(S,\bar S)$ for the set of
edges with one endpoint in $S$ and one in $\bar S$. The (uniform) \emph{sparsity} of $S$ and the
related \emph{conductance} $\phi(S)$ are
\[
  \Phi(S) \;=\; \frac{\abs{E(S,\bar S)}}{\abs S\,\abs{\bar S}},
  \qquad
  \phi(S) \;=\; \frac{\abs{E(S,\bar S)}}{\min\bigl(\operatorname{vol}(S),\,\operatorname{vol}(\bar S)\bigr)},
\]
where $\operatorname{vol}(S)=\sum_{v\in S}\deg(v)$ is the sum of degrees in $S$. The \emph{sparsest cut}
value of $\Gph$ is $\Phi(\Gph) = \min_{0<\abs S\le n/2}\Phi(S)$, and its conductance (\emph{Cheeger constant},
Chapter~32) is $\phi(\Gph) = \min_{\emptyset\neq S\subsetneq V} \phi(S)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Global minimum cut is fooled by a single \emph{pendant vertex} (a degree-$1$ vertex attached by one edge):
cutting that edge costs $1$ yet isolates a single vertex while leaving the rest of the graph intact.
Dividing by $\abs{S}\abs{\bar S}$ (which ranges from $n-1$ on a $1$-vertex cut up to $n^2/4$ on a balanced
bisection) penalizes lopsided splits and isolates bottlenecks between large communities
(Figure~\ref{ch38:fig-sparsest-vs-mincut}). That balance makes sparsest cut useful for divide-and-conquer,
image segmentation, and graph clustering, and also makes it \textsf{NP}-hard to compute exactly.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{The spectral bound (Cheeger) and where it fails}

Let $\mu_2$ denote the second-smallest eigenvalue of the normalized Laplacian $\mathcal{L}$ of a connected
graph $\Gph$ (Chapter~32; on a $d$-regular graph, $\mu_2 = \lambda_2/d$ where $\lambda_2$ is the second-smallest
eigenvalue of the unnormalized Laplacian $\Lap$). \emph{Cheeger's inequality} (Chapter~32) bounds $\phi(\Gph)$
from both sides:
\[
  \frac{\mu_2}{2} \;\le\; \phi(\Gph) \;\le\; \sqrt{2\mu_2}.
\]
Sorting vertices by the eigenvector of $\mu_2$ and taking the best prefix (\emph{sweep cut}) produces a set
$S$ of conductance $\phi(S) \le \sqrt{2\mu_2}$. Substituting the left inequality $\mu_2 \le 2\phi(\Gph)$ gives
\[
  \phi(S) \;\le\; \sqrt{2\mu_2} \;\le\; \sqrt{4\phi(\Gph)} \;=\; 2\sqrt{\phi(\Gph)},
\]
an approximation ratio of $\tfrac{\phi(S)}{\phi(\Gph)} \le \tfrac{2}{\sqrt{\phi(\Gph)}}$. When $\Gph$ is an
expander with $\phi(\Gph)=\Om(1)$ (Chapter~34), this ratio is $\Oh(1)$; on the $n$-cycle $C_n$, however,
$\phi(C_n)=\Th(1/n)$ and $\mu_2 = 1-\cos(2\pi/n) = \Th(1/n^2)$, so the Cheeger guarantee degrades to $\Oh(\sqrt{n})$.

\subsection{Leighton--Rao: LP, multicommodity flow, and $\Oh(\log n)$}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Why does every cut $(S,\bar S)$ define a distance function on $V$? Set the \emph{cut metric}
$\delta_S(u,v)=1$ when $u$ and $v$ lie on opposite sides of $(S,\bar S)$ and $0$ when they lie on the same
side. Among all $\binom{n}{2}$ unordered pairs $\{u,v\}$, there are $\abs{S}\abs{\bar S}$ separated pairs in
total, and $\abs{E(S,\bar S)}$ of them are edges of $\Gph$, so scaling $d_{uv} = \delta_S(u,v)/(\abs{S}\abs{\bar S})$
gives $\sum_{\{u,v\}} d_{uv} = 1$ and $\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E} d_{uv} = \Phi(S)$.

Dropping the requirement that $d$ come from a cut and asking only that $d$ form a \emph{semimetric}
($d_{uv}=d_{vu}\ge 0$, $d_{uu}=0$, and $d_{uz}\le d_{uv}+d_{vz}$) produces the Leighton--Rao LP relaxation:
\[
  \min \sum_{\{u,v\}\in E} d_{uv}
  \quad\text{subject to}\quad
  d_{uz} \le d_{uv} + d_{vz}\ \ \forall u,v,z\in V,\quad
  \sum_{\{u,v\}} d_{uv} = 1,\quad d_{uv}\ge 0.
\]
Its LP dual is \emph{maximum concurrent multicommodity flow}: maximize $f$ such that $f$ units of flow can be
routed simultaneously between \emph{every} unordered pair $\{u,v\}$ (treating each pair's demand as a separate
commodity that cannot cancel or mix with other pairs) while total flow across all pairs on each edge stays at most $1$.

To round the LP's optimal metric $d^\star$ into an actual cut, Linial, London, and Rabinovich (1995) used a
theorem of Bourgain (1985) to \emph{embed} $(V,d^\star)$ into $\ell_1$ space: map each vertex $u$ to a vector
$\psi(u)\in\R^m$ with Manhattan distance $\norm{\psi(u)-\psi(v)}_1 = \sum_{i=1}^m \abs{\psi(u)_i-\psi(v)_i}$
so that distances change by at most a factor (\emph{distortion}) $\alpha = \Oh(\log n)$, meaning
\[
  d^\star(u,v) \;\ge\; \norm{\psi(u)-\psi(v)}_1 \;\ge\; \frac{1}{\alpha}\,d^\star(u,v) \qquad\text{for all } u,v\in V.
\]
(When $\alpha=1$, the embedding preserves distances without change and is called \emph{isometric}.) Once we
are in $\ell_1$, choosing the best threshold cut along the coordinates of $\psi$ recovers a graph cut losing
zero further factor (Figure~\ref{ch38:fig-metric-hierarchy}).
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{theorem}[Leighton--Rao, 1988]\label{ch38:thm-lr}
Sparsest cut admits a polynomial-time $\Oh(\log n)$-approximation via the metric LP relaxation and
Bourgain's $\ell_1$ embedding. Equivalently, the worst-case gap between maximum concurrent multicommodity
flow and minimum sparsest cut is $\Th(\log n)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why $\ell_1$ metrics give cuts with no loss.} For a single coordinate $x\in\R^V$, sorting vertices and
integrating threshold indicators shows $\abs{x_u - x_v} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \delta_{\{w : x_w \le t\}}(u,v)\,dt$.
Summing over all $m$ coordinates of $\psi:V\to\R^m$ writes any $\ell_1$ distance function as a nonnegative
weighted sum $\sum_S \lambda_S\,\delta_S$ of cut metrics ($\lambda_S\ge 0$, the \emph{cut cone}). Hence for
any nonzero $\ell_1$ metric $d^{\ell_1} = \sum_S \lambda_S\,\delta_S$,
\[
  \frac{\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E} d^{\ell_1}(u,v)}{\sum_{\{u,v\}} d^{\ell_1}(u,v)}
  \;=\; \frac{\sum_S \lambda_S\,\abs{E(S,\bar S)}}{\sum_S \lambda_S\,\abs{S}\abs{\bar S}}
  \;\ge\; \min_{S:\,\lambda_S>0} \frac{\abs{E(S,\bar S)}}{\abs{S}\abs{\bar S}}
  \;=\; \min_{S:\,\lambda_S>0} \Phi(S),
\]
because a ratio of positive sums is at least the minimum termwise ratio. Applying Bourgain's contraction-and-expansion
bounds to $d^{\ell_1}(u,v)=\norm{\psi(u)-\psi(v)}_1$ shrinks the numerator relative to $d^\star$ and shrinks the
denominator by at most $\alpha = \Oh(\log n)$, producing a threshold cut $S$ with
$\Phi(S) \le \alpha\cdot\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{LP}} \le \Oh(\log n)\,\Phi(\Gph)$. Constant-degree expander
graphs exhibit an $\Om(\log n)$ integrality gap between shortest-path distances and cuts, proving that
$\Oh(\log n)$ cannot be improved using this LP alone. $\blacksquare$
\end{whybox}

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Bourgain's embedding in brief.} Pick $\Oh(\log n)$ random subsets $A\subseteq V$ independently at each
power-of-two density $2^{-1}, 2^{-2}, \dots, 2^{-\ceil{\log_2 n}}$ (so $m = \Oh(\log^2 n)$ subsets $A_1,\dots,A_m$
in total), and set coordinate $\psi(v)_i = \tfrac{1}{m}\,d^\star(v,A_i)$, where $d^\star(v,A_i)=\min_{a\in A_i} d^\star(v,a)$.
By the triangle inequality, $\abs{d^\star(u,A_i)-d^\star(v,A_i)} \le d^\star(u,v)$, so $\norm{\psi(u)-\psi(v)}_1 \le d^\star(u,v)$.
Conversely, summing across the $\log_2 n$ density scales gives $\mathbb{E}[\norm{\psi(u)-\psi(v)}_1] = \Om(d^\star(u,v)/\log n)$,
yielding distortion $\Oh(\log n)$ with high probability.
\end{focusbox}

\subsection{ARV: the SDP and $\Oh(\sqrt{\log n})$}

\begin{intuitionbox}
To beat the $\Om(\log n)$ integrality gap of the metric LP, Arora, Rao, and Vazirani (ARV, 2004) tightened
the relaxation to an SDP: assign each vertex $v$ a vector $x_v\in\R^n$ and set $d_{uv} = \norm{x_u - x_v}_2^2$,
requiring that these \emph{squared} Euclidean distances still satisfy the triangle inequality
\[
  \norm{x_u - x_z}_2^2 \;\le\; \norm{x_u - x_v}_2^2 + \norm{x_v - x_z}_2^2 \qquad\text{for all } u,v,z\in V.
\]
Such metrics are called \emph{$\ell_2^2$} (or \emph{negative-type}) metrics; geometrically, the inequality
forbids obtuse angles ($\langle x_u-x_v,\, x_z-x_v\rangle < 0$) among every triple of vertex vectors, ruling
out the expander metrics that gave the LP an $\Om(\log n)$ gap (Figure~\ref{ch38:fig-metric-hierarchy}).
Every cut metric $\delta_S$ satisfies this constraint (place $S$ at $0$ and $\bar S$ at $1$), so the SDP remains
a valid relaxation of sparsest cut.

ARV proved a geometric \emph{structure theorem} for any feasible $\ell_2^2$ configuration of average squared
spread $1$: either many vertices cluster tightly around a ball whose boundary already gives an
$\Oh(\sqrt{\log n})$-approximate cut, or there exist two subsets $A,B\subseteq V$ each of size $\Om(n)$ whose
pairwise distances satisfy $\norm{x_u - x_v}_2^2 = \Om(1/\sqrt{\log n})$ for all $u\in A, v\in B$, across
which a sweep cut achieves ratio $\Oh(\sqrt{\log n})$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{theorem}[Arora--Rao--Vazirani, 2004]\label{ch38:thm-arv}
The $\ell_2^2$ (negative-type) SDP yields a polynomial-time $\Oh(\sqrt{\log n})$-approximation for uniform
sparsest cut, and its integrality gap is at least $\Om(\log\log n)$ and strictly $o(\log n)$.
\end{theorem}

Arora, Lee, and Naor (2005) strengthened the ARV geometry into a general metric-embedding theorem: every
$n$-point $\ell_2^2$ metric embeds into $\ell_1$ with distortion $\Oh(\sqrt{\log n}\log\log n)$ (later refined
to $\Oh(\sqrt{\log n})$ for sparsest-cut rounding). Figure~\ref{ch38:fig-metric-hierarchy} and the table below
summarize the three relaxations of sparsest cut.

All approximation loss comes from embedding the outer relaxation into $\ell_1$: $\Oh(\log n)$ from arbitrary LP metrics (Bourgain / Leighton--Rao) versus $\Oh(\sqrt{\log n})$ from $\ell_2^2$ SDP metrics (ARV).}
\label{ch38:fig-metric-hierarchy}
\end{figure}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Relaxation & Feasible object & Sparsest-cut ratio \\
\midrule
Spectral (Cheeger) & eigenvector of normalized Laplacian $\mathcal{L}$ & $\Oh(1/\sqrt{\phi(\Gph)})$ \\
Leighton--Rao (LP) & arbitrary semimetric embedded into $\ell_1$ & $\Oh(\log n)$ \\
Arora--Rao--Vazirani (SDP) & $\ell_2^2$ metric rounded / embedded into $\ell_1$ & $\Oh(\sqrt{\log n})$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{connectionbox}
The three rows connect three parts of this book. The spectral row is Cheeger's inequality from Chapters~31--34,
and the sweep cut is the spectral bisection step of Chapter~33. The Leighton--Rao LP dualizes to multicommodity
flow, while fast algorithms that compute approximate sparse cuts without general-purpose SDP solvers combine
multiplicative-weights updates with \emph{electrical flows} and near-linear-time Laplacian solvers (Chapter~41).
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{marginnotebox}
Leighton and Rao's 1988 $\Oh(\log n)$ theorem gave the first worst-case polylogarithmic sparsest-cut
guarantee on arbitrary graphs (where Cheeger can degrade to $\Oh(\sqrt{n})$) and launched the use of
low-distortion metric embeddings in algorithm design. Devanur, Khot, Saket, and Vishnoi (2006) later
pinned the integrality gap of the ARV SDP at $\Om(\log\log n)$.
\end{marginnotebox}

\section{A unifying picture}

Every algorithm in this chapter bounds its output against a polynomial-time benchmark for $\mathrm{OPT}$.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
Problem & Ratio & Benchmark & Rounding / method \\
\midrule
Vertex cover & $2$ & matching / LP & endpoints / threshold / primal-dual \\
Set cover & $H_n \le \ln n + 1$ & element price cap & greedy + harmonic charging \\
Metric TSP & $3/2$ & MST $+$ matching & Christofides shortcutting \\
Steiner tree & $2$ ($1.39$) & metric-closure MST & path expansion (hypergraph LP) \\
Max-cut & $0.878$ & Gram-matrix SDP & random Gaussian hyperplane \\
Sparsest cut & $\Oh(\sqrt{\log n})$ & LP / $\ell_2^2$ SDP metric & $\ell_1$ metric embedding \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{aestheticbox}
The table traces a clear hierarchy of benchmarks. Combinatorial structures (matchings and spanning trees)
give fast algorithms with small constant factors $2$ and $3/2$. Linear programs expose dual certificates
and handle vertex or set weights cleanly, though their integrality gaps cap vertex cover at $2$ and
sparsest cut at $\Th(\log n)$. Semidefinite programs add spherical geometry, shrinking Max-Cut's gap to
$0.878$ and sparsest cut's gap to $\Oh(\sqrt{\log n})$. For vertex cover, set cover, and Max-Cut,
complexity-theoretic hardness results match these relaxation constants (unconditionally for set cover,
and under the Unique Games Conjecture for vertex cover and Max-Cut), indicating that the geometric gap of
the relaxation captures the computational boundary of polynomial time.
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{Historical Context and Controversies}

\begin{historybox}
Worst-case approximation guarantees originated in 1966, when Ronald Graham analyzed greedy list scheduling
--- assigning jobs one by one to the earliest available processor among $m$ identical machines to minimize
total completion time --- proving an approximation ratio of $2 - 1/m$ and exhibiting counterintuitive
instances where adding more processors slows down the overall schedule. After Cook (1971) and Karp (1972)
established \textsf{NP}-completeness, David Johnson's 1974 paper systematized approximation ratios across
covering, coloring, and satisfiability problems, proving the $H_n$ bound for greedy set cover (discovered
independently by Lov\'asz in 1975). Nicos Christofides followed in 1976 (and Anatoliy Serdyukov independently
in 1978) with the $\tfrac32$-approximation for metric TSP.

On the lower-bound side, how could one ever prove that \emph{approximating} an optimum is \textsf{NP}-hard?
That barrier fell in 1992 with the \emph{probabilistically checkable proofs} (PCP) theorem of Arora, Lund,
Motwani, Sudan, and Szegedy: every problem in \textsf{NP} has proofs checkable by reading only $\Oh(1)$
random bits, which translates exact satisfiability into a constant gap between $100\%$-satisfiable and
$(1-\eps)$-satisfiable constraint instances. Using PCP reductions, H\aa stad and Feige proved tight
inapproximability bounds in the late 1990s, including $(1-o(1))\ln n$ for set cover and $n^{1-\eps}$ for
maximum clique. Meanwhile, Goemans and Williamson's 1995 SDP rounding for Max-Cut and Arora, Rao, and
Vazirani's 2004 SDP analysis for sparsest cut demonstrated that positive-semidefinite geometry can bypass
linear-programming bottlenecks. Whether Khot's 2002 Unique Games Conjecture is true remains one of the
most debated open problems in theoretical computer science: if true, a single generic SDP rounding scheme
(Raghavendra, 2008) achieves the optimal approximation ratio for every constraint satisfaction problem at once;
if false, better polynomial-time algorithms for Vertex Cover, Max-Cut, and Sparsest Cut may still await discovery.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{What We Can Now Do and Its Limitations}
\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{What we can now do}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Solve \textsf{NP}-hard graph optimization problems in polynomial time within proved worst-case factors of $\mathrm{OPT}$.
  \item Relax discrete $0/1$ or $\pm 1$ choices to linear programs or unit vectors on the sphere, then round back to valid combinatorial solutions via thresholds, random hyperplanes, or $\ell_1$ embeddings.
  \item Extract per-instance lower bounds ($\mathrm{OPT} \ge \mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{LP}}$) or upper bounds ($\mathrm{OPT} \le \mathrm{SDP}$) that certify how close a computed solution is on the specific input in hand, often far tighter than the worst-case guarantee.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Limitations}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Integrality gap ceilings}: No rounding procedure can beat the integrality gap of the relaxation it starts from; improving the ratio requires strengthening the relaxation itself.
  \item \emph{Hardness of approximation}: Not every \textsf{NP}-hard problem admits a useful approximation. Non-metric TSP cannot be approximated within any computable factor, and Maximum Clique cannot be approximated within $n^{1-\eps}$ for any $\eps > 0$ unless $\textsf{P} = \textsf{NP}$ (Chapter~29).
  \item \emph{Runtime of general SDP solvers}: Interior-point SDP solvers take $\tilde{\Oh}(n^{3.5})$ time (where $\tilde{\Oh}(\cdot)$ hides $\poly(\log n)$ factors), making off-the-shelf SDP solvers too slow for million-vertex graphs without problem-specific first-order (gradient-based) or Laplacian-solver methods.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Where We Go Next}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item In Chapter~39 (Dynamic Graph Algorithms), edges are inserted and deleted over time, and we maintain connected components and spanning forests in $\Oh(\log^2 n)$ time per update instead of recomputing from scratch.
  \item In Chapter~40 (Streaming and Massive Graphs), the edge set is too large to fit in memory, and we approximate cuts, spanning forests, and shortest-path distances in $\Oh(n\,\poly(\log n))$ space.
\end{itemize}
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch38:ex-vc-tight}
Show that Algorithm~\ref{ch38:alg-vc-match} can return $2\,\mathrm{OPT}$ by exhibiting an infinite family
of graphs (hint: disjoint single edges, or any graph with a perfect matching scanned first) where $\abs{S}=2\,\mathrm{OPT}$.
Conclude that no tighter constant can hold for this algorithm.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch38:ex-vc-weighted}
Implement the LP-rounding weighted vertex-cover algorithm and run it on the $5$-cycle $1\!-\!2\!-\!3\!-\!4\!-\!5\!-\!1$
first with vertex weights $(1,2,1,2,1)$ and then with vertex weights $(2,2,2,2,1)$. What is $\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{LP}}$
in each case, which vertices take fractional value $\tfrac12$, and what cover cost does threshold rounding return?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch38:ex-setcover-tight}
Construct, for each integer $k\ge 1$, a unit-cost set-cover instance on $n=2^{k+1}-2$ elements where
\textsc{GreedySetCover} returns $k$ sets while $\mathrm{OPT}=2$. Conclude that Theorem~\ref{ch38:thm-greedy-setcover}
is tight up to a constant factor. (Hint: partition $U$ into two optimal sets $O_1,O_2$ each of size $n/2 = 2^k - 1$,
and add $k$ greedy sets of sizes $2^k, 2^{k-1}, \dots, 2$, each taking half its elements from $O_1$ and half from $O_2$,
as in Figure~\ref{ch38:fig-setcover-harmonic}.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch38:ex-christofides-tight}
Build a family of metric TSP instances on $2k$ vertices where \textsc{Christofides} returns a tour of weight
approaching $\tfrac32\,\mathrm{OPT}$ as $k\to\infty$. (Hint: take shortest-path distances on a graph with path
edges $w(v_i,v_{i+1})=1$ for $i=1,\dots,2k-1$ and leapfrog edges $w(v_i,v_{i+2})=1+\eps$ for small $\eps>0$,
so the MST is the path of weight $2k-1$ with odd set $O=\{v_1,v_{2k}\}$.) Trace both Algorithm~\ref{ch38:alg-doubletree}
and Algorithm~\ref{ch38:alg-christofides} on your instance.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch38:ex-tsp-nonmetric}
Prove that for \emph{general} (non-metric) TSP, any polynomial-time $\alpha$-approximation for a constant
$\alpha \ge 1$ (or even $\alpha = 2^{\poly(n)}$) would imply $\textsf{P}=\textsf{NP}$. (Hint: reduce from
Hamiltonian cycle on an $n$-vertex graph by assigning weight $1$ to edges of $\Gph$ and weight $\alpha n + 1$
to non-edges.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch38:ex-steiner-gap}
Generalize Figure~\ref{ch38:fig-steiner-closure} to a wheel with one central Steiner hub $c$ at distance $1$
from $\abs{R}$ terminals that also have pairwise distance $2$ without $c$. Show that $\bar w(\bar T)/\mathrm{OPT}
= 2 - \tfrac{2}{\abs{R}} = 2(1 - \tfrac{1}{\abs{R}})$, matching Theorem~\ref{ch38:thm-steiner2}. Where does the
$-1/\abs{R}$ term come from in the proof?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch38:ex-maxcut-half}
Prove that \textsc{LocalMaxCut} (Algorithm~\ref{ch38:alg-localcut}) terminates in at most $\abs E$
iterations and returns a cut of size at least $\abs E/2$. Exhibit a bipartite graph and an initial partition
$(A,B)$ that is already a local optimum of cut size $\tfrac12\abs E$ even though $\mathrm{OPT}=\abs E$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch38:ex-gw-curve}
Numerically minimize $f(\theta)=2\theta/\bigl(\pi(1-\cos\theta)\bigr)$ over $(0,\pi]$ and verify that the
minimizer is $\theta^\ast\approx 2.331\text{ rad}$ with value $\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}\approx 0.87856$. Why
does $\mathrm{SDP}\ge\mathrm{OPT}$ allow replacing $\mathrm{SDP}$ with $\mathrm{OPT}$ in the final inequality
of Theorem~\ref{ch38:thm-gw}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch38:ex-cut-metric}
Verify that a single cut metric $\delta_S$ embeds isometrically into $\R^1$ with the $\ell_1$ norm and
that
\[
  \frac{\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}\delta_S(u,v)}{\sum_{\{u,v\}}\delta_S(u,v)} \;=\; \Phi(S)
\]
from Definition~\ref{ch38:def-sparsest}. Deduce that minimizing sparsity over the nonnegative cone of cut
metrics ($\ell_1$-embeddable metrics) equals $\Phi(\Gph)$ with no integrality gap, so that all of the
Leighton--Rao approximation loss occurs in the metric-embedding step.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch38:ex-cheeger-vs-lr}
On the $n$-vertex cycle $C_n$ (assume $n$ is even), compute the exact conductance $\phi(C_n)$ and sparsity
$\Phi(C_n)$, the Cheeger upper bound $\sqrt{2\mu_2}$ (using $\mu_2 = \tfrac12\lambda_2 = 1-\cos\tfrac{2\pi}{n}$
for the $2$-regular cycle), and the Leighton--Rao metric LP value. Which bound is tighter on $C_n$, and why
does the general $\Oh(\log n)$ embedding bound not occur on a cycle?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch38:ex-primal-dual-vc}
Formalize the \emph{primal-dual} weighted vertex-cover $2$-approximation sketched at the end of Section~\ref{ch38:sec-vc}:
start with dual edge variables $y_e=0$, pick any uncovered edge $e=\{u,v\}$, raise $y_e$ until at least one
endpoint satisfies $\sum_{e'\ni w} y_{e'} = w_w$, add every such tight endpoint to $S$, and repeat. Prove
that $\sum_{v\in S} w_v \le 2\sum_{e\in E} y_e \le 2\,\mathrm{OPT}$ and explain how this generalizes
Algorithm~\ref{ch38:alg-vc-match}.
\end{exercise}
