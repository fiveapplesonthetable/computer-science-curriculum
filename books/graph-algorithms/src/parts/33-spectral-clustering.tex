The previous two chapters developed the algebra of the graph Laplacian: the matrix
$\Lap = \mathbf{D} - \Adjm$ (where $\mathbf{D}$ is the diagonal matrix of vertex degrees and $\Adjm$ is the weighted adjacency matrix), its non-negative eigenvalues $0 = \lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \dots \le \lambda_n$ on an $n$-vertex graph,
and the quadratic form
\[
  x^\top \Lap x \;=\; \sum_{(i,j)\in E} w_{ij}(x_i - x_j)^2,
\]
which assigns a real number $x_i$ to each vertex $i$ and sums the weighted squared differences across all edges $(i,j)\in E$ with weights $w_{ij} \ge 0$. That algebra gives a practical method for \emph{graph partitioning}: splitting a graph's vertices into clusters that have many edges inside each cluster and few edges between different clusters. Such clusters represent communities in a social network, distinct objects in an image, or functional modules in a biological network.

Finding an exact balanced cut is NP-hard (no polynomial-time algorithm is known for general graphs; see Chapter~\ref{ch:np-hard}).
Instead of assigning each vertex a discrete cluster label directly, we assign each vertex a real coordinate --- a step called \emph{continuous relaxation}. Once vertex positions can vary continuously, the optimal coordinates are given by the eigenvectors of $\Lap$ with the smallest eigenvalues. Computing those eigenvectors in polynomial time maps the vertices to points in low-dimensional coordinate space $\R^k$, pulling densely connected groups apart so they can be separated geometrically.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Imagine pinning beads onto a taut wire, with elastic springs connecting any two beads that share an edge.
Each spring exerts a restoring force pulling its endpoints together, with total potential energy
$\sum_{(i,j)\in E}(x_i - x_j)^2$. If we release the beads with no constraints, every bead rushes to a single point, giving zero energy and collapsing all cluster structure.

To prevent collapse, we impose two constraints: the average position (center of mass) must stay at zero ($\sum_{i=1}^n x_i = 0$), and the sum of squared positions must equal $n$ ($\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2 = n$). Under these
rules, the beads cannot pile onto one point. Vertices joined by many internal springs
huddle together, while the few sparse bridges between clusters stretch across the gap.
The resulting equilibrium positions are the coordinates of the \emph{Fiedler vector} $v_2$,
the eigenvector of $\Lap$ for its second-smallest eigenvalue $\lambda_2$ (scaled to $\norm{f}^2 = n$). Slicing the wire
at a threshold reveals the bottleneck cut.
\end{intuitionbox}

\section{The cut we wish we could compute}
\label{ch33:the-cut}

Fix an undirected graph $\Gph=(V,E)$ with vertex set $V$, edge set $E$, and nonnegative edge weights $w_{ij}\ge 0$. For disjoint vertex sets
$A,B\subseteq V$, define the cut weight
\[
  \cut(A,B) \;=\; \sum_{i\in A,\,j\in B} w_{ij},
\]
and for a single set $S \subseteq V$ write $\cut(S)=\cut(S,\overline S)$, where $\overline S = V\setminus S$ is the set of all vertices not in $S$. The
\emph{minimum cut} --- the smallest $\cut(S)$ over all subsets $S$ that are neither empty nor all of $V$ --- can be computed in polynomial time
(Chapter~11), but it fails at clustering: the cheapest cut usually peels off a single low-degree
vertex. For clustering we need a \emph{balanced} cut, one that leaves a substantial piece on \emph{each} side.

Two standard objectives penalize unbalanced splits. Let $d_i = \sum_{j:(i,j)\in E} w_{ij}$ be the weighted degree of vertex $i$, let $\vol(S)=\sum_{i\in S}d_i$ be the \emph{volume} of $S$ (the sum of weighted
degrees in $S$), and let $\abs{S}$ be the cardinality (number of vertices) of $S$. For any \emph{bipartition} $(A,B)$ of $V$ --- a split of $V$ into two disjoint nonempty subsets $A$ and $B$ with $A \cup B = V$ --- define the \emph{ratio cut} ($\RatioCut$) and \emph{normalized cut} ($\NCut$):
\[
  \RatioCut(A,B) \;=\; \cut(A,B)\!\left(\frac{1}{\abs{A}}+\frac{1}{\abs{B}}\right),
  \qquad
  \NCut(A,B) \;=\; \cut(A,B)\!\left(\frac{1}{\vol(A)}+\frac{1}{\vol(B)}\right).
\]

\begin{focusbox}
Both objectives multiply the raw cut by a penalty that \emph{grows large when one side is small}. Peeling off
a single vertex $i$ makes $\abs{A}=1$ (or $\vol(A)=d_i$ small), so the factor $1/\abs{A}$ (or $1/\vol(A)$) is large and
the objective is penalized even when $\cut(A,B)$ is tiny. Minimizing either objective keeps both sides substantial. RatioCut
balances by \emph{vertex count}; normalized cut ($\NCut$) balances by \emph{volume}. They differ only in which measure of
cluster size they protect.
\end{focusbox}

Figure~\ref{ch33:fig-mincut-vs-balanced} compares the two regimes on a seven-vertex, nine-edge graph: two triangles linked by two bridge edges $(2,4)$ and $(3,5)$, plus a degree-$1$ leaf vertex~$0$ (a ``whisker'') attached to vertex~$1$. The vertex degrees are $d_0 = 1$, $d_1 = d_2 = d_3 = d_4 = d_5 = 3$, and $d_6 = 2$, giving total volume $\vol(V) = 18$. Severing the whisker $(0,1)$ achieves the global minimum cut $\cut = 1$ with sides $\{0\}$ and $\{1,\dots,6\}$, whereas cutting the two bridge edges pays $\cut = 2$ to split $\{0,1,2,3\}$ from $\{4,5,6\}$:
\begin{align*}
  \text{Whisker cut:} &\quad
    \RatioCut = 1\!\left(\frac{1}{1}+\frac{1}{6}\right) = \frac{7}{6} \approx 1.17,
    &
    \NCut &= 1\!\left(\frac{1}{1}+\frac{1}{17}\right) = \frac{18}{17} \approx 1.06,\\[4pt]
  \text{Bridge cut:} &\quad
    \RatioCut = 2\!\left(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{3}\right) = \frac{7}{6} \approx 1.17,
    &
    \NCut &= 2\!\left(\frac{1}{10}+\frac{1}{8}\right) = \frac{9}{20} = 0.45.
\end{align*}
The two cuts tie under $\RatioCut$, while $\NCut$ strongly prefers the community split ($0.45$ versus $1.06$) because $\vol(\{0\})=1$ is tiny compared to $\vol(\{0,1,2,3\})=10$.

\begin{theorem}[Hardness]
\label{ch33:hardness}
Minimizing $\RatioCut$ over all bipartitions of $V$ is NP-hard, as is minimizing $\NCut$.
\end{theorem}

To work around Theorem~\ref{ch33:hardness}, we use continuous relaxation: allowing vertex coordinates to take arbitrary real values turns the discrete cut objective into a smooth quadratic minimization problem in the graph Laplacian $\Lap$. We compute the needed eigenvector of $\Lap$ in polynomial time and then round its real coordinates back to a discrete bipartition.

\section{Bisection by the Fiedler vector}
\label{ch33:fiedler}

We first express $\RatioCut$ as an exact quadratic form in $\Lap$. Encode any bipartition $(A,B)$ of $V$ by the vector $f \in \R^n$ with entries
\[
  f_i \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    +\sqrt{\dfrac{\abs{B}}{\abs{A}}}, & i\in A,\\[8pt]
    -\sqrt{\dfrac{\abs{A}}{\abs{B}}}, & i\in B.
  \end{cases}
\]
This scaling is chosen so that two identities hold:
\begin{align*}
  \sum_{i=1}^n f_i &= \abs{A}\sqrt{\frac{\abs{B}}{\abs{A}}} - \abs{B}\sqrt{\frac{\abs{A}}{\abs{B}}} \;=\; \sqrt{\abs{A}\abs{B}} - \sqrt{\abs{A}\abs{B}} \;=\; 0,\\[4pt]
  \norm{f}^2 &= \sum_{i=1}^n f_i^2 \;=\; \abs{A}\cdot\frac{\abs{B}}{\abs{A}} + \abs{B}\cdot\frac{\abs{A}}{\abs{B}} \;=\; \abs{B} + \abs{A} \;=\; n.
\end{align*}
Thus $f$ is orthogonal to the all-ones vector $\mathbf{1} = (1,\dots,1)^\top$ (written $f \perp \mathbf{1}$, meaning $\mathbf{1}^\top f = \sum_{i=1}^n f_i = 0$) and has squared Euclidean length $\norm{f}^2 = n$.

\begin{lemma}[RatioCut as a quadratic form]
\label{ch33:ratiocut-quad}
With $f$ defined above, $\; f^\top \Lap f = n\cdot\RatioCut(A,B).$
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
In $f^\top\Lap f=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}w_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2$, edges inside $A$ or inside $B$ have $f_i = f_j$ and contribute $0$. Only edges crossing between $A$ and $B$ contribute, and each crossing edge has
\begin{align*}
  (f_i - f_j)^2
  &= \left(\sqrt{\frac{\abs{B}}{\abs{A}}} + \sqrt{\frac{\abs{A}}{\abs{B}}}\right)^{\!2}\\[3pt]
  &= \frac{\abs{B}}{\abs{A}} + 2 + \frac{\abs{A}}{\abs{B}}\\[3pt]
  &= \frac{\abs{A}+\abs{B}}{\abs{A}} + \frac{\abs{A}+\abs{B}}{\abs{B}} && \text{(splitting $2 = 1 + 1$)}\\[3pt]
  &= n\left(\frac{1}{\abs{A}}+\frac{1}{\abs{B}}\right) && \text{(since $\abs{A}+\abs{B}=n$).}
\end{align*}
Summing $w_{ij}(f_i - f_j)^2$ over all crossing edges gives
\[
  f^\top\Lap f \;=\; \cut(A,B)\cdot n\!\left(\frac{1}{\abs{A}}+\frac{1}{\abs{B}}\right) \;=\; n\cdot\RatioCut(A,B).\qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

Minimizing $\RatioCut$ is therefore equivalent to the discrete optimization problem
\[
  \min_{A,B}\; f^\top \Lap f
  \quad\text{subject to}\quad
  f \text{ of the two-valued form above},\ \ f\perp\mathbf 1,\ \ \norm f^2=n.
\]
The constraint forcing $f$ to take only two discrete values is the source of NP-hardness.
We relax the problem by dropping the two-valued requirement and allowing $f\in\R^n$ to be any real vector that satisfies the centering constraint $f\perp\mathbf 1$ and the norm constraint $\norm f^2 = n$:
\[
  \min_{f\in\R^n}\ f^\top\Lap f
  \quad\text{subject to}\quad f\perp\mathbf 1,\ \norm f^2 = n.
\]

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{The Rayleigh quotient and the Courant--Fischer theorem.}
For any real symmetric matrix $\mathbf{M}$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \dots \le \lambda_n$
and orthonormal eigenvectors $u_1, \dots, u_n$ (unit vectors satisfying $u_i^\top u_j = 0$ for $i\ne j$), the \emph{Rayleigh quotient}
\[
  R_{\mathbf{M}}(x) \;=\; \frac{x^\top \mathbf{M} x}{x^\top x}
\]
measures the quadratic form per unit squared length for any nonzero $x \in \R^n$. Expanding $x$ in the eigenvector basis as
$x = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i u_i$ gives $\norm{x}^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2$ and $x^\top \mathbf{M} x = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i c_i^2$.
The Rayleigh quotient is therefore a weighted average of the eigenvalues:
\[
  R_{\mathbf{M}}(x) \;=\; \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i c_i^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2}.
\]
Because the weights $c_i^2$ are non-negative, this weighted average lies between $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_n$, reaching $\lambda_1$ when $x = u_1$.

If we constrain $x$ to be orthogonal to $u_1$, then $c_1 = u_1^\top x = 0$. The $\lambda_1 c_1^2$ term drops out of the sum, leaving a weighted average of $\lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_n$ whose minimum is $\lambda_2$, attained at $x = u_2$.

More generally, the \emph{Courant--Fischer min-max theorem} states that for each index $k \in \{1,\dots,n\}$,
\[
  \lambda_k \;=\; \min_{\dim S = k}\; \max_{0\ne x\in S}\; R_{\mathbf{M}}(x)
  \;=\; \min_{\substack{x \perp u_1, \dots, u_{k-1} \\ x\ne 0}} R_{\mathbf{M}}(x),
\]
where $S$ ranges over all $k$-dimensional subspaces of $\R^n$. Applying this to the graph Laplacian $\Lap$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The smallest eigenvalue is $\lambda_1 = 0$, with unit eigenvector $u_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbf{1}$
        (since $\Lap \mathbf{1} = 0$).
  \item The constraint $f \perp \mathbf{1}$ in our relaxed bisection is equivalent to $f \perp u_1$.
\end{itemize}
By Courant--Fischer, minimizing $f^\top \Lap f = n\,R_{\Lap}(f)$ over all vectors orthogonal to $\mathbf{1}$ with $\norm{f}^2 = n$
gives minimum value $n \lambda_2$, attained at $f = \sqrt{n}\,v_2$, where $v_2$ is the unit eigenvector for $\lambda_2$. This eigenvector is
the \emph{Fiedler vector}, named after Miroslav Fiedler, who in 1973 called $\lambda_2$ the
\emph{algebraic connectivity} of the graph.
\end{whybox}

\begin{theorem}[Fiedler relaxation]
\label{ch33:fiedler-relax}
The continuous relaxation of $\RatioCut$ bisection is minimized by $f = \sqrt{n}\,v_2$, where $v_2$ is the unit Fiedler vector, with optimal objective value $\frac{1}{n}\min f^\top\Lap f = \lambda_2$. Consequently, the optimal discrete ratio cut $\RatioCut^\star = \min_{A,B}\RatioCut(A,B)$ satisfies $\RatioCut^\star \ge \lambda_2$: \emph{the algebraic connectivity is a lower bound on the best ratio cut.}
\end{theorem}

Figure~\ref{ch33:fig-relaxation-geom} shows why the relaxation gives a lower bound: enlarging the feasible set from isolated discrete points to the entire sphere $\set{f \perp \mathbf{1},\, \norm{f}^2 = n}$ can only lower the minimum of $f^\top \Lap f$.

To \emph{round} the real vector $v_2$ back to a discrete bipartition, we pick a threshold $t \in \R$
and set $A=\set{i: (v_2)_i \le t}$ and $B = V \setminus A$. Two standard choices are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Sign threshold} ($t=0$): split vertices by whether $(v_2)_i \le 0$ or $(v_2)_i > 0$. This works when the cluster gap straddles zero.
  \item \textbf{Sweep threshold}: sort the vertices into an ordering $\pi(1),\dots,\pi(n)$ with $(v_2)_{\pi(1)} \le \dots \le (v_2)_{\pi(n)}$, evaluate all $n-1$ prefix sets $A_k = \set{\pi(1),\dots,\pi(k)}$ (called \emph{sweep cuts}), and keep the one with the smallest $\RatioCut$. Practical implementations always use this sweep.
\end{itemize}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{SpectralBisection}$(\Gph)$ -- Fiedler partition with sweep rounding}
\label{ch33:alg-bisection}
\DontPrintSemicolon
\KwIn{Connected weighted graph $\Gph=(V,E,w)$, $n=\abs V$.}
\KwOut{A bipartition $(A,B)$ of $V$.}
Form the Laplacian $\Lap = \mathbf D - \Adjm$.\;
Compute the unit eigenvector $v_2$ for the second-smallest eigenvalue $\lambda_2$.\;
Sort vertices into an order $\pi(1),\dots,\pi(n)$ so that $(v_2)_{\pi(1)} \le (v_2)_{\pi(2)} \le \dots \le (v_2)_{\pi(n)}$.\;
$best \gets +\infty$;\quad $A^\star\gets\emptyset$.\;
\For{$k \gets 1$ \KwTo $n-1$}{
   $A \gets \set{\pi(1),\dots,\pi(k)}$,\quad $B \gets V\setminus A$.\;
   $q \gets \RatioCut(A,B)$ \tcp*{or $\NCut$ for the normalized variant}
   \If{$q < best$}{ $best \gets q$;\quad $A^\star \gets A$. }
}
\Return $(A^\star,\, V\setminus A^\star)$.\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{trapbox}
An eigenvalue solver returns $v_2$ only up to sign and scale. Because $-v_2$ is an equally valid eigenvector, \emph{never} treat ``positive entry = cluster A'' as a fixed label across separate runs; always extract the partition via a sign split or sweep.
A second trap: if $\Gph$ is \emph{disconnected}, then $\lambda_2=0$ and the $\lambda=0$ eigenspace has dimension at least $2$, so $v_2$ is not unique. Check connectivity first ($\lambda_2 > 0 \iff \Gph$ connected, proved in Chapter~31) and handle connected components separately.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection*{Tracing $\Gph_0$ by hand}
\label{ch33:trace-bisection}

Ordering the vertices $1,2,3,4,5,6$, the Laplacian of $\Gph_0$ is
\[
  \Lap \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    2 & -1 & -1 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
    -1 & 2 & -1 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
    -1 & -1 & 3 & -1 & 0 & 0\\
    0 & 0 & -1 & 3 & -1 & -1\\
    0 & 0 & 0 & -1 & 2 & -1\\
    0 & 0 & 0 & -1 & -1 & 2
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The graph has a mirror symmetry swapping $L=\set{1,2,3}$ with $R=\set{4,5,6}$ ($1\!\leftrightarrow\!6$,
$2\!\leftrightarrow\!5$, $3\!\leftrightarrow\!4$). Every eigenvector can be chosen either \emph{symmetric} (unchanged by the swap) or \emph{antisymmetric} (negated by the swap). \section{Why the relaxation is good: gaps and Cheeger}
\label{ch33:cheeger}

A continuous relaxation is useful when rounding its solution stays provably close to the discrete optimum. For balanced cuts
that guarantee is \emph{Cheeger's inequality} (proved in full in Chapter~32). For any nonempty proper subset $S \subsetneq V$ with complement $\overline{S} = V \setminus S$, recall the
\emph{conductance} of $S$ and of $\Gph$:
\[
  \phi(S) \;=\; \frac{\cut(S)}{\min\set{\vol(S),\,\vol(\overline S)}},
  \qquad
  \phi(\Gph) \;=\; \min_{S\ne\emptyset,\,S\ne V} \phi(S).
\]
Dividing by $\min\set{\vol(S),\vol(\overline{S})}$ measures the cut weight relative to the volume of the smaller side, so small conductance means a sparse, volume-balanced cut. Let $\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}$
be the second-smallest eigenvalue of the \emph{symmetric normalized Laplacian}
\[
  \Lap_{\mathrm{sym}} \;=\; \mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\Lap\,\mathbf{D}^{-1/2},
\]
where $\mathbf{D}^{-1/2} = \diag(1/\sqrt{d_1},\dots,1/\sqrt{d_n})$ (derived in Section~\ref{ch33:ncut}, assuming no isolated vertices so every $d_i > 0$).

\begin{theorem}[Cheeger's inequality]
\label{ch33:cheeger-thm}
For any connected graph $\Gph$,
\[
  \frac{\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}}{2} \;\le\; \phi(\Gph) \;\le\; \sqrt{2\,\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
The left inequality lower-bounds the best conductance: no cut in $\Gph$ can have conductance below $\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}/2$. The right inequality is constructive: sweeping the rescaled normalized eigenvector $g = \mathbf{D}^{-1/2}u_2$ (just as Algorithm~\ref{ch33:alg-bisection} sweeps $v_2$, scoring each prefix by $\phi(A)$) produces a cut of conductance at most $\sqrt{2\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}}$. Because $\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}} \le 2\phi(\Gph)$, the sweep cut satisfies $\phi(A^\star) \le 2\sqrt{\phi(\Gph)}$, within a square-root loss of optimal.
\end{whybox}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch of the upper bound]
Let $u_2$ be the unit eigenvector of $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$ for $\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}$, and set $g=\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}u_2$. Its degree-weighted quotient equals $\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}$:
\[
  R(g) \;=\; \frac{g^\top\Lap g}{\sum_{i=1}^n d_i g_i^2} \;=\; \lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}.
\]
Sort the vertices by $g_i$ and examine the $n-1$ sweep cuts. After shifting $g$ so that each sign holds at most half the total volume and keeping the better sign as a nonnegative vector $h \in [0,1]^n$ (as in Chapter~32), draw a threshold $\theta$ uniformly at random from $[0,1]$ and take the cut $S_\theta = \set{i : h_i^2 > \theta}$. If every sweep cut had conductance exceeding $\phi$, every nonempty $S_\theta$ would satisfy $\cut(S_\theta) > \phi\,\vol(S_\theta)$, so the expected cut over $\theta$ would exceed $\phi$ times the expected volume. Applying the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality ($\sum_e a_e b_e \le \sqrt{\sum_e a_e^2}\sqrt{\sum_e b_e^2}$) across edges bounds that expected cut from above by $\sqrt{2 R(g)}$ times the expected volume, forcing $R(g) > \phi^2/2$. Setting $\phi = \sqrt{2\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}}$ contradicts $R(g) = \lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}$, so at least one sweep cut has conductance at most $\sqrt{2\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{connectionbox}
Cheeger's inequality is the discrete counterpart of a theorem in Riemannian geometry (the geometry of smooth curved surfaces; Cheeger 1970, adapted to graphs by Dodziuk and Alon--Milman): the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of the Laplace operator (measuring local curvature) bounds the narrowest bottleneck of the space. On graphs it links three topics: \textbf{spectra} (companion \emph{Linear Algebra} volume: eigenvalues of symmetric matrices), \textbf{cuts} (this volume: minimum cuts and separators), and \textbf{random walks} (companion \emph{Probability} volume and Chapters~35--36: $\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}$ is the spectral gap that governs the \emph{mixing time}, the number of steps a random walk needs to spread nearly uniformly across the vertices). A sparse graph is an \emph{expander} (Chapter~34) when $\phi(\Gph)$ stays bounded below by a positive constant independent of $n$ --- equivalently, when $\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}=\Om(1)$.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Normalized cuts and the random-walk view}
\label{ch33:ncut}

$\RatioCut$ balances clusters by \emph{vertex count}; $\NCut$ balances them by \emph{volume} ($\sum_{i\in A} d_i$), which suits networks with uneven degrees where cluster size is better measured by total edge weight than by headcount. Its relaxation mirrors Section~\ref{ch33:fiedler}, replacing ordinary dot products $x^\top y = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i y_i$ with degree-weighted inner products $x^\top \mathbf{D} y = \sum_{i=1}^n d_i x_i y_i$.

Encode a bipartition $(A,B)$ by the vector $h \in \R^n$ with entries
\[
  h_i \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    +\sqrt{\dfrac{\vol(B)}{\vol(A)}}, & i \in A,\\[8pt]
    -\sqrt{\dfrac{\vol(A)}{\vol(B)}}, & i \in B.
  \end{cases}
\]
Repeating the calculation of Lemma~\ref{ch33:ratiocut-quad} with weights $d_i$ in place of $1$ gives
\begin{align*}
  h^\top\Lap h &= \vol(V)\cdot\NCut(A,B),\\
  h^\top\mathbf{D} h &= \vol(V),\\
  (\mathbf{D} h)^\top \mathbf{1} &= \sum_{i=1}^n d_i h_i \;=\; 0.
\end{align*}
Substituting $h = \mathbf{D}^{-1/2}g$ (so $g = \mathbf{D}^{1/2}h$, where $\mathbf{D}^{1/2} = \diag(\sqrt{d_1},\dots,\sqrt{d_n})$) and dropping the two-valued constraint turns the relaxed problem into
\[
  \min_{g \ne 0}\ \frac{g^\top \mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\Lap\,\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\, g}{g^\top g}
  \;=\; \min_{g \ne 0}\ \frac{g^\top \Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}\,g}{g^\top g}
  \quad\text{subject to}\quad g\perp \mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{1}.
\]
Because $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}(\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{1}) = \mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\Lap\mathbf{1} = 0$, the vector $\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{1}$ is the first eigenvector of $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$ with eigenvalue $0$. By Courant--Fischer, the minimizing vector $g$ is the second eigenvector $u_2$ of the \emph{symmetric normalized Laplacian}
\[
  \Lap_{\mathrm{sym}} \;=\; \mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\Lap\,\mathbf{D}^{-1/2} \;=\; \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\Adjm\,\mathbf{D}^{-1/2},
\]
where $\mathbf{I}$ is the $n\times n$ identity matrix, and the relaxed vertex coordinates are recovered as $h=\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}u_2$. A closely related matrix is the
\emph{random-walk Laplacian}
\[
  \Lap_{\mathrm{rw}} \;=\; \mathbf{D}^{-1}\Lap \;=\; \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{D}^{-1}\Adjm \;=\; \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{P},
\]
whose eigenvectors are the unrescaled vectors $h$ directly, and whose matrix $\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{D}^{-1}\Adjm$ is the \emph{transition matrix} of the random walk on $\Gph$ (entry $P_{ij} = w_{ij}/d_i$ is the probability that a step from vertex $i$ moves to neighbor $j$).

\begin{intuitionbox}
Place a random walker on $\Gph$ in its \emph{stationary distribution}, where vertex $i$ is occupied with probability $\pi_i = d_i / \vol(V)$ proportional to its degree. In one step, the probability that the walker is in $A$ and crosses to $B$, conditioned on starting in $A$, equals $\cut(A,B)/\vol(A)$. Summing that escape probability for $A \to B$ and $B \to A$ gives $\NCut(A,B)$ exactly. A dense cluster traps the walker inside for many steps, so minimizing $\NCut$ finds the bipartition the walker is least likely to cross. That is why $\Lap_{\mathrm{rw}} = \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{P}$ governs normalized cuts, and why its second eigenvalue $\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}$ controls how fast the walk mixes.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{formalbox}
The two normalized Laplacians have identical eigenvalues: multiplying $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}} u = \lambda^{\mathrm{nrm}} u$ on the left by $\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}$ gives $\Lap_{\mathrm{rw}}(\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}u) = \lambda^{\mathrm{nrm}}(\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}u)$, with every eigenvalue $\lambda^{\mathrm{nrm}} \in [0,2]$ (proved in Chapter~32) and eigenvectors related by $h = \mathbf{D}^{-1/2}u$. Use $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$ for computation (because it is symmetric, numerical eigensolvers avoid rounding instability and return orthonormal eigenvectors) and $\Lap_{\mathrm{rw}}$ for interpretation (its eigenvectors are the cluster coordinates $h$ directly). Unnormalized $\Lap$ relaxes $\RatioCut$; $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$ and $\Lap_{\mathrm{rw}}$ relax $\NCut$. In practice the normalized pair is preferred because it is less sensitive to wide degree variation, matching Cheeger's bound in Theorem~\ref{ch33:cheeger-thm}.
\end{formalbox}

\subsection*{Tracing the normalized cut on $\Gph_0$}

For $\Gph_0$ the degree matrix is $\mathbf{D}=\diag(2,2,3,3,2,2)$. Because all degrees are $2$ or $3$ (the graph is nearly \emph{regular}, meaning all vertices have nearly the same degree), $u_2$ of $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$ is close to the unnormalized Fiedler vector $v_2$, with the degree-$3$ bridge endpoints $3$ and $4$ pulled slightly closer to zero when forming $h = \mathbf{D}^{-1/2}u_2$ (divided by $\sqrt{3}$ rather than $\sqrt{2}$). Numerically, the second eigenvalue of
$\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$ is $\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}} \approx 0.205$, and $h = \mathbf{D}^{-1/2}u_2 \approx (0.31,\, 0.31,\, 0.19,\, -0.19,\, -0.31,\, -0.31)^\top$ changes sign between $\set{1,2,3}$ and $\set{4,5,6}$. Since $\vol(L)=\vol(R) = 2+2+3 = 7$, the $\NCut$ value of that split is
\[
  \NCut(L,R) \;=\; \cut(L,R)\!\left(\frac{1}{\vol(L)}+\frac{1}{\vol(R)}\right)
  \;=\; 1\cdot\!\left(\frac{1}{7}+\frac{1}{7}\right) \;=\; \frac{2}{7} \;\approx\; 0.286.
\]
Every other sweep cut scores worse (peeling a single degree-$2$ vertex
costs $2\cdot(\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{12}) = \tfrac{7}{6} \approx 1.17$), so the sweep again returns the two triangles. Cheeger's
bounds $\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}/2 \approx 0.102 \le \phi(\Gph_0) \le \sqrt{2\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}} \approx 0.640$ bracket $\phi(\Gph_0)=\cut(L)/\vol(L)=1/7\approx 0.143$, as plotted in Figure~\ref{ch33:fig-cheeger-band}.

\section{$k$-way spectral clustering}
\label{ch33:kway}

For $k>2$ clusters, recursively bisecting into two pieces and then splitting each piece can commit to an early bad cut that later splits cannot undo; a better approach uses the \emph{bottom $k$
eigenvectors at once}. This generalizes the line embedding of Figure~\ref{ch33:fig-line} from one
coordinate to $k$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
One non-constant eigenvector places the vertices along a line; two place them in a plane. Using the bottom $k$ eigenvectors of $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$ turns each vertex $i$ into a point $y_i\in\R^{k}$ whose $k$ coordinates come from those eigenvectors. Well-separated clusters in the graph become separated groups of points in this
\emph{spectral embedding}, and we finish with geometric clustering --- $k$-means (an algorithm that partitions points into $k$ clusters by alternating between assigning each point to its nearest cluster center and recomputing each center as the mean of its assigned points) --- in $\R^k$.
The Laplacian maps the graph into coordinates where ordinary Euclidean distance reflects how hard it is to move between vertices along edges.
\end{intuitionbox}

Why use the bottom $k$ eigenvectors? Consider the ideal case of $k$ \emph{disconnected} clusters $C_1,\dots,C_k$ with no isolated vertices. Ordering vertices by component makes $\Lap$
\emph{block-diagonal} (one square block per component and zeros elsewhere), so $\lambda_1=\dots=\lambda_k=0$, and the $0$-\emph{eigenspace} (the subspace of vectors satisfying $\Lap x = 0$) is spanned by the component indicator vectors
$\mathbf{1}_{C_1},\dots,\mathbf{1}_{C_k}$ (where $\mathbf{1}_{C_\ell}$ has $1$ on $C_\ell$ and $0$ elsewhere). Any orthonormal basis of that eigenspace places all vertices of a single component at the \emph{same point} in $\R^k$, giving $k$ distinct points that $k$-means separates with zero error.

For $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$, used in Algorithm~\ref{ch33:alg-kway}, the $0$-eigenspace is spanned by $\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{1}_{C_1},\dots,\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{1}_{C_k}$ instead, so all
vertices of cluster $C_\ell$ lie along a single ray from the origin at distance proportional to $\sqrt{d_i}$. Dividing
each vertex's coordinate row $U_{i,\cdot} \in \R^k$ by its Euclidean length $\norm{U_{i,\cdot}}_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^k U_{ij}^2}$ cancels the $\sqrt{d_i}$ factor and collapses each cluster back to one point on the unit sphere $\set{y \in \R^k : \norm{y}_2 = 1}$ (requiring every $d_i > 0$ so no row is zero). When the $k$ clusters are connected by a few sparse bridges rather than completely disconnected, the eigenvalues $\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}},\dots,\lambda_k^{\mathrm{nrm}}$ lift slightly above $0$ and the $k$ points spread into
$k$ tight clusters on the sphere; matrix perturbation theory (specifically the Davis--Kahan $\sin\Theta$ theorem in the companion \emph{Linear Algebra}
volume, which bounds how much an eigenspace rotates under a small matrix change in terms of the gap to $\lambda_{k+1}^{\mathrm{nrm}}$) guarantees that the points stay tightly grouped for $k$-means.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{NormalizedSpectralClustering}$(\Gph,k)$ -- Ng--Jordan--Weiss}
\label{ch33:alg-kway}
\DontPrintSemicolon
\KwIn{Weighted graph $\Gph=(V,E,w)$ with no isolated vertices, number of clusters $k$.}
\KwOut{A partition $V = C_1\cup\dots\cup C_k$.}
$\mathbf D \gets \diag(d_1,\dots,d_n)$;\quad $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}} \gets \mathbf I - \mathbf D^{-1/2}\Adjm\,\mathbf D^{-1/2}$.\;
Compute orthonormal eigenvectors $u_1,\dots,u_k$ of $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$ for its $k$ \emph{smallest} eigenvalues.\;
$U \gets [\,u_1\ \cdots\ u_k\,] \in \R^{n\times k}$ \tcp*{vertex $i$ is row $U_{i,\cdot}$ of $U$}
\For{$i \gets 1$ \KwTo $n$}{
   $y_i \gets U_{i,\cdot} \,/\, \norm{U_{i,\cdot}}_2$ \tcp*{row-normalize onto the unit sphere}
}
$\set{C_1,\dots,C_k} \gets \textsc{$k$-Means}(\set{y_1,\dots,y_n},\,k)$.\;
\Return the partition assigning $i$ to its $k$-means cluster.\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{trapbox}
Row-normalization on line 5 (projecting each row onto the unit sphere) is the Ng--Jordan--Weiss step, and skipping it breaks clustering when degrees vary widely: high-degree and low-degree vertices land at different distances from the origin, causing $k$-means to group by degree rather than by cluster. Second, Algorithm~\ref{ch33:alg-kway} needs the $k$ \emph{smallest} eigenvalues of $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$, so use a bottom-eigenvector solver (such as the Lanczos method or inverse iteration, which repeatedly solves Laplacian linear systems as in Chapter~41 to amplify small-eigenvalue directions), not a largest-eigenvalue power method. Finally, the $k$-means objective (the sum of squared distances from points to their assigned cluster centers, called \emph{inertia}) has local minima; run $k$-means from several randomized starting centers (using $k$-means++, which spreads initial centers apart with probability proportional to squared distance) and keep the run with the smallest inertia.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection*{A worked $k=2$ embedding, and a $k=3$ sketch}

For $k=2$ on $\Gph_0$, $U=[\,u_1\ u_2\,] \in \R^{6\times 2}$ has first column $u_1 = \mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{1}/\sqrt{\vol(V)}$ (positive on every vertex) and second column $u_2$, the second eigenvector of $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$. After
row-normalization every vertex lies on the unit circle in $\R^2$; the three $L$-vertices land together in one quadrant and
the three $R$-vertices in the adjacent quadrant (since $u_2$ has opposite signs on $L$ and $R$ while $u_1 > 0$ on both). Running $2$-means recovers $\set{1,2,3}$ and $\set{4,5,6}$, matching the sweep cut geometrically.

To see $k=3$, attach a third triangle $C_3=\set{7,8,9}$ to $\Gph_0$ by a single bridge $(6,7)$, forming the nine-vertex chain in Figure~\ref{ch33:fig-chain}. The bottom four eigenvalues of $\Lap$ are $\lambda_1=0,\ \lambda_2\approx 0.21,\ \lambda_3\approx 0.70,\ \lambda_4=3$, and for $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$ the full nine-eigenvalue spectrum is
\[
  0,\ 0.09,\ 0.30,\ \mathbf{1.09},\ 1.27,\ 1.50,\ 1.50,\ 1.60,\ 1.65.
\]
The sharp jump between the third eigenvalue ($0.30$, governed by the two sparse bridges) and the fourth ($1.09$, governed by edges \emph{inside} the triangles) indicates $k=3$ communities --- the \emph{eigengap heuristic} for choosing $k$, plotted in Figure~\ref{ch33:fig-eigengap}. In the $(u_2,u_3)$ plane (Figure~\ref{ch33:fig-blobs}), each triangle collapses to a tight three-vertex group, and radial normalization places the three groups at widely separated angles where $3$-means recovers $C_1, C_2, C_3$.

\section{Complexity}
\label{ch33:complexity}

Running time is dominated by computing the bottom eigenvectors. For a sparse graph with $n$ vertices, $m$ edges, and target $k$ clusters,
an iterative eigensolver --- such as the Lanczos method or Locally Optimal Block Preconditioned Conjugate Gradient (LOBPCG), which find bottom eigenvectors by repeated sparse matrix--vector multiplications --- spends $\Oh(m)$ work per multiplication and requires $\Oh(1/\sqrt{\mathrm{gap}})$ iterations (up to logarithmic factors) when the relative eigenvalue gap $\mathrm{gap} = (\lambda_{k+1}^{\mathrm{nrm}} - \lambda_k^{\mathrm{nrm}})/\lambda_{k+1}^{\mathrm{nrm}}$ separates the $k$-th and $(k+1)$-st eigenvalues.
Building the sparse Laplacian takes $\Oh(n + m)$ time. The sweep in Algorithm~\ref{ch33:alg-bisection} sorts the $n$ coordinates in $\Oh(n\log n)$ time
and updates $\cut(A_k,B_k)$ and $\vol(A_k)$ incrementally as each vertex crosses into $A_k$ (scanning each incident edge once), so all $n-1$ sweep cuts cost
$\Oh(m)$ total rather than $\Oh(nm)$. Finally, $k$-means on $n$ points in $\R^k$ costs $\Oh(nkI)$ per run
of $I$ iterations.

\begin{complexitybox}
\textbf{Spectral bisection (Algorithm~\ref{ch33:alg-bisection}).} Normalized eigenpair $(\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}},u_2)$ by Lanczos:
$\widetilde\Oh(m / \sqrt{\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}})$ to constant relative accuracy (where $\widetilde\Oh(\cdot)$ hides $\poly(\log n)$ factors). Sweep rounding: $\Oh(m + n\log n)$.
\\[3pt]
\textbf{$k$-way clustering (Algorithm~\ref{ch33:alg-kway}).} Bottom $k$ eigenvectors: $\widetilde\Oh(mk/\sqrt{\mathrm{gap}})$.
Embedding and row-normalization: $\Oh(nk)$. $k$-means: $\Oh(nkI)$ per restart.
\\[3pt]
\textbf{Exact dense eigendecomposition} (small graphs): $\Oh(n^3)$ --- feasible up to a few thousand vertices.
\\[3pt]
Using nearly-linear-time Laplacian linear solvers (Spielman--Teng and successors, Chapter~41) inside inverse iteration reduces the eigenvector step to
$\widetilde\Oh(m)$ per eigenvector, making the pipeline nearly linear in the graph size.
\end{complexitybox}

\begin{applicationbox}
Spectral clustering applies whenever data can be represented as a similarity graph. \textbf{Image segmentation}: pixels are
vertices, edge weights measure color and spatial similarity, and $\NCut$ separates foreground objects from background (Shi--Malik).
\textbf{Community detection} in social and biological networks. \textbf{Mesh and circuit partitioning} for
parallel finite-element simulations and very-large-scale integration (VLSI) chip layout, where a balanced cut balances processor loads while minimizing wire or message traffic.
\textbf{Dimensionality reduction} (mapping high-dimensional data into a few coordinates that preserve local neighborhoods, often called \emph{manifold learning}): the \emph{Laplacian eigenmaps} method uses the bottom eigenvector coordinates of Algorithm~\ref{ch33:alg-kway} directly as low-dimensional features. \textbf{Document and gene-expression clustering} via
$k$-nearest-neighbor similarity graphs.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Building the graph: from data to similarity}
\label{ch33:graph-construction}

Often the input is not a graph at all, but $n$ feature vectors $x_1,\dots,x_n \in \R^d$. Clustering quality then depends directly on
how we turn pairwise distances $\norm{x_i - x_j}_2$ into a similarity graph. Three constructions are standard (Figure~\ref{ch33:fig-similarity-graphs}):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{$\eps$-neighborhood graph}: connect $(i,j)$ with an unweighted edge whenever $\norm{x_i-x_j}_2\le\eps$ for a chosen radius $\eps > 0$. This produces a sparse symmetric graph, but a single $\eps$ struggles when cluster densities vary: a radius small enough to separate two dense clusters can disconnect a looser cluster into isolated points.
  \item \textbf{$k$-nearest-neighbor ($k$-NN) graph}: connect each point $i$ to the $k$ other points closest to it, then make the directed edges undirected --- either keeping $(i,j)$ whenever $i$ is among $j$'s $k$ nearest \emph{or} $j$ is among $i$'s (the usual $k$-NN graph), or keeping $(i,j)$ only when \emph{both} hold (the mutual $k$-NN graph). Because degree adapts to local point spacing, $k$-NN is the standard default.
  \item \textbf{Fully connected Gaussian radial basis function (RBF) graph}: connect all pairs $i \ne j$ with weight
  \[
    w_{ij} \;=\; \exp\!\left(-\frac{\norm{x_i-x_j}_2^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).
  \]
  The \emph{bandwidth} $\sigma > 0$ sets the distance scale over which similarity decays toward zero.
\end{itemize}

\begin{trapbox}
The bandwidth $\sigma$ and neighbor count $k$ are user-chosen \emph{hyperparameters} (tuning settings fixed before running the algorithm), and they affect the output more than any detail of the eigensolver. Set $\sigma$ or $k$ too large and every point connects strongly to every other ($\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}$ grows large and cluster boundaries wash out); set them too small and the graph shatters into isolated single-vertex pieces ($\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}} \to 0$, producing artificial clusters). A practical default sets $\sigma$ to the median distance from each point to its $k$-th nearest neighbor (typically $k \approx 10$ or $k \approx \ceil{\log n}$), or uses a local per-point scale $\sigma_i$ equal to $x_i$'s $k$-th neighbor distance with $w_{ij} = \exp(-\norm{x_i-x_j}_2^2 / (\sigma_i\sigma_j))$ (Zelnik-Manor and Perona). When spectral clustering returns poor clusters, check the similarity graph before blaming the eigensolver.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Historical Context and Controversies}
\label{ch33:history-context}

\begin{historybox}
In the early 1970s, two research communities arrived at the same algebra independently. In Prague, Miroslav Fiedler studied elastic trusses (pin-jointed structural frames) and electrical resistor networks, proving in 1973 that the second-smallest Laplacian eigenvalue $\lambda_2$ vanishes if and only if the graph is disconnected, while the signs of its eigenvector split a connected graph along its bottleneck. Around the same time at IBM, William Donath and Alan Hoffman partitioned logic circuits across silicon chips to minimize inter-chip wire delays; because balanced partitioning is NP-hard, they used eigenvalues of adjacency and Laplacian matrices to derive polynomial-time bounds and heuristics.

Nearly three decades later, in 2000, Jianbo Shi and Jitendra Malik brought these ideas into computer vision to segment photographs into foreground and background objects. Local edge detectors struggled whenever texture or noise broke an object's contour; framing segmentation as a global normalized cut ($\NCut$) on a pixel-similarity graph solved the relaxation via the generalized eigenvector of $(\Lap, \mathbf{D})$. In 2001, Andrew Ng, Michael Jordan, and Yair Weiss introduced the row-normalized $k$-eigenvector pipeline (Algorithm~\ref{ch33:alg-kway}) and analyzed its stability via matrix perturbation bounds.

Spectral clustering overcame two classic failures of earlier methods. Unconstrained minimum cut from network flows often peels off a single leaf or outlier (an isolated data point far from the main groups) instead of splitting a network into balanced communities, while penalizing imbalance makes the discrete cut NP-hard. Conversely, running $k$-means directly on raw feature coordinates $x_i \in \R^d$ partitions space by straight hyperplanes, so it assumes clusters are roughly spherical and \emph{convex} (meaning the line segment joining any two points in a cluster stays inside that cluster) and fails on curved shapes such as concentric rings (Figure~\ref{ch33:fig-concentric-rings}). Continuous Laplacian relaxation avoids both failure modes, backed on the cut side by discrete \emph{isoperimetric inequalities} (bounds relating a set's boundary size to its volume, led by Cheeger's inequality).
\end{historybox}

\subsection{What We Can Now Do and Its Limitations}

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{What spectral clustering handles well}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Non-convex cluster shapes}: Mapping data into the bottom eigenspace of $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$ untangles curved or nested structures (such as concentric rings) into separated groups on the unit sphere where $k$-means succeeds.
  \item \textbf{Global continuous optimization}: Unlike local swap heuristics that move one vertex at a time and get stuck in \emph{local minima} (partitions where no single-vertex move helps even though a much better cut exists), the eigenvector relaxation finds its global continuous optimum in polynomial time.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Limitations}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Cost when the eigengap is tiny}: Dense eigendecomposition costs $\Oh(n^3)$ time, and sparse iterative solvers (Lanczos, LOBPCG) take $\widetilde\Oh(mk/\sqrt{\mathrm{gap}})$ time, slowing down when $\lambda_{k+1}^{\mathrm{nrm}} - \lambda_k^{\mathrm{nrm}}$ is near zero.
  \item \textbf{Cheeger's square-root loss}: When $\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}$ is tiny, the upper bound $\phi \le \sqrt{2\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}}$ leaves a wide interval: at $\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}} = 10^{-4}$, $\phi(\Gph)$ can sit anywhere between $0.5\times 10^{-4}$ and $\sqrt{2\times 10^{-4}} \approx 0.014$, a factor of $280$ above the lower bound.
  \item \textbf{Sensitivity of unnormalized $\Lap$ to hubs}: With the unnormalized Laplacian $\Lap$, a high-degree hub vertex can dominate $x^\top \Lap x$ and pull $v_2$ onto itself, isolating the hub rather than finding balanced communities (which is why $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$ is preferred).
\end{itemize}
\end{focusbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
The relaxations in this chapter are Rayleigh-quotient minimizations analyzed via the Courant--Fischer and Davis--Kahan theorems (companion \emph{Linear Algebra} volume), built on the graph Laplacian of Chapter~31 and the Cheeger bounds of Chapter~32. Next, \textbf{Chapter~34 (Expander Graphs)} studies the opposite regime where $\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}$ stays bounded away from zero so no sparse cut exists, while \textbf{Chapter~35 (Random Walks and Electrical Networks)} connects $\Lap_{\mathrm{rw}}$ to Markov chain mixing times and resistor networks. \textbf{Chapter~36 (PageRank and Markov Chains)} uses \emph{personalized random walks} (walks that periodically restart at a seed vertex) to find local clusters without scanning the whole graph, and \textbf{Chapter~41 (Laplacian Solvers and Electrical Flows)} develops the Spielman--Teng solvers that solve $\Lap x = b$ in $\widetilde\Oh(m)$ time. \end{connectionbox}

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch33:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Verify the Fiedler trace]
Compute the full eigendecomposition of the Laplacian of $\Gph_0$ (Figure~\ref{ch33:fig-running}) by hand or
by computer. Confirm $\lambda_2\approx0.44$ and that the Fiedler vector is antisymmetric under the
$L\leftrightarrow R$ mirror. Which eigenvalue and eigenvector correspond to the \emph{symmetric} mode, and
what cut, if any, does that mode suggest?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The barbell and the dumbbell]
Let $\Gph$ be two complete graphs (cliques) $K_t$ on $t$ vertices joined by a single edge (a barbell). Show $\lambda_2 = \Th(1/t^2)$ and that
the Fiedler vector cleanly separates the two cliques. Then replace the joining edge by a path of length
$\ell$ (a dumbbell). What happens to $\lambda_2$ and to the Fiedler partition as $\ell$ grows? Where does the
sweep cut the path?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Prove the easy direction of Cheeger]
Show $\phi(\Gph)\ge\lambda_2^{\mathrm{nrm}}/2$ directly: plug the indicator-derived test vector $h$ of the
optimal conductance set into the Rayleigh quotient of $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$ and bound it. Identify exactly
where the factor of $2$ is lost.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[RatioCut vs.\ NCut diverge]
Construct a graph on which the optimal RatioCut bipartition and the optimal NCut bipartition are
\emph{different}. (Hint: make one side have many low-degree vertices and the other few high-degree vertices,
so cardinality balance and volume balance disagree.) Compute both objectives for both partitions.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Sweep beats sign]
Exhibit a connected graph whose Fiedler vector has a sign split ($t=0$) that is a \emph{bad} cut, while some
non-zero sweep cut is good. Conclude that the sign threshold is not
safe to use alone, justifying the full sweep in Algorithm~\ref{ch33:alg-bisection}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Disconnected $=$ degenerate]
Show that if $\Gph$ has no isolated vertices and $c$ connected components, then $\lambda_1^{\mathrm{nrm}}=\dots=\lambda_c^{\mathrm{nrm}}=0$ and the
$0$-eigenspace of $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$ is spanned by $\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{1}_{C_1},\dots,\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{1}_{C_c}$ (while for $\Lap$ it is spanned by $\mathbf{1}_{C_1},\dots,\mathbf{1}_{C_c}$). Explain why $k$-way spectral clustering with $k=c$ then
recovers the components \emph{exactly}, for any choice of orthonormal basis in line 2 of
Algorithm~\ref{ch33:alg-kway}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Implement and break]
Implement Algorithm~\ref{ch33:alg-kway}. Run it on three Gaussian point clouds, then on two concentric
circles (where raw $k$-means fails, as in Figure~\ref{ch33:fig-concentric-rings}, while spectral clustering with a $k$-NN or Gaussian RBF graph
succeeds). Now \emph{break} the Gaussian RBF version: shrink $\sigma$ until the inner circle fragments into pieces. Report the eigenvalue
spectrum at the breaking point and explain it via Section~\ref{ch33:graph-construction}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Eigengap heuristic]
For the three-triangle chain of Section~\ref{ch33:kway}, compute the bottom five eigenvalues and exhibit the
gap between $\lambda_3$ and $\lambda_4$. Then add edges between the triangles until the gap closes; relate
the closing gap to the merging of clusters and to Cheeger's inequality.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Recursive vs.\ simultaneous]
Compare recursive spectral bisection (bisect, then bisect each half) against the simultaneous $k$-eigenvector
Algorithm~\ref{ch33:alg-kway} on a graph with three clusters of widely different sizes. Find an instance
where recursion makes the wrong first cut and never recovers, while the simultaneous method succeeds.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Normalized vs.\ unnormalized perturbation]
Take a graph with a few high-degree ``hub'' vertices. Show empirically that unnormalized spectral
clustering ($\Lap$) tends to isolate hubs while normalized clustering ($\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$) does not.
Explain using the role of $\mathbf{D}$ in the relaxation of Section~\ref{ch33:ncut}.
\end{exercise}
