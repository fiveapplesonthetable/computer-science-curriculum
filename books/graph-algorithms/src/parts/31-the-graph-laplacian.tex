\chapter{The Graph Laplacian}

In the preceding chapter, continuous semidefinite relaxations (optimization over matrices) solved discrete combinatorial problems on perfect graphs. How do we turn an arbitrary graph into a matrix whose algebra exposes its bottlenecks, clusters, and electrical behavior? Assign a real number $x_i$ to each vertex $i$ and measure how much those numbers disagree across every edge. That edge-by-edge disagreement defines the \emph{graph Laplacian} $\Lap$, an $n\times n$ matrix that translates questions about connectivity, smooth vertex signals, cuts, and resistor networks into linear equations and eigenvalues.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Think of a graph $\Gph=(V,E)$ on $n$ vertices as a network of springs, as in Figure~\ref{ch31:fig-springs}. Put a bead at each vertex and let bead $i$ slide vertically to height $x_i$ along a frictionless wire. Every edge $\{i,j\}\in E$ is an ideal spring of stiffness $w_{ij} > 0$ linking bead $i$ and bead $j$. Stack the heights into a column vector $x = (x_1,\dots,x_n)^\top \in \R^n$, where the superscript $\top$ denotes the \emph{transpose} (turning a column into a row, so $x^\top = (x_1,\dots,x_n)$). When the beads are held at heights $x$, the total elastic potential energy stored across all springs is
\[
  \frac{1}{2}\, x^\top \Lap x \;=\; \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\{i,j\}\in E} w_{ij}\,(x_i - x_j)^2 .
\]
Here $x^\top \Lap x$ multiplies the $1\times n$ row $x^\top$, the $n\times n$ matrix $\Lap$, and the $n\times 1$ column $x$ into a single number. Flat height assignments, where every bead sits at the same height $c$, stretch no springs and store zero energy; consequently $\Lap$ sends the all-ones vector $\mathbf{1} = (1,\dots,1)^\top$ to the zero vector $\mathbf{0} = (0,\dots,0)^\top$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{historybox}
The Laplacian matrix originated in nineteenth-century physics. In 1847, twenty-three-year-old
German physicist Gustav Kirchhoff was analyzing networks of electrical resistors driven by
batteries. Using Kirchhoff's Current and Voltage Laws to compute vertex voltages from injected currents, he found that the conductance matrix relating them (where conductance, the reciprocal of resistance, measures how easily current flows) took the form $\Lap = \mathbf{D} - \Adjm$, the degree matrix minus the adjacency matrix. Along the way, Kirchhoff proved the
\emph{Matrix--Tree Theorem}: every cofactor of $\Lap$ (the determinant of the $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ submatrix left after deleting one row and the matching column, where the determinant is the scalar volume factor that vanishes exactly on non-invertible matrices) equals the number of spanning
trees in the graph. The name \emph{Laplacian} comes from the continuous second-derivative operator $\Delta f$ in calculus, which measures how much a function deviates
from its local average; on a graph, $x^\top \Lap x = \sum_{\{i,j\}}(x_i - x_j)^2$ is the discrete counterpart of Dirichlet energy.

For over a century, mathematicians treated $\Lap$ mainly as a circuit tool and a determinant identity, while algebraic graph theorists studied the adjacency
matrix $\Adjm$. Yet $\Adjm$ has awkward quirks when degrees vary: isolated vertices add extra zero rows, and high-degree hubs stretch its \emph{eigenvalues} (the scalars $\lambda$ satisfying $\Adjm v = \lambda v$ for some nonzero \emph{eigenvector} $v$) in ways unrelated to graph bottlenecks.

The modern spectral viewpoint began in 1973, when Czech mathematician Miroslav Fiedler studied $\Lap$ through its \emph{quadratic form}, the degree-two polynomial $x^\top \Lap x = \sum_{\{i,j\}} (x_i - x_j)^2$. Because a sum of squares is never negative ($x^\top \Lap x \ge 0$ for all $x \in \R^n$), $\Lap$ has real, nonnegative eigenvalues $0 = \lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \dots \le \lambda_n$. Fiedler proved that the second-smallest eigenvalue
$\lambda_2$, which he named \emph{algebraic connectivity}, is strictly positive if and only
if the graph is connected, and that its eigenvector (the \emph{Fiedler vector}) lays vertices along a line so that tightly linked clusters stay close while bottlenecks stretch apart. In 2004,
Daniel Spielman and Shang-Hua Teng combined spectral ideas with graph decompositions to solve Laplacian linear systems $\Lap x = b$ in nearly linear time. Extending deterministic nearly linear-time solvers to directed, non-symmetric Laplacians remains an open research problem.
\end{historybox}

\section{Three definitions, one matrix}

Fix an undirected graph $\Gph=(V,E)$ with $n=\abs{V}$ vertices, labeled $1,\dots,n$, and $m=\abs{E}$ edges. For now take the graph unweighted and simple (no self-loops, no parallel edges); Section~\ref{ch31:weighted} adds edge weights.

Recall from Chapter~1 that the \emph{adjacency matrix} $\Adjm \in \R^{n\times n}$ has $\Adjm_{ij}=1$ when $\{i,j\}\in E$ and $0$ otherwise. Because edges are undirected and there are no self-loops, $\Adjm$ is \emph{symmetric} ($\Adjm_{ij} = \Adjm_{ji}$) with zeros along its main diagonal ($\Adjm_{ii}=0$). Pair it with the \emph{degree matrix} $\mathbf{D} \in \R^{n\times n}$, the \emph{diagonal} matrix whose diagonal entry $(\mathbf{D})_{ii} = \deg(i) = \sum_{j=1}^n \Adjm_{ij}$ is the degree of vertex $i$ and whose off-diagonal entries are all $0$.

\begin{definition}[Combinatorial Laplacian]\label{ch31:def-laplacian}
The \emph{Laplacian} of $\Gph$ is the $n\times n$ symmetric matrix
\[
  \Lap \;=\; \mathbf{D} - \Adjm,
  \qquad
  \Lap_{ij} \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    \deg(i) & \text{if } i = j,\\
    -1 & \text{if } \{i,j\}\in E,\\
    0 & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
\]
\end{definition}

Each diagonal entry holds a vertex's degree; each off-diagonal entry is $-1$ when the two vertices share an edge and $0$ otherwise. Every row sums to zero: the diagonal entry $\deg(i)$ cancels the $\deg(i)$ entries equal to $-1$ in row $i$. Multiplying $\Lap$ by the all-ones column vector $\mathbf{1} = (1,\dots,1)^\top$ sums each row, so $\Lap \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{0}$ for every graph.

\begin{example}[A five-vertex graph]\label{ch31:ex-running}
One five-vertex graph illustrates every construction in this chapter: a $4$-cycle $1\text{--}2\text{--}3\text{--}4\text{--}1$ with a \emph{pendant} vertex $5$ (a vertex of degree $1$) attached to vertex $3$, shown in Figure~\ref{ch31:fig-running}.

\subsection{The incidence matrix and \texorpdfstring{$\Lap = \mathbf{B} \mathbf{B}^\top$}{L = B B\^T}}

A second representation factors $\Lap$ into a matrix times its transpose, which explains why $x^\top \Lap x$ can never be negative. To write it down, orient each undirected edge arbitrarily by choosing one endpoint as its tail and the other as its head. This orientation is only a bookkeeping sign convention; the resulting product $\Lap$ does not depend on which direction we pick.

\begin{definition}[Incidence matrix]\label{ch31:def-incidence}
Orient each edge $e\in E$ from a tail $t(e)$ to a head $h(e)$. The signed vertex--edge \emph{incidence matrix} $\mathbf{B} \in \R^{n\times m}$ has one row per vertex and one column per edge, with
\[
  (\mathbf{B})_{v,e} \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    +1 & \text{if } v = h(e),\\
    -1 & \text{if } v = t(e),\\
     0 & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
\]
\end{definition}

Each column of $\mathbf{B}$ has a single $+1$ and a single $-1$: it equals $\mathbf{e}_{h(e)} - \mathbf{e}_{t(e)}$, where $\mathbf{e}_v \in \R^n$ is the $v$-th \emph{standard basis vector} ($1$ in coordinate $v$ and $0$ in all other coordinates). For any column vector $u \in \R^n$, its \emph{outer product} with itself is the $n\times n$ matrix $u u^\top$ with $(i,j)$ entry $u_i u_j$ (unlike the \emph{dot product} $u^\top u = \sum_{i=1}^n u_i^2$, which collapses to one number).

\begin{theorem}[Incidence factorization]\label{ch31:thm-bbt}
For any orientation of the edges, $\Lap = \mathbf{B} \mathbf{B}^\top$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Idea.} Multiplying $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^\top$ sums the outer product $\mathbf{b}_e \mathbf{b}_e^\top$ of each edge column $\mathbf{b}_e$ with itself (Figure~\ref{ch31:fig-edge-stamp}). For an edge $\{u,v\}$, $\mathbf{b}_e = \pm(\mathbf{e}_u - \mathbf{e}_v)$, and squaring the sign away gives $(\mathbf{e}_u-\mathbf{e}_v)(\mathbf{e}_u-\mathbf{e}_v)^\top$: an $n\times n$ ``edge stamp'' with $+1$ at $(u,u)$ and $(v,v)$, and $-1$ at $(u,v)$ and $(v,u)$. Summing these stamps over all edges accumulates $\deg(i)$ on the diagonal and $-1$ on each edge, recovering $\Lap$.
\end{whybox}

\begin{proof}
Write $\mathbf{b}_e \in \R^n$ for the column of $\mathbf{B}$ indexed by edge $e$, so $\mathbf{b}_e = \mathbf{e}_{h(e)} - \mathbf{e}_{t(e)}$. By the column-by-row rule for matrix multiplication,
\[
  \mathbf{B} \mathbf{B}^\top \;=\; \sum_{e\in E} \mathbf{b}_e \mathbf{b}_e^\top .
\]
For a single edge $e$ with endpoints $u$ and $v$ (in either orientation),
\[
  \mathbf{b}_e \mathbf{b}_e^\top
  \;=\; (\mathbf{e}_u - \mathbf{e}_v)(\mathbf{e}_u - \mathbf{e}_v)^\top
  \;=\; \mathbf{e}_u\mathbf{e}_u^\top + \mathbf{e}_v\mathbf{e}_v^\top - \mathbf{e}_u\mathbf{e}_v^\top - \mathbf{e}_v\mathbf{e}_u^\top .
\]
Reversing the arrow on $e$ negates $\mathbf{b}_e$, and $(-\mathbf{b}_e)(-\mathbf{b}_e)^\top = \mathbf{b}_e\mathbf{b}_e^\top$, so the orientation cancels out. Now sum over all $e \in E$. The term $\mathbf{e}_u\mathbf{e}_u^\top$ adds $+1$ to entry $(u,u)$ once for each edge incident to $u$, giving diagonal total $\deg(u)$. Because the graph is simple, at most one edge joins $u$ and $v$, so $-\mathbf{e}_u\mathbf{e}_v^\top - \mathbf{e}_v\mathbf{e}_u^\top$ puts $-1$ at $(u,v)$ and $(v,u)$ when $\{u,v\}\in E$ and $0$ otherwise. Thus $(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^\top)_{ii} = \deg(i)$ and $(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^\top)_{ij} = -1$ if and only if $\{i,j\}\in E$, matching Definition~\ref{ch31:def-laplacian}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The sum-over-edges form}

For any symmetric matrix $\mathbf{M} \in \R^{n\times n}$ and vector $x \in \R^n$, the scalar $x^\top \mathbf{M} x = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n \mathbf{M}_{ij} x_i x_j$ is called the \emph{quadratic form} of $\mathbf{M}$ because every term has degree two in the entries of $x$. Evaluating the quadratic form of $\Lap$ yields our third description.

\begin{theorem}[The quadratic form]\label{ch31:thm-quadform}
For every $x\in\R^n$,
\[
  x^\top \Lap x \;=\; \sum_{\{i,j\}\in E} (x_i - x_j)^2 \;\ge\; 0 .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $\norm{y}^2 = y^\top y = \sum_{k=1}^m y_k^2$ for the squared Euclidean length of a vector $y \in \R^m$. Applying Theorem~\ref{ch31:thm-bbt} and the transpose rule $(CD)^\top = D^\top C^\top$,
\begin{align*}
  x^\top \Lap x
  &\;=\; x^\top \mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^\top x && \text{(since $\Lap = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^\top$)}\\
  &\;=\; (\mathbf{B}^\top x)^\top (\mathbf{B}^\top x) && \text{(regrouping factors)}\\
  &\;=\; \norm{\mathbf{B}^\top x}^2 .
\end{align*}
In the coordinate of edge $e=(u\to v)$, the vector $\mathbf{B}^\top x \in \R^m$ has entry $\mathbf{b}_e^\top x = x_v - x_u$. Summing the squares of all $m$ entries gives
\[
  \norm{\mathbf{B}^\top x}^2
  \;=\; \sum_{e\in E} \bigl(x_{h(e)} - x_{t(e)}\bigr)^2
  \;=\; \sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}(x_i - x_j)^2 \;\ge\; 0,
\]
since each squared difference is nonnegative and independent of edge orientation.
\end{proof}

\begin{focusbox}
Three forms of the same matrix, used for three purposes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\Lap = \mathbf{D} - \Adjm$: for \emph{building} $\Lap$ from a graph and reading off entries.
  \item $\Lap = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^\top$: for \emph{proving} matrix properties (nonnegative energy, rank, and null space).
  \item $x^\top\Lap x = \sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}(x_i-x_j)^2$: for \emph{measuring} smoothness, cut sizes, and eigenvalues.
\end{itemize}
Memorize the third. Most estimates in spectral graph theory start by writing $x^\top \Lap x$ as a sum of squared edge differences and bounding that sum.
\end{focusbox}

\section{Smoothness: what \texorpdfstring{$x^\top \Lap x$}{x\^T L x} measures}

Read $x \in \R^n$ as a \emph{signal} on the vertices: a real number $x_i$ assigned to each vertex $i$. Theorem~\ref{ch31:thm-quadform} says $x^\top \Lap x$ penalizes disagreement across edges. A signal that is nearly constant inside each tightly connected cluster has a small quadratic form; a signal that jumps sharply between neighbors has a large one. That smoothness penalty is why $\Lap$ drives spectral clustering, semi-supervised learning (inferring missing vertex labels from a few known ones), mesh smoothing, and image segmentation.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Think of $x^\top \Lap x$ (normalized by $x^\top x$ so rescaling $x$ does not cheat) as a \emph{roughness meter}. Feed it a constant signal and the needle reads zero: no edge sees any disagreement. Feed it a signal that stays nearly flat within dense clusters and changes only across a sparse bottleneck, and the needle stays low. Feed it an alternating signal that flips sign across edges, and the needle swings toward its maximum. Ordering the eigenvectors of $\Lap$ from smallest eigenvalue to largest orders vertex signals from constant, to the gentlest zero-mean variation the graph permits, all the way up to the roughest oscillation (Figure~\ref{ch31:fig-smoothness}).
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{formalbox}
\textbf{Spectral foundations.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Eigenvectors and eigenvalues:} For a matrix $\Lap \in \R^{n\times n}$, a nonzero vector $v \in \R^n$ satisfying $\Lap v = \lambda v$ is an \emph{eigenvector}, and the scalar $\lambda$ is its \emph{eigenvalue}: multiplying $v$ by $\Lap$ scales $v$ by $\lambda$ without changing its line of direction.
  \item \textbf{Positive semidefiniteness (PSD):} A symmetric matrix is \emph{positive semidefinite}, written $\Lap \succeq 0$, if $x^\top \Lap x \ge 0$ for every $x\in\R^n$. Theorem~\ref{ch31:thm-quadform} wrote $x^\top \Lap x = \sum_{\{i,j\}\in E} (x_i - x_j)^2$ (or $\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E} w_{ij}(x_i - x_j)^2$ with positive edge weights $w_{ij}$) as a sum of squares, so every graph Laplacian is PSD. By the \emph{spectral theorem} of linear algebra, any real symmetric $n\times n$ PSD matrix has real, nonnegative eigenvalues
  \[
    0 \;\le\; \lambda_1 \;\le\; \lambda_2 \;\le\; \dots \;\le\; \lambda_n
  \]
  and an \emph{orthonormal eigenbasis} $u_1,\dots,u_n \in \R^n$: $n$ eigenvectors of unit length ($u_i^\top u_i = 1$) that are pairwise \emph{orthogonal} ($u_i^\top u_j = \sum_{k=1}^n (u_i)_k (u_j)_k = 0$ for $i\neq j$), so every vector $x\in\R^n$ expands uniquely as $x = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i u_i$ with $c_i = u_i^\top x$.
  \item \textbf{Rayleigh quotient:} For any nonzero vector $x \in \R^n$, the ratio
  \[
    R_{\Lap}(x) \;=\; \frac{x^\top \Lap x}{x^\top x} \;=\; \frac{\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E} w_{ij}(x_i - x_j)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2}
  \]
  divides the edge-disagreement energy by the squared length of $x$ (take $w_{ij}=1$ when unweighted). On an eigenvector $v$ with $\Lap v = \lambda v$, the Rayleigh quotient equals $(v^\top(\lambda v))/(v^\top v) = \lambda$.
  \item \textbf{Courant--Fischer minimum principle:} Over all nonzero vectors, the minimum of $R_{\Lap}(x)$ is $\lambda_1 = 0$, achieved at $x = \mathbf{1}$. Requiring $x$ to be orthogonal to $\mathbf{1}$ (written $x \perp \mathbf{1}$, meaning $\mathbf{1}^\top x = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i = 0$, so $x$ has mean zero) removes that constant mode and characterizes the second eigenvalue:
  \[
    \lambda_2 \;=\; \min_{\substack{x \perp \mathbf{1} \\ x\neq \mathbf{0}}} \frac{x^\top \Lap x}{x^\top x}.
  \]
\end{itemize}
To verify this formula on a connected graph, take an orthonormal eigenbasis with $u_1=\mathbf{1}/\sqrt n$. Any $x\perp\mathbf{1}$ has $c_1 = u_1^\top x = 0$, so $x=\sum_{i=2}^n c_i u_i$, $x^\top x = \sum_{i=2}^n c_i^2$, and $x^\top \Lap x = \sum_{i=2}^n \lambda_i c_i^2$; thus
\[
  R_{\Lap}(x) \;=\; \frac{\sum_{i=2}^n \lambda_i c_i^2}{\sum_{i=2}^n c_i^2} \;\ge\; \lambda_2,
\]
with equality at $x=u_2$.
\end{formalbox}

Thus $\lambda_2$ is the smallest Rayleigh quotient achievable by any nonzero zero-mean signal, and an eigenvector $v_2$ achieving it (the \emph{Fiedler vector}) is the smoothest way to assign numbers to vertices without making them all equal. Chapter~32 uses $v_2$ to partition graphs along narrow bottlenecks.

\begin{connectionbox}
This section applies the companion \emph{Linear Algebra} volume's treatment of \emph{symmetric PSD matrices and the spectral theorem}. Three imported facts do the work: (1) every real symmetric matrix has a real orthonormal eigenbasis; (2) a symmetric matrix $\mathbf{M}$ is PSD ($x^\top \mathbf{M} x \ge 0$ for all $x$) if and only if all of its eigenvalues are nonnegative; (3) the Rayleigh quotient $x^\top \mathbf{M} x / x^\top x$ ranges over $[\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}]$, with its subspace-constrained minima given by Courant--Fischer. On a graph, these algebraic quantities become combinatorial: eigenvalues measure connectivity, and eigenvectors give geometric layouts.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{trapbox}
$\Lap$ is positive \emph{semi}definite, never \emph{positive definite} (a matrix is positive definite when $x^\top \mathbf{M} x > 0$ for every nonzero $x$, equivalently when all eigenvalues are strictly positive), because $\Lap \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{0}$ always gives eigenvalue $0$. Consequently $\Lap$ is \emph{singular} (not invertible, with $\det \Lap = 0$). Algorithms that ``solve $\Lap x = b$'' solve on the zero-mean subspace $\mathbf{1}^\perp = \set{x\in\R^n : \mathbf{1}^\top x = 0}$ using the Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse $\Lap^{+}$ (the matrix that inverts every nonzero eigenvalue on its eigenspace while sending the null space to $\mathbf{0}$), and they require the compatibility condition $\mathbf{1}^\top b = 0$ (the entries of $b$ must sum to zero). Forgetting that $\Lap$ is singular is a common implementation bug. Chapter~35 develops $\Lap^{+}$ in detail.
\end{trapbox}

\section{The null space and connected components}

The \emph{null space} (or \emph{kernel}) of $\Lap$ is the set of vectors sent to zero, $\ker\Lap = \set{x\in\R^n : \Lap x = \mathbf{0}}$. The flat vector $\mathbf{1}$ lies in $\ker\Lap$ for every graph, and the entire kernel is built from flat vectors---one independent flat vector per connected component (Figure~\ref{ch31:fig-components-kernel}). A purely algebraic invariant, the matrix \emph{rank} $\rank\Lap$ (the number of linearly independent rows or columns of $\Lap$), therefore counts connected components.

\begin{theorem}[Kernel counts components]\label{ch31:thm-kernel}
Let $\Gph$ have connected components $C_1,\dots,C_k$. Then $\ker\Lap$ has dimension $k$ and is spanned by the component \emph{indicator vectors} $\mathbf{1}_{C_1},\dots,\mathbf{1}_{C_k}$, where $(\mathbf{1}_{C_\ell})_i = 1$ for $i\in C_\ell$ and $0$ otherwise. Equivalently, $\rank \Lap = n - k$. In particular, $\Gph$ is connected if and only if $\lambda_2 > 0$, and the multiplicity of the eigenvalue $0$ (the number of times $0$ appears among $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$) equals $k$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Idea.} $\Lap x = \mathbf{0}$ implies $x^\top \Lap x = 0$, and the sum-of-squares formula forces $x_i = x_j$ across every edge. A vertex signal that never changes across an edge must be constant on each connected component, while different components impose no constraints on each other. Thus $\ker\Lap$ is the $k$-dimensional space of signals that are constant on each component.
\end{whybox}

\begin{proof}
Write $\operatorname{span}\set{\mathbf{1}_{C_1},\dots,\mathbf{1}_{C_k}}$ for the set of all linear combinations $c_1\mathbf{1}_{C_1} + \dots + c_k\mathbf{1}_{C_k}$ with $c_1,\dots,c_k\in\R$. We show $\ker \Lap = \operatorname{span}\set{\mathbf{1}_{C_1},\dots,\mathbf{1}_{C_k}}$.

\textbf{($\supseteq$)} No edge crosses between distinct connected components, so for every edge $\{i,j\}\in E$, either both endpoints lie in $C_\ell$ (both entries of $\mathbf{1}_{C_\ell}$ equal $1$) or neither does (both equal $0$). Hence $(\mathbf{1}_{C_\ell})_i - (\mathbf{1}_{C_\ell})_j = 0$ on every edge, giving $\mathbf{1}_{C_\ell}^\top \Lap \mathbf{1}_{C_\ell} = 0$ by Theorem~\ref{ch31:thm-quadform}. For $\Lap = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^\top$, vanishing quadratic form implies vanishing $\Lap x$:
\[
  0 \;=\; x^\top \Lap x \;=\; \norm{\mathbf{B}^\top x}^2
  \;\implies\; \mathbf{B}^\top x = \mathbf{0}
  \;\implies\; \Lap x = \mathbf{B}(\mathbf{B}^\top x) = \mathbf{0}.
\]
Thus each $\mathbf{1}_{C_\ell}\in\ker\Lap$, and by linearity every combination in their span lies in $\ker\Lap$.

\textbf{($\subseteq$)} Suppose $\Lap x = \mathbf{0}$. Then $x^\top \Lap x = \sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}(x_i - x_j)^2 = 0$, forcing $x_i = x_j$ on every edge $\{i,j\}\in E$. Within any component $C_\ell$, every two vertices are joined by a path, and $x$ stays constant step by step along that path, so $x$ takes a single value $c_\ell$ across all of $C_\ell$. Consequently $x = \sum_{\ell=1}^k c_\ell \mathbf{1}_{C_\ell} \in \operatorname{span}\set{\mathbf{1}_{C_1},\dots,\mathbf{1}_{C_k}}$.

Because $C_1,\dots,C_k$ are pairwise disjoint, the vectors $\mathbf{1}_{C_1},\dots,\mathbf{1}_{C_k}$ have non-overlapping nonzero coordinates (\emph{disjoint supports}). Hence $\sum_{\ell=1}^k c_\ell \mathbf{1}_{C_\ell} = \mathbf{0}$ forces every $c_\ell = 0$, meaning the $k$ indicators are \emph{linearly independent} and $\dim\ker\Lap = k$. By the rank--nullity theorem ($\rank\Lap + \dim\ker\Lap = n$), $\rank\Lap = n-k$. For a symmetric matrix, the multiplicity of eigenvalue $0$ equals $\dim\ker\Lap = k$; since $0=\lambda_1\le\lambda_2\le\cdots\le\lambda_n$, having $k=1$ component is equivalent to $\lambda_2 > 0$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{An algorithm: counting components by Laplacian rank}

Theorem~\ref{ch31:thm-kernel} reduces counting connected components to computing $\rank\Lap$. On an explicit graph, BFS or DFS finds components in $\Oh(n+m)$ time, so nobody computes a matrix rank for that job in practice. Still, the reduction shows how linear algebra detects connectivity, and the Gaussian elimination inside Algorithm~\ref{ch31:alg-rank} (where $\Lap_{q,\bullet}$ denotes row $q$ of $\Lap$) is the starting point for fast Laplacian solvers.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{ComponentsByRank}$(\Gph)$ --- count components via $\dim\ker\Lap$}
\label{ch31:alg-rank}
\DontPrintSemicolon
\KwIn{Graph $\Gph=(V,E)$ on $n$ vertices.}
\KwOut{Number of connected components $k$.}
Build $\Lap = \mathbf{D} - \Adjm \in \R^{n\times n}$.\;
$r \gets 0$\tcp*{rank accumulator}
\For{$c \gets 1$ \KwTo $n$ (sweep columns)}{
  Find a pivot row $p$ with $\abs{\Lap_{p,c}}$ maximal among rows not yet used\;
  \If{$\abs{\Lap_{p,c}} > \tau$ \tcp*{numerical zero tolerance $\tau$}}{
    Mark row $p$ used; $r \gets r+1$\;
    \For{every unused row $q$}{
      $\Lap_{q,\bullet} \gets \Lap_{q,\bullet} - \dfrac{\Lap_{q,c}}{\Lap_{p,c}}\,\Lap_{p,\bullet}$ \tcp*{eliminate column $c$}
    }
  }
}
\Return $n - r$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{whybox}
Algorithm~\ref{ch31:alg-rank} performs Gaussian elimination with \emph{partial pivoting}: in each column $c$, it selects the unused row with largest $\abs{\Lap_{p,c}}$ as the \emph{pivot} (the nonzero entry used to clear column $c$ in all remaining rows). The total number of pivots found is $r = \rank\Lap$. By Theorem~\ref{ch31:thm-kernel}, $\rank\Lap = n-k$, so the $n-r$ columns without pivots count the $k$ free component values in $\ker\Lap$. Partial pivoting keeps rounding errors small so that tolerance $\tau$ separates true zeros from nonzeros.
\end{whybox}

\begin{complexitybox}
Building $\Lap$ takes $\Oh(n^2)$ time and space to initialize all entries plus $\Oh(m)$ to record the edges. Dense Gaussian elimination on an $n\times n$ matrix takes $\Oh(n^3)$ time and $\Oh(n^2)$ space, which dominates the total cost and makes \textsc{ComponentsByRank} $\Th(n^3)$. Compared to $\Oh(n+m)$ for BFS, dense elimination wastes work on zeros; Chapter~41 shows how exploiting sparsity cuts Laplacian solves down to nearly linear time.
\end{complexitybox}

\begin{labbox}
Trace \textsc{ComponentsByRank} on the running graph's $\Lap$ to watch $r = 4 = n-1$ emerge. Sweep columns $1$ through $5$ from left to right. Column $1$ pivots on $\Lap_{1,1} = 2$ in row $1$; adding $\tfrac12$ of row $1$ to rows $2$ and $4$ clears column $1$ and creates fill-in $-\tfrac12$ at $(2,4)$ and $(4,2)$ between vertex $1$'s two neighbors. Continuing through columns $2$, $3$, and $4$ yields four nonzero pivots and leaves row $5$ all zeros:
\[
  \Lap \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
     2 & -1 &  0 & -1 &  0\\[2pt]
    -1 &  2 & -1 &  0 &  0\\[2pt]
     0 & -1 &  3 & -1 & -1\\[2pt]
    -1 &  0 & -1 &  2 &  0\\[2pt]
     0 &  0 & -1 &  0 &  1
  \end{pmatrix}
  \;\xrightarrow{\text{eliminate}}\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    2 & -1 & 0 & -1 & 0\\[3pt]
    0 & \tfrac32 & -1 & -\tfrac12 & 0\\[3pt]
    0 & 0 & \tfrac73 & -\tfrac43 & -1\\[3pt]
    0 & 0 & 0 & \tfrac47 & -\tfrac47\\[3pt]
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The four pivots ($2,\tfrac32,\tfrac73,\tfrac47$) give $r=4$ and $k = n-r = 5-4 = 1$ component. The final zero row reflects the single null direction $\mathbf{1}$; deleting edge $\{3,5\}$ produces a second zero row and $k=2$.
\end{labbox}

\section{Weighted Laplacians}\label{ch31:weighted}

Most networks carry positive edge weights $w_{ij} > 0$, representing spring stiffness, electrical conductance, pipe capacity, or vertex similarity. All three descriptions of $\Lap$ extend directly to weighted graphs.

\begin{definition}[Weighted Laplacian]\label{ch31:def-weighted}
Let $w:E\to\R_{>0}$ assign a positive weight $w_{ij}$ to each edge $\{i,j\}\in E$. The \emph{weighted degree} of vertex $i$ is $d_i = \sum_{j:\{i,j\}\in E} w_{ij}$. The \emph{weighted adjacency matrix} $\Adjm$ has $\Adjm_{ij} = w_{ij}$ for $\{i,j\}\in E$ and $0$ otherwise, and the \emph{weighted degree matrix} $\mathbf{D}$ is diagonal with $(\mathbf{D})_{ii} = d_i$. The \emph{weighted Laplacian} is
\[
  \Lap \;=\; \mathbf{D} - \Adjm,
  \qquad
  \Lap_{ij} \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    d_i & \text{if } i=j,\\
    -w_{ij} & \text{if } \{i,j\}\in E,\\
    0 & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
\]
\end{definition}

The incidence factorization keeps the same unweighted signed incidence matrix $\mathbf{B} \in \R^{n\times m}$ and inserts the diagonal edge-weight matrix $\mathbf{W} = \operatorname{diag}(w_e)_{e\in E} \in \R^{m\times m}$ (which places the weights $w_e$ on its main diagonal and $0$ off it) in the middle.

\begin{theorem}[Weighted factorization and quadratic form]\label{ch31:thm-weighted}
With $\mathbf{B}$ and $\mathbf{W}$ as above,
\[
  \Lap \;=\; \mathbf{B}\, \mathbf{W}\, \mathbf{B}^\top
  \;=\; \sum_{e\in E} w_e\, \mathbf{b}_e\mathbf{b}_e^\top,
  \qquad
  x^\top \Lap x \;=\; \sum_{\{i,j\}\in E} w_{ij}\,(x_i - x_j)^2 \;\ge\; 0 .
\]
Consequently $\Lap$ is PSD, its kernel is spanned by the component indicators $\mathbf{1}_{C_1},\dots,\mathbf{1}_{C_k}$ as in Theorem~\ref{ch31:thm-kernel}, and $\tfrac12 x^\top\Lap x$ equals the spring-network energy with stiffnesses $w_{ij}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Because $\mathbf{W}$ is diagonal, $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{B}^\top = \sum_{e\in E} w_e\,\mathbf{b}_e\mathbf{b}_e^\top$: column $e$ of $\mathbf{B}$ is multiplied by $w_e$ before taking the outer product. Each stamp $w_e\,\mathbf{b}_e\mathbf{b}_e^\top$ adds $+w_e$ to the two diagonal entries of its endpoints and puts $-w_e$ on the two off-diagonal entries, summing to $d_i$ on the diagonal and $-w_{ij}$ on each edge. Writing $\mathbf{W}^{1/2} = \operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{w_e})_{e\in E}$, we have $\mathbf{W} = (\mathbf{W}^{1/2})^\top \mathbf{W}^{1/2}$, so
\[
  x^\top \Lap x
  \;=\; x^\top \mathbf{B}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{B}^\top x
  \;=\; \norm{\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\mathbf{B}^\top x}^2
  \;=\; \sum_{e\in E} w_e \bigl(x_{h(e)} - x_{t(e)}\bigr)^2
  \;=\; \sum_{\{i,j\}\in E} w_{ij}(x_i - x_j)^2 .
\]
Because every $w_{ij}>0$, each term $w_{ij}(x_i - x_j)^2$ vanishes if and only if $x_i = x_j$, so the kernel proof of Theorem~\ref{ch31:thm-kernel} carries over unchanged.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A weighted twist on the running graph]\label{ch31:ex-weighted}
Keep the graph of Figure~\ref{ch31:fig-running}, give the four cycle edges weight $2$, and give the pendant edge $\{3,5\}$ weight $5$:
\[
  w_{12}=w_{23}=w_{34}=w_{14}=2,\qquad w_{35}=5.
\]
Then $d_1=d_2=d_4 = 2+2 = 4$, $d_3 = 2+2+5 = 9$, $d_5 = 5$, and
\[
  \Lap \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
     4 & -2 &  0 & -2 &  0\\
    -2 &  4 & -2 &  0 &  0\\
     0 & -2 &  9 & -2 & -5\\
    -2 &  0 & -2 &  4 &  0\\
     0 &  0 & -5 &  0 &  5
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Every row sums to zero, so $\Lap\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}$. Evaluate the quadratic form on $x = (1,1,1,1,0)^\top$, which sets the cycle to $1$ and vertex $5$ to $0$:
\[
  x^\top \Lap x \;=\; \sum_{\{i,j\}\in E} w_{ij}(x_i-x_j)^2 \;=\; w_{35}(1-0)^2 \;=\; 5,
\]
because $\{3,5\}$ is the only edge whose endpoints disagree; every cycle edge contributes $0$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Cuts as quadratic forms}

Evaluating $x^\top \Lap x$ on $0/1$ vectors turns cuts directly into algebra. Given a vertex subset $S\subseteq V$, write $\bar S = V\setminus S$ for its complement (all vertices not in $S$), and let $\mathbf{1}_S \in \set{0,1}^n$ be the indicator vector of $S$. Substituting $x = \mathbf{1}_S$ into Theorem~\ref{ch31:thm-weighted} gives
\[
  \mathbf{1}_S^\top \Lap\, \mathbf{1}_S
  \;=\; \sum_{\{i,j\}\in E} w_{ij}\bigl((\mathbf{1}_S)_i - (\mathbf{1}_S)_j\bigr)^2 .
\]
The squared difference $\bigl((\mathbf{1}_S)_i - (\mathbf{1}_S)_j\bigr)^2$ equals $1$ when one endpoint lies in $S$ and the other lies in $\bar S$, and $0$ when both lie on the same side. Writing $w(S,\bar S) = \sum_{\{i,j\}\in E:\, i\in S,\, j\in\bar S} w_{ij}$ for the total weight of edges crossing the cut $(S,\bar S)$, we obtain
\[
  \mathbf{1}_S^\top \Lap\, \mathbf{1}_S \;=\; w(S,\bar S),
\]
so $\mathbf{1}_S^\top \Lap\, \mathbf{1}_S$ equals the cut weight (Figure~\ref{ch31:fig-cut-quadform}). Relaxing the discrete constraint $x \in \set{0,1}^n$ to a continuous vector $x \in \R^n$ with $x\perp\mathbf{1}$ turns combinatorial cut minimization into the eigenvalue problem for $\lambda_2$; \emph{Cheeger's inequality} (a theorem bounding a graph's narrowest balanced cut from both sides using $\lambda_2$, proved in Chapter~32) controls how much the continuous relaxation can lose.

\begin{applicationbox}
Where the Laplacian smoothness penalty appears in practice:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Spectral clustering and image segmentation.} Minimizing a size-balanced version of $\mathbf{1}_S^\top\Lap\mathbf{1}_S$ over subsets $S$ is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard; relaxing $\mathbf{1}_S$ to the Fiedler vector $v_2$ yields a polynomial-time partition certified by Cheeger's inequality. Shi and Malik's normalized-cuts algorithm segments images this way.
  \item \textbf{Semi-supervised learning.} Given known labels on a small subset of vertices, \emph{harmonic extension} (Section~\ref{ch31:sec-harmonic}) minimizes $x^\top\Lap x$ subject to matching those labels, assigning each unlabeled vertex the weighted average of its neighbors via one linear solve.
  \item \textbf{Graph drawing.} Plotting vertex $i$ at planar coordinates $\bigl((v_2)_i, (v_3)_i\bigr)$ from the second and third eigenvectors produces a \emph{spectral layout} that keeps adjacent vertices close while separating clusters.
  \item \textbf{Mesh processing.} In computer graphics, the \emph{cotangent Laplacian} sets edge weights $w_{ij}$ from triangle angles on a 3D surface mesh to smooth noisy scans, flatten surfaces onto 2D texture maps, and simulate heat diffusion.
\end{itemize}
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Eigenvalues of small graphs, by hand}

Computing the spectra of paths, cycles, stars, and complete graphs makes the eigenvalue bounds concrete; Figure~\ref{ch31:fig-small-spectra} compares all four on five vertices. Write $\mathbf{I}$ for the $n\times n$ \emph{identity matrix} ($1$ on the diagonal and $0$ off it).

\begin{example}[The cycle and the path]\label{ch31:ex-path-cycle}
On the $n$-vertex cycle $C_n$ (with vertices $0,1,\dots,n-1$ connected in a ring modulo $n$), every vertex has degree $2$, so $\Lap = 2\mathbf{I} - \Adjm$ and row $j$ of $\Lap x$ computes $2x_j - x_{j-1} - x_{j+1}$. Test a discrete cosine wave $c^{(\omega)}_j = \cos(2\pi \omega j/n)$ of integer frequency $\omega \in \set{0,1,\dots,n-1}$. Using $\cos(\alpha-\theta)+\cos(\alpha+\theta) = 2\cos\alpha\cos\theta$ with $\alpha = 2\pi\omega j/n$ and $\theta = 2\pi\omega/n$,
\begin{align*}
  \bigl(\Lap c^{(\omega)}\bigr)_j
  &\;=\; 2\cos\!\Bigl(\frac{2\pi\omega j}{n}\Bigr) - \cos\!\Bigl(\frac{2\pi\omega(j-1)}{n}\Bigr) - \cos\!\Bigl(\frac{2\pi\omega(j+1)}{n}\Bigr)\\
  &\;=\; \Bigl(2 - 2\cos\!\Bigl(\frac{2\pi\omega}{n}\Bigr)\Bigr) c^{(\omega)}_j .
\end{align*}
The sine waves $s^{(\omega)}_j = \sin(2\pi\omega j/n)$ satisfy the same identity (in complex shorthand, both combine via Euler's formula $e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ into the discrete Fourier modes $v^{(\omega)}_j = e^{2\pi i \omega j/n}$). Unsorted by size, the $n$ mode eigenvalues are
\[
  \mu_\omega \;=\; 2 - 2\cos\!\Bigl(\frac{2\pi\omega}{n}\Bigr), \qquad \omega = 0,1,\dots,n-1 .
\]
Frequency $\omega = 0$ gives the constant mode $\lambda_1 = \mu_0 = 0$. Frequencies $\omega=1$ and $\omega=n-1$ share the next smallest value (spanned by $\cos(2\pi j/n)$ and $\sin(2\pi j/n)$), so in sorted $1$-based order the algebraic connectivity has multiplicity $2$ (for $n\ge 3$) and equals
\[
  \lambda_2 \;=\; 2 - 2\cos\!\Bigl(\frac{2\pi}{n}\Bigr) \;\approx\; \frac{4\pi^2}{n^2} \quad \text{for large $n$},
\]
using $1-\cos\theta \approx \theta^2/2$. A long cycle therefore has $\lambda_2 = \Th(1/n^2)$: cutting just two opposite edges splits the ring in half. On the $n$-vertex path $P_n$, removing one cycle edge leaves cosine waves that flatten out at the two degree-$1$ endpoints, giving sorted eigenvalues $\lambda_{k} = 2 - 2\cos\bigl(\pi(k-1)/n\bigr)$ for $k=1,\dots,n$, with $\lambda_2 = 2 - 2\cos(\pi/n) \approx \pi^2/n^2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The complete graph and the star]\label{ch31:ex-kn-star}
In the complete graph $K_n$, every pair of distinct vertices shares an edge, so each diagonal entry of $\Lap$ is $n-1$ and each off-diagonal entry is $-1$. Using the all-ones matrix $\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top$, we can write $\Lap = n\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top$. We already have $\Lap\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{0}$; for any nonzero vector $x \perp \mathbf{1}$ (meaning $\mathbf{1}^\top x = 0$),
\[
  \Lap x \;=\; (n\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top)x \;=\; nx - \mathbf{1}(\mathbf{1}^\top x) \;=\; nx .
\]
Because the subspace $\mathbf{1}^\perp$ has dimension $n-1$, the eigenvalues of $K_n$ are $0$ (once) and $n$ (with multiplicity $n-1$). The large gap $\lambda_2 = n$ reflects that no cheap cut exists in $K_n$. By contrast, the star $K_{1,n-1}$ (one central hub joined to $n-1$ leaves, with $n\ge 3$) has eigenvalues $0$ (once), $1$ (with multiplicity $n-2$), and $n$ (once): detaching any single leaf costs only one edge ($\lambda_2 = 1$), while oscillating the hub against all leaves gives $\lambda_n = n$.
\end{example}

\section{Basic spectral facts to carry forward}

Five identities follow from the three definitions of $\Lap$ and recur throughout the spectral chapters.

\begin{proposition}[Spectral toolbox]\label{ch31:prop-toolbox}
Let $\Lap$ be the weighted Laplacian of $\Gph$ with eigenvalues $0=\lambda_1\le\cdots\le\lambda_n$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i = \operatorname{tr}\Lap = \sum_{i=1}^n d_i = 2\sum_{e\in E} w_e$ (twice the total edge weight).
  \item $\lambda_n \le 2\max_i d_i$, and $\lambda_n \ge \max_i d_i + 1$ for unweighted graphs with at least one edge.
  \item $\lambda_2 > 0$ if and only if $\Gph$ is connected, and the multiplicity of $0$ equals the number of connected components.
  \item (Monotonicity) Adding an edge or increasing an edge weight cannot decrease any eigenvalue: if $\Lap' = \Lap + w\,\mathbf{b}_e\mathbf{b}_e^\top$ with $w\ge0$, then $\lambda_i(\Lap') \ge \lambda_i(\Lap)$ for all $i=1,\dots,n$.
  \item (Kirchhoff's Matrix--Tree Theorem) For a connected graph, any cofactor of $\Lap$ equals $\tfrac1n \prod_{i=2}^{n}\lambda_i$, which equals $\sum_{T} \prod_{e\in T} w_e$ summed over all spanning trees $T$ of $\Gph$; in particular, when $\Gph$ is unweighted ($w_e = 1$), $\tfrac1n \prod_{i=2}^{n}\lambda_i$ counts the spanning trees of $\Gph$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketches]
(1) Definition~\ref{ch31:def-weighted} puts $d_i$ on the diagonal, so $\operatorname{tr}\Lap = \sum_{i=1}^n d_i = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i$. Each edge $\{i,j\}$ adds $w_{ij}$ to both $d_i$ and $d_j$, giving $\sum_{i=1}^n d_i = 2\sum_{e\in E} w_e$.

(2) By Gershgorin's circle theorem, every eigenvalue lies within $\sum_{j\neq i}\abs{\Lap_{ij}} = d_i$ of some diagonal entry $d_i$, so $\lambda_n \le 2\max_i d_i$. For an unweighted graph with maximum degree $\Delta \ge 1$, Cauchy's eigenvalue interlacing theorem (which bounds the eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix using those of any principal submatrix obtained by keeping a subset of rows and matching columns) relates $\Lap$ to the star centered at a degree-$\Delta$ vertex and yields $\lambda_n \ge \Delta + 1$.

(3) Proved in Theorem~\ref{ch31:thm-kernel}.

(4) The term $w\,\mathbf{b}_e\mathbf{b}_e^\top$ is PSD of rank one (all of its columns are scalar multiples of $\mathbf{b}_e$), so $x^\top \Lap' x = x^\top \Lap x + w(\mathbf{b}_e^\top x)^2 \ge x^\top \Lap x$ for every $x\in\R^n$. Raising the Rayleigh quotient at every vector raises every subspace min--max value in the general Courant--Fischer theorem (which expresses $\lambda_i$ as the minimum over $i$-dimensional subspaces of the maximum Rayleigh quotient on that subspace), an instance of Weyl's monotonicity theorem.

(5) Deleting one row and matching column of $\Lap = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{B}^\top$ and expanding the determinant via the Cauchy--Binet formula (which writes $\det(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{C}^\top)$ as the sum of squared determinants of maximal square submatrices of $\mathbf{C}$) produces one term $\prod_{e\in T} w_e$ for each spanning tree $T$ and $0$ for non-trees. Relating the characteristic polynomial's linear coefficient to the sum of cofactors gives $\tfrac1n\prod_{i=2}^n \lambda_i$.
\end{proof}

\begin{connectionbox}
Item (5) links this chapter to three others. Cauchy--Binet on $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{B}^\top$ proves Kirchhoff's Matrix--Tree Theorem above; Chapter~35 (\emph{Random Walks and Electrical Networks}) interprets $\Lap^{+}$ through effective resistance and proves that an edge's effective resistance in an unweighted graph equals the fraction of spanning trees containing it; and Karger's random-contraction bound (Chapter~11) also admits an effective-resistance view through $\Lap$. The companion \emph{Linear Algebra} volume develops the spectral theorem, Courant--Fischer, Weyl monotonicity, and the Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse behind these results.
\end{connectionbox}

\subsection{The normalized Laplacian}

When vertex degrees span several orders of magnitude, as in social or web graphs, raw degrees can dominate $\Lap$. Assuming every vertex has $d_i > 0$ (no isolated vertices), write $\mathbf{D}^{-1/2} = \operatorname{diag}(1/\sqrt{d_i})$ and $\mathbf{D}^{-1} = \operatorname{diag}(1/d_i)$. Two standard rescalings divide out the degrees:
\[
  \Lap_{\mathrm{sym}} \;=\; \mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\,\Lap\,\mathbf{D}^{-1/2} \;=\; \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\Adjm\,\mathbf{D}^{-1/2},
  \qquad
  \Lap_{\mathrm{rw}} \;=\; \mathbf{D}^{-1}\Lap \;=\; \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{D}^{-1}\Adjm .
\]
Here $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$ is symmetric PSD with all eigenvalues in $[0,2]$, while $\Lap_{\mathrm{rw}} = \mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}$ subtracts the random-walk transition matrix $\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{D}^{-1}\Adjm$ (whose entry $P_{ij} = w_{ij}/d_i$ is the probability of stepping from $i$ to $j$ proportional to edge weight). Because $\Lap_{\mathrm{rw}} = \mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}\mathbf{D}^{1/2}$, the two matrices are \emph{similar} (they represent the same linear transformation in rescaled coordinates) and therefore have identical eigenvalues. Setting $y=\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}x$ in Theorem~\ref{ch31:thm-weighted} gives the quadratic form of $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$:
\[
  x^\top \Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}\, x
  \;=\; y^\top \Lap\, y
  \;=\; \sum_{\{i,j\}\in E} w_{ij}\Bigl(\frac{x_i}{\sqrt{d_i}} - \frac{x_j}{\sqrt{d_j}}\Bigr)^2,
\]
measuring smoothness relative to vertex degrees. Cheeger's inequality and random-walk mixing bounds use this normalized spectrum; Chapters~32, 33, and~35 develop it in full.

\begin{trapbox}
$\Lap_{\mathrm{rw}} = \mathbf{D}^{-1}\Lap$ is \emph{not symmetric} whenever degrees differ, so do not pass it to a symmetric eigensolver or assume its eigenvectors are orthogonal. Because $\Lap_{\mathrm{rw}} = \mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}\mathbf{D}^{1/2}$, an eigenvector $u$ of $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$ converts into a right eigenvector $v = \mathbf{D}^{-1/2}u$ of $\Lap_{\mathrm{rw}}$ with the same eigenvalue: solve on the symmetric $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$, then rescale. Also remember that $\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}$ divides by $\sqrt{d_i}$, so any isolated vertices ($d_i = 0$) must be handled separately before normalizing.
\end{trapbox}

\section{A worked solve: harmonic extension}\label{ch31:sec-harmonic}

To see a Laplacian linear system at work, consider \emph{harmonic extension}, the problem behind both semi-supervised label propagation and resistor-network voltages. Fix the values of $x$ on a nonempty subset $B\subset V$ of \emph{boundary} (labeled) vertices and choose the remaining values on the \emph{interior} $I = V\setminus B$ to minimize $x^\top\Lap x$. At the minimum, $x$ is \emph{harmonic} on $I$: every interior vertex takes the weighted average of its neighbors.

\begin{theorem}[Harmonic extension]\label{ch31:thm-harmonic}
Reorder the vertices so interior $I$ precedes boundary $B$, partitioning $\Lap$ and $x$ into blocks:
\[
  \Lap \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} \Lap_{II} & \Lap_{IB}\\ \Lap_{BI} & \Lap_{BB}\end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  x \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} x_I \\ x_B \end{pmatrix}.
\]
With $x_B$ fixed, $x^\top\Lap x$ is minimized by the unique solution of $\Lap_{II}\,x_I = -\Lap_{IB}\,x_B$, provided every connected component of $\Gph$ contains at least one vertex of $B$ (which makes $\Lap_{II}$ invertible). At the optimum, $(\Lap x)_i = 0$ for every $i\in I$; writing $j\sim i$ for $\{i,j\}\in E$, each interior vertex satisfies the weighted-average condition
\[
  d_i x_i \;=\; \sum_{j\sim i} w_{ij} x_j, \qquad \text{or} \qquad x_i \;=\; \frac{1}{d_i}\sum_{j\sim i} w_{ij} x_j .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Using block multiplication and symmetry ($\Lap_{BI}=\Lap_{IB}^\top$), expand $f(x_I) = x^\top\Lap x$ as a function of the unknown interior vector $x_I$:
\[
  f(x_I) \;=\; x_I^\top\Lap_{II}x_I + 2 x_I^\top\Lap_{IB}x_B + x_B^\top\Lap_{BB}x_B .
\]
For any vector $z_I \in \R^{\abs{I}}$, padding $z_I$ with zeros on $B$ gives $z = (z_I^\top, \mathbf{0}^\top)^\top$ with $z_I^\top \Lap_{II} z_I = z^\top \Lap z \ge 0$, so $\Lap_{II} \succeq 0$. Moreover, if $z_I^\top \Lap_{II} z_I = 0$, then $z^\top \Lap z = 0$, making $z$ constant on each component of $\Gph$ by Theorem~\ref{ch31:thm-kernel}; since every component meets $B$, where $z$ is zero, we get $z_I = \mathbf{0}$. Thus $\Lap_{II}$ is strictly positive definite and invertible. Because $f(x_I)$ bowls upward (a strictly convex quadratic), its unique global minimum occurs where its \emph{gradient} $\nabla_{x_I} f$ (the vector of partial derivatives $\partial f / \partial x_i$ for $i\in I$) equals $\mathbf{0}$:
\[
  \nabla_{x_I} f(x_I) \;=\; 2\Lap_{II}x_I + 2\Lap_{IB}x_B \;=\; \mathbf{0}
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \Lap_{II}x_I \;=\; -\Lap_{IB}x_B .
\]
That equation says the $I$-block of $\Lap x$ is $\Lap_{II}x_I + \Lap_{IB}x_B = \mathbf{0}$, so $(\Lap x)_i = d_i x_i - \sum_{j\sim i}w_{ij}x_j = 0$ for every $i\in I$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Solving on the running graph]\label{ch31:ex-harmonic}
Take the unweighted $\Lap$ of Example~\ref{ch31:ex-running}. Pin the boundary $B=\set{1,5}$ at $x_1 = 0$ and $x_5 = 1$ (vertex $1$ grounded at $0$ volts, vertex $5$ held at $1$ volt), and solve for the interior $I=\set{2,3,4}$. Extracting rows $2,3,4$ and columns $2,3,4$ (for $\Lap_{II}$) or columns $1,5$ (for $\Lap_{IB}$) gives
\[
  \Lap_{II} \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0\\ -1 & 3 & -1\\ 0 & -1 & 2\end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  -\Lap_{IB}x_B \;=\; -\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0\\ 0 & -1\\ -1 & 0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}
  \;=\; \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Writing out the three rows of $\Lap_{II}x_I = (0,1,0)^\top$ gives
\[
  2x_2 = x_3,\qquad 3x_3 = x_2 + x_4 + 1,\qquad 2x_4 = x_3 .
\]
From the first and third equations, $x_2 = x_4 = \tfrac12 x_3$. Substituting into the middle equation yields
\begin{align*}
  3x_3 &\;=\; \tfrac12 x_3 + \tfrac12 x_3 + 1 \;=\; x_3 + 1,\\
  x_3  &\;=\; \tfrac12, \qquad x_2 \;=\; x_4 \;=\; \tfrac14 .
\end{align*}
The harmonic extension is $x = (0,\ \tfrac14,\ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac14,\ 1)^\top$, drawn in Figure~\ref{ch31:fig-harmonic}. At interior vertex $3$, the three neighbors $2,4,5$ carry values $\tfrac14,\tfrac14,1$, whose average is $\tfrac13(\tfrac14 + \tfrac14 + 1) = \tfrac12 = x_3$. Physically, $x$ gives the steady voltages when a $1$-volt source is connected across unit resistors between vertices $1$ and $5$. Summing squared voltage drops across all five unit resistors gives
\[
  x^\top \Lap x
  \;=\; (0-\tfrac14)^2 + (\tfrac14-\tfrac12)^2 + (\tfrac12-\tfrac14)^2 + (\tfrac14-0)^2 + (\tfrac12-1)^2
  \;=\; \tfrac{1}{16} + \tfrac{1}{16} + \tfrac{1}{16} + \tfrac{1}{16} + \tfrac{1}{4}
  \;=\; \tfrac12 .
\]
Under a $1$-volt drop, dissipated power equals $x^\top\Lap x = C_{\mathrm{eff}}(1,5) = 1/R_{\mathrm{eff}}(1,5)$, so the effective conductance between vertices $1$ and $5$ is $\tfrac12$ and its reciprocal, the \emph{effective resistance}, is $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(1,5) = 2$ (two parallel length-$2$ paths across the $4$-cycle give resistance $1$, in series with edge $\{3,5\}$ of resistance $1$; see Chapter~35).
\end{example}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{HarmonicExtend}$(\Lap, B, x_B)$ --- smoothest interior given boundary}
\label{ch31:alg-harmonic}
\DontPrintSemicolon
\KwIn{Laplacian $\Lap$; boundary set $B$ with fixed values $x_B$; interior $I = V\setminus B$.}
\KwOut{Signal $x$ minimizing $x^\top\Lap x$ with $x_i = (x_B)_i$ for all $i\in B$.}
Form blocks $\Lap_{II}$ (rows/cols $I$) and $\Lap_{IB}$ (rows $I$, cols $B$).\;
Assert every component of $\Gph$ meets $B$ \tcp*{else $\Lap_{II}$ is singular}
Solve the symmetric positive definite (SPD) system $\Lap_{II}\, x_I = -\Lap_{IB}\, x_B$ \tcp*{Cholesky, conjugate gradient, or fast solver}
$x \gets$ interleave $x_I$ and $x_B$ back into original vertex order\;
\Return $x$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{complexitybox}
\textsc{HarmonicExtend} solves one SPD system of dimension $\abs{I}\le n$. Dense \emph{Cholesky factorization} (factoring $\Lap_{II} = \mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}$ into triangular matrices) takes $\Oh(\abs{I}^3)$ time. Iterative \emph{conjugate gradient} (CG) multiplies sparse $\Lap_{II}$ by one vector per step and converges to relative error $\eps$ in $\Oh\bigl(m\sqrt{\kappa}\log(1/\eps)\bigr)$ time, where $\kappa = \lambda_{\max}(\Lap_{II})/\lambda_{\min}(\Lap_{II})$ is the \emph{condition number} (large $\kappa$ means $\Lap_{II}$ is close to singular and convergence slows). Spielman and Teng (2004) and subsequent work achieve \emph{nearly linear} time $\Oh\bigl(m\log^{c}n\log(1/\eps)\bigr)$ by \emph{preconditioning} (transforming the system with an easy-to-invert approximation of $\Lap_{II}$ to shrink $\kappa$) using \emph{spectral sparsifiers} (sparse reweighted subgraphs that preserve $x^\top\Lap x$) or \emph{low-stretch spanning trees} (spanning trees whose tree paths approximate graph distances on average). Chapter~41 builds those solvers.
\end{complexitybox}

\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{What This Made Possible:}
The graph Laplacian bridges discrete graphs, circuit physics, and numerical linear algebra. It underlies Kirchhoff's Matrix--Tree Theorem, Tutte's spring embeddings (placing interior vertices at neighbor averages to draw planar graphs without edge crossings), electrical analyses of random walks (relating effective resistance to \emph{commute times}, the expected round-trip walk steps between two vertices), and nearly linear-time solvers for \emph{symmetric diagonally dominant} (SDD) systems (symmetric matrices where each diagonal entry is at least the sum of absolute off-diagonal entries in its row).

\textbf{Limitations:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Continuous relaxation gap:} Solving $\Lap x = b$ or $\Lap v = \lambda v$ returns real coordinates $x \in \R^n$. Rounding continuous coordinates back into a discrete cut $(S,\bar S)$ incurs an approximation gap governed by Cheeger's inequality.
  \item \emph{Eigenvector sensitivity:} Adding an edge of weight $w$ shifts each eigenvalue by at most $2w$, yet individual eigenvectors can be unstable: when two eigenvalues are nearly equal, a small edge perturbation can rotate their eigenvectors within the shared subspace by a large angle.
  \item \emph{Numerical overhead on basic tasks:} For reachability, components, or unweighted shortest paths, BFS and DFS run in $\Oh(n+m)$ time using simple integer arrays, whereas computing Laplacian eigenvectors requires floating-point iterative methods such as the Lanczos algorithm.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Where We Go Next:}
We proved that $\lambda_2 > 0$ if and only if $\Gph$ is connected. What does the numerical size of $\lambda_2$ tell us about the narrowest bottleneck in $\Gph$? In \textbf{Chapter~32 (Spectral Graph Theory)}, we prove the discrete Cheeger inequality, showing that sweeping a threshold across the Fiedler vector extracts a provably sparse cut.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{exercise}[Build it]\label{ch31:ex-build}
Write down the Laplacian of the $3\times3$ grid graph (nine vertices, edges between horizontally or vertically adjacent cells). Verify by hand that every row sums to zero and that the diagonal is the degree sequence $(2,3,2,3,4,3,2,3,2)$. Confirm $\operatorname{tr}\Lap = 2m$ by counting edges.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Orientation independence]\label{ch31:ex-orient}
Prove directly from Definition~\ref{ch31:def-incidence} that $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^\top$ does not depend on the chosen edge orientations, without invoking Theorem~\ref{ch31:thm-bbt}. (Hint: flipping one edge negates one column of $\mathbf{B}$; show the outer product is unchanged.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Disconnect and watch the rank]\label{ch31:ex-rank-drop}
Take the running graph (Example~\ref{ch31:ex-running}) and delete edges until it has three connected components. Predict $\dim\ker\Lap$ and $\rank\Lap$ before computing, then verify by row-reducing the new $\Lap$. Identify the three indicator vectors spanning the kernel.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Cut = quadratic form]\label{ch31:ex-cut-form}
For the weighted graph of Example~\ref{ch31:ex-weighted}, compute $\mathbf{1}_S^\top\Lap\,\mathbf{1}_S$ for $S = \set{1,2}$, $S=\set{5}$, and $S=\set{1,2,4}$. Check each against the total weight of edges crossing the cut, and find the minimum-weight cut by testing all $2^4-1$ proper nonempty vertex subsets containing vertex $1$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Cycle spectrum]\label{ch31:ex-cycle}
Verify the cycle eigenvalue formula $\mu_\omega = 2-2\cos(2\pi\omega/n)$ of Example~\ref{ch31:ex-path-cycle} for $C_4$ by computing the $4\times4$ Laplacian's characteristic polynomial $\det(\Lap - \lambda\mathbf{I})$. Confirm the sorted spectrum is $\set{0,2,2,4}$, exhibit two orthogonal Fiedler vectors for $\lambda_2 = 2$, and explain from the symmetry of $C_4$ why $\lambda_2$ has multiplicity $2$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Fiedler vector by hand]\label{ch31:ex-fiedler-hand}
For the running graph, we found $\lambda_2 \approx 0.83$ with the Fiedler vector taking its extreme coordinate at vertex $5$. Set up the eigenproblem $\Lap x = \lambda x$ with $x\perp\mathbf{1}$, use the symmetry $x_2=x_4$ to eliminate variables, and reduce to a cubic polynomial in $\lambda$. Solve for $\lambda_2$ to two decimal places and confirm that the signs of $v_2$ separate the pendant from the cycle.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[PSD without incidence]\label{ch31:ex-psd-direct}
Prove $\Lap\succeq 0$ directly from Definition~\ref{ch31:def-laplacian} ($\Lap = \mathbf{D}-\Adjm$) by expanding and completing the square in
\[
  x^\top\Lap x \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^n \deg(i)\,x_i^2 \;-\; 2\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E} x_i x_j
\]
to recover $\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}(x_i-x_j)^2$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Weighted monotonicity]\label{ch31:ex-monotone}
Prove Proposition~\ref{ch31:prop-toolbox}(4): if $\Lap' = \Lap + w\,\mathbf{b}_e\mathbf{b}_e^\top$ with $w\ge0$, then $\lambda_i(\Lap')\ge\lambda_i(\Lap)$ for all $i$. Use the Courant--Fischer min--max formula. Then give a graph where adding one edge strictly increases $\lambda_2$ and another where adding one edge leaves $\lambda_2$ unchanged.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Harmonic = electrical]\label{ch31:ex-electrical}
Recompute the harmonic extension of Example~\ref{ch31:ex-harmonic}, grounding vertex $4$ instead of vertex $1$ ($x_4=0$, $x_5=1$). Interpret the result as voltages in a unit-resistor network, compute the dissipated power $x^\top\Lap x$, and deduce the effective resistance $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(4,5) = (x^\top\Lap x)^{-1}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Normalized quadratic form]\label{ch31:ex-normalized}
Assuming $d_i > 0$ for all $i$, verify from $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}} = \mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\Lap\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}$ that
\[
  x^\top\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}x \;=\; \sum_{\{i,j\}\in E} w_{ij}\Bigl(\frac{x_i}{\sqrt{d_i}} - \frac{x_j}{\sqrt{d_j}}\Bigr)^2 .
\]
Conclude that $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}\succeq 0$ and that its smallest eigenvalue is $0$ with eigenvector $\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{1}$. Why does $\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{1}$, rather than $\mathbf{1}$, play the role of the constant mode for $\Lap_{\mathrm{sym}}$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Spectral lower bound on cuts]\label{ch31:ex-spectral-cut}
Let $S\subset V$ be a vertex subset of size $\abs{S}=s$ in an unweighted graph. Using $\mathbf{1}_S^\top\Lap\,\mathbf{1}_S = w(S,\bar S)$ and the Rayleigh quotient bound for $\lambda_2$, prove that
\[
  w(S,\bar S) \;\ge\; \lambda_2 \cdot \frac{s(n-s)}{n} .
\]
(Hint: subtract the mean $(s/n)\mathbf{1}$ from $\mathbf{1}_S$ to project onto $\mathbf{1}^\perp$, then apply $R_{\Lap}(x) \ge \lambda_2$.) This gives a lower bound on every cut from $\lambda_2$; Cheeger's inequality supplies the converse upper bound.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Implement and time it]\label{ch31:ex-implement}
Implement \textsc{ComponentsByRank} (Algorithm~\ref{ch31:alg-rank}) and compare its running time against BFS on random graphs of increasing size $n$, confirming the $\Th(n^3)$ versus $\Oh(n+m)$ scaling. Then store $\Lap$ as a sparse matrix, solve a \textsc{HarmonicExtend} instance with conjugate gradient, and observe how the iteration count grows with $\lambda_n/\lambda_2$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Trace the elimination]\label{ch31:ex-elim-trace}

\begin{exercise}[Spring energy minimization]\label{ch31:ex-springs}
Use the spring model of Figure~\ref{ch31:fig-springs} on a triangle $K_3$ with unit-stiffness springs. Pin beads $1$ and $3$ at heights $x_1 = 0$ and $x_3 = 1$, and let bead $2$ settle freely. Write the elastic energy $\tfrac12 x^\top\Lap x$ as a quadratic function of $x_2$, minimize it, and check that the minimizer is the harmonic average $x_2=\tfrac12$. Relate $x^\top\Lap x$ at the minimum to the effective resistance $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(1,3)$ between the two pinned beads.
\end{exercise}
