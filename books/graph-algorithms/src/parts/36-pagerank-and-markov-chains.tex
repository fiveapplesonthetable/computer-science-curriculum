\chapter{PageRank and Markov Chains on Graphs}
\label{ch:pagerank}

A web crawler (a program that follows links to collect web pages) faces a ranking problem once it has fetched billions of pages: a single search word might appear on hundreds of thousands of them. To decide which page a searcher should see first, we can treat the web as a directed graph and model a random surfer who clicks links step after step. Pages the surfer visits often count as important. That long-run fraction of steps spent at each page is the \emph{stationary distribution} of a Markov chain (Chapter~35): a random walk whose next move depends only on its current vertex. Computing this distribution on a web-sized graph gives each page its PageRank score.

\begin{connectionbox}
Chapters~31--35 studied random walks on \emph{undirected} graphs, where every edge can be crossed both ways and the walk behaves like electric current in a resistor network. Hyperlinks on the web are one-way arrows, so that symmetry disappears: a page can receive thousands of links without linking back. This chapter adapts the matrix and eigenvalue ideas from Chapters~31--35 (together with one positivity theorem recalled from the companion \emph{Probability} volume) to rank vertices in directed graphs, both globally across the whole network and locally around a single starting vertex.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{The web as a graph}
\label{ch36:web-graph}

Model the web as a directed graph $\Gph = (V, E)$. Each page is a vertex in $V$, and a hyperlink from page $u$ to page $v$ is a directed edge $u \to v$, also written $(u,v) \in E$. Let $n = \abs{V}$ be the number of pages and $m = \abs{E}$ be the number of links. For a vertex $u$, its \emph{out-degree} $\deg^+(u)$ is the number of links leaving $u$ (its \emph{out-links}), and its \emph{in-degree} $\deg^-(u)$ is the number of links pointing into $u$ (its \emph{in-links}). Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-web4} shows a four-page example that we will trace throughout the chapter.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Set page text aside for a moment. A link $u \to v$ acts as an endorsement: ``$u$ considers $v$ worth visiting.'' Yet endorsements are not equal. A link from a widely cited page should count for more than a link from an obscure one. That definition looks circular because importance is defined in terms of importance, and the random-surfer model turns the circle into a linear system with a single computable answer.
\end{intuitionbox}

Ranking items by weighted endorsements predates the web: Pinski and Narin used weighted citation counts (\emph{influence weights}) to rank scientific journals in 1976, and \emph{eigenvector centrality} (scoring each vertex in proportion to the sum of the scores of vertices pointing to it) goes back to sociology in the 1950s. Page and Brin added the random-surfer model, whose occasional random resets handle dead ends and closed loops while keeping the computation fast on billions of pages.

\section{The random-surfer model}
\label{ch36:surfer}

Picture a surfer standing on page $u$. At each step, if $\deg^+(u) \ge 1$, they pick one of the $\deg^+(u)$ outgoing links \emph{uniformly at random} (meaning each outgoing link has equal probability $1/\deg^+(u)$) and follow it. Where the surfer stands after $t$ steps is a random variable. Its \emph{probability distribution} is a column vector $x_t \in \R^n$ with entries
\[
  x_t(v) \;=\; \Pr[\text{surfer is at page } v \text{ at step } t],
\]
so every $x_t(v) \ge 0$ and $\sum_{v \in V} x_t(v) = 1$. Any column vector with nonnegative entries summing to $1$ is called a \emph{probability vector}.

\subsection{The transition matrix}

Collect the one-step link probabilities into the \emph{transition matrix} $\mathbf{P} \in \R^{n\times n}$ defined by
\[
  \mathbf{P}_{vu} \;=\; \Pr[u \to v \text{ in one step}] \;=\;
  \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{\deg^+(u)} & \text{if } (u,v) \in E,\\[1ex] 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
\]
Column $u$ of $\mathbf{P}$ lists the surfer's next-step probabilities when starting at $u$. A matrix of nonnegative entries whose columns each sum to $1$ is called \emph{column-stochastic}. When every page has at least one outgoing link ($\deg^+(u) \ge 1$ for all $u$), every column of $\mathbf{P}$ sums to $1$ and $\mathbf{P}$ is column-stochastic. In that case, let $\Adjm$ be the directed adjacency matrix ($\Adjm_{uv}=1$ if and only if $u\to v$, and $0$ otherwise) and let $\mathbf{D}=\operatorname{diag}(\deg^+(u))$ be the $n \times n$ diagonal matrix with out-degrees on the diagonal. Taking the transpose $\Adjm^\top$ places the edge $u \to v$ in row $v$, column $u$, so
\[
  \mathbf{P} \;=\; \Adjm^\top \mathbf{D}^{-1}.
\]
Because we store distributions as column vectors $x_t$, one step of the walk updates the distribution by
\[
  x_{t+1} \;=\; \mathbf{P}\, x_t.
\]
(Chapter~35 wrote distributions as row vectors multiplying a row-stochastic matrix whose rows sum to $1$; for PageRank we use column vectors and column-stochastic matrices throughout.)

\begin{example}[Transition matrix of Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-web4}]
\label{ch36:ex-Pmatrix}
Order the vertices $A,B,C,D$. The outgoing links are $A\to\{B,C\}$, $B\to\{A,D\}$, $C\to\{D\}$, and $D\to\{B,C\}$, giving
\[
  \mathbf{P} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0 & \tfrac12 & 0 & 0\\[4pt]
    \tfrac12 & 0 & 0 & \tfrac12\\[4pt]
    \tfrac12 & 0 & 0 & \tfrac12\\[4pt]
    0 & \tfrac12 & 1 & 0
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  \begin{array}{l}
  \text{column } A:\ \text{probability splits equally to } B,C\\[3pt]
  \text{column } B:\ \text{probability splits equally to } A,D\\[3pt]
  \text{column } C:\ \text{all probability goes to } D\\[3pt]
  \text{column } D:\ \text{probability splits equally to } B,C.
  \end{array}
\]
Every column sums to $1$, confirming that $\mathbf{P}$ is column-stochastic.
\end{example}

\subsection{Two diseases: dangling nodes and traps}

On a real web graph, following raw links runs into two structural problems that ruin the ``long-run fraction of time'' definition.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Dangling nodes (rank sinks).} A page with no outgoing links ($\deg^+(u)=0$) produces an all-zero column in $\mathbf{P}$. A surfer who lands there has nowhere to go: total probability $\sum_v x_t(v)$ drops below $1$, and $\mathbf{P}$ is not column-stochastic. PDF files, image files, and pages the crawler has discovered but not yet fetched are all dangling nodes.

\textbf{Spider traps and cycles.} Even when every page has $\deg^+(u) \ge 1$, a subset of pages with no links leaving the subset absorbs all probability that ever enters it. Multiple such traps mean the chain is \emph{reducible} (not every page can reach every other page, so the graph is not strongly connected), creating multiple competing stationary distributions. Worse, a trap or graph can be \emph{periodic}, meaning the surfer can return to a page only after multiples of some fixed step count $d \ge 2$ (such as bouncing back and forth between two pages $p \leftrightarrow q$ on odd and even steps), so $x_t$ oscillates forever instead of settling down.
\end{trapbox}

Both problems share one fix: \textbf{teleportation}, allowing the surfer to occasionally ignore links and jump to a page chosen across the whole web. Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-diseases} shows both failure modes alongside their teleportation escape routes.

\section{PageRank as a stationary distribution}
\label{ch36:stationary}

Give the surfer a biased coin that lands heads with probability $\alpha \in (0,1)$, called the \emph{damping factor} (classically $\alpha=0.85$), and fix a \emph{teleport probability vector} $v \in \R^n$ (with $v_i \ge 0$ and $\sum_{i=1}^n v_i = 1$). Let $\mathbf{1} = (1,1,\dots,1)^\top \in \R^n$ denote the all-ones column vector; in classic PageRank, $v = \tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}$, which chooses every page with equal probability $1/n$. At each step:
\begin{itemize}
  \item if the current page is dangling ($\deg^+(u) = 0$), \emph{teleport} with probability $1$ by drawing the next page from $v$;
  \item otherwise, with probability $\alpha$ follow a uniformly random out-link, and with probability $1-\alpha$ teleport by drawing the next page from $v$.
\end{itemize}
Algebraically, replacing each all-zero column of $\mathbf{P}$ by the probability vector $v$ produces a column-stochastic matrix $\mathbf{P}'$. Including the $\alpha$ coin flip then gives the \emph{Google matrix}:
\[
  \boxed{\;\mathbf{G} \;=\; \alpha\, \mathbf{P}' \;+\; (1-\alpha)\, v\,\mathbf{1}^\top.\;}
\]
Here $v\,\mathbf{1}^\top$ is the $n \times n$ \emph{rank-one} outer-product matrix whose $(i,j)$ entry is $v_i \cdot 1 = v_i$, so every column of $v\,\mathbf{1}^\top$ equals $v$: regardless of where the surfer starts, teleporting lands them on pages distributed as $v$. (Notice that for a dangling column $u$, column $u$ of $\mathbf{P}'$ is already $v$, so column $u$ of $\mathbf{G}$ is $\alpha v + (1-\alpha)v = v$, matching ``teleport with probability $1$.'')

The \emph{PageRank vector} $\pi \in \R^n$ is the stationary distribution of $\mathbf{G}$:
\[
  \boxed{\;\mathbf{G}\,\pi = \pi, \qquad \pi \ge 0, \qquad \mathbf{1}^\top \pi = 1,\;}
\]
where $\pi \ge 0$ means $\pi_i \ge 0$ for every page $i$, and $\mathbf{1}^\top \pi = \sum_{i=1}^n \pi_i = 1$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
A fraction $1-\alpha$ of the time, the surfer ignores links and resets to a random page drawn from $v$. When every entry of $v$ is positive, this single rule cures both diseases at once: dangling nodes get an exit, closed traps leak a $1-\alpha$ fraction of their probability on every step, and periodic cycles lose their lockstep rhythm because the surfer can jump from any page to any page in a single step. Every entry of $\mathbf{G}$ becomes strictly greater than zero, forcing a single positive stationary distribution $\pi$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Why a unique stationary distribution exists}

\begin{theorem}[Existence and uniqueness of PageRank]
\label{ch36:thm-existence}
For any $0 < \alpha < 1$ and any probability vector $v > 0$ (meaning $v_i > 0$ for all $i$), the Google matrix $\mathbf{G}$ has a unique stationary distribution $\pi$, every entry of $\pi$ satisfies $\pi_i > 0$, and $\pi$ is the unique eigenvector of $\mathbf{G}$ for eigenvalue $1$ up to scalar multiples.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
Unlike the symmetric Laplacians of Chapters~31--35, directed walk matrices need not be symmetric, so their eigenvalues $\lambda = a + bi \in \C$ can be complex numbers with \emph{modulus} (complex magnitude) $\abs{\lambda} = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$. Also distinguish \emph{right eigenvectors} ($\mathbf{M}x = \lambda x$) from \emph{left eigenvectors} ($y^\top \mathbf{M} = \lambda y^\top$, or $\mathbf{M}^\top y = \lambda y$).

The proof applies the \emph{Perron--Frobenius theorem} from linear algebra. Call a square nonnegative matrix $\mathbf{M}$ \emph{primitive} if some positive integer power $\mathbf{M}^k$ has all entries strictly positive, and let its \emph{spectral radius} $\rho(\mathbf{M}) = \max_i \abs{\lambda_i}$ be the largest modulus among its eigenvalues. Perron--Frobenius states that for any primitive matrix $\mathbf{M}$, $\rho(\mathbf{M})$ is itself a real positive eigenvalue, it is \emph{simple} (a single root of the characteristic polynomial, so its eigenspace has dimension $1$), no other eigenvalue has modulus equal to $\rho(\mathbf{M})$, and its eigenvector can be chosen with all entries strictly positive.
\end{whybox}

\begin{proof}
Because $v_i > 0$ for all $i$, $\alpha < 1$, and $\mathbf{P}'_{ij} \ge 0$, every entry of $\mathbf{G} = \alpha\mathbf{P}' + (1-\alpha)v\mathbf{1}^\top$ satisfies
\[
  \mathbf{G}_{ij} \;\ge\; (1-\alpha)v_i \;>\; 0.
\]
Hence $\mathbf{G}^1 = \mathbf{G}$ is already strictly positive, so $\mathbf{G}$ is primitive. Next verify that $\mathbf{G}$ is column-stochastic, using $\mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{P}' = \mathbf{1}^\top$ and $\mathbf{1}^\top v = 1$:
\begin{align*}
  \mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{G}
  &= \alpha\,\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{P}' + (1-\alpha)(\mathbf{1}^\top v)\mathbf{1}^\top \\
  &= \alpha\,\mathbf{1}^\top + (1-\alpha)\mathbf{1}^\top \\
  &= \mathbf{1}^\top.
\end{align*}
Thus $\mathbf{1}$ is a left eigenvector of $\mathbf{G}$ with eigenvalue $1$. Moreover, no eigenvalue of a column-stochastic matrix can exceed $1$ in modulus: if $\mathbf{G}z = \lambda z$ with $z \ne \mathbf{0}$, the triangle inequality gives
\[
  \abs{\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^n \abs{z_i}
  \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^n \abs{(\mathbf{G}z)_i}
  \;\le\; \sum_{j=1}^n \Big(\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{G}_{ij}\Big)\abs{z_j}
  \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^n \abs{z_j},
\]
so $\abs{\lambda} \le 1$. Therefore $\rho(\mathbf{G}) = 1$. By the Perron--Frobenius theorem, $1$ is a simple eigenvalue, it is the sole eigenvalue of modulus $1$, and its right eigenspace is spanned by a strictly positive vector. Scaling that vector so $\mathbf{1}^\top \pi = 1$ gives the unique stationary distribution $\pi > 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{connectionbox}
A Markov chain that is both irreducible (every state can reach every other) and aperiodic (no fixed return rhythm) is called \emph{ergodic}; in the companion \emph{Probability} volume, Perron--Frobenius proves that every finite ergodic chain converges to a unique stationary distribution. Because $\mathbf{G}$ has strictly positive entries, every page reaches every other page in a single step, making the PageRank walk ergodic regardless of how disconnected the underlying web links are.
\end{connectionbox}

\subsection{Handling dangling nodes without densifying}

On a web of $n = 10^9$ pages averaging $10$ links per page, $\mathbf{P}$ has only $10^{10}$ nonzero entries, whereas an explicit $n \times n$ dense matrix has $10^{18}$ entries and cannot fit in memory. Writing $v$ directly into every dangling column of $\mathbf{P}$, or forming $\mathbf{G}$ explicitly, would destroy sparsity.

Instead, we keep $\mathbf{P}$ sparse and handle both dangling nodes and teleportation with one scalar per step. Let $D = \{u \in V : \deg^+(u) = 0\}$ be the set of dangling pages, and let $d \in \{0,1\}^n$ be its indicator vector ($d_u = 1$ if $u \in D$ and $d_u = 0$ otherwise). Then $\mathbf{P}' = \mathbf{P} + v\, d^\top$. Whenever $x$ is a probability vector ($\mathbf{1}^\top x = 1$), multiplying $\mathbf{G}$ by $x$ expands as
\begin{align*}
  \mathbf{G}\,x
  &= \alpha(\mathbf{P} + v\,d^\top)x + (1-\alpha)v(\mathbf{1}^\top x) \\
  &= \alpha\mathbf{P}x + \alpha(d^\top x)\,v + (1-\alpha)v \\
  &= \alpha\mathbf{P}x \;+\; \big(\alpha\,(d^\top x) + (1-\alpha)\big)\,v.
\end{align*}
Here $s = d^\top x = \sum_{u \in D} x_u$ is the total probability currently sitting on dangling pages, a single sum. Notice why the pooled teleport weight is $\beta = \alpha s + (1-\alpha)$: non-dangling pages carry mass $1 - s$ and teleport at rate $1 - \alpha$, while dangling pages carry mass $s$ and teleport at rate $1$, and $(1-\alpha)(1-s) + 1 \cdot s = \alpha s + (1-\alpha)$. Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-google-step} shows how each step routes probability through $\mathbf{P}$ and this scalar pool without ever building $\mathbf{P}'$ or $\mathbf{G}$.

\section{Power iteration and convergence}
\label{ch36:power}

Inverting an $n \times n$ matrix directly is out of reach when $n$ is in the billions. Instead, we run the surfer's update step repeatedly from the starting vector $x^{(0)} = v$:
\[
  x^{(0)} = v, \qquad x^{(k+1)} = \mathbf{G}\, x^{(k)}.
\]
Repeatedly multiplying a vector by a matrix to converge to its dominant eigenvector is called \emph{power iteration}. To decide when to stop, we measure the change between consecutive iterates using the \emph{$\ell_1$ norm} $\norm{w}_1 = \sum_{i=1}^n \abs{w_i}$ (the sum of absolute values of a vector's entries), halting once $\norm{x^{(k+1)} - x^{(k)}}_1 \le \eps$ for a chosen tolerance $\eps > 0$. Below, $\mathbf{0} = (0,0,\dots,0)^\top$ denotes the all-zeros vector.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{PageRank by power iteration (sparse, dangling-aware)}
\label{ch36:alg-pagerank}
\KwIn{Out-adjacency lists of $\Gph$; damping $\alpha$; teleport vector $v$; tolerance $\eps$}
\KwOut{PageRank vector $\pi$}
$x \gets v$\;
\Repeat{$\norm{x - x_{\mathrm{old}}}_1 \le \eps$}{
  $x_{\mathrm{old}} \gets x$\;
  $y \gets \mathbf{0}$ \tcp*{accumulate $\alpha\,\mathbf{P}x$}
  \ForEach{vertex $u$ with $\deg^+(u) > 0$}{
    \ForEach{$u \to w$}{ $y_w \gets y_w + \alpha\, x_u / \deg^+(u)$\;}
  }
  $s \gets \sum_{u \in D} x_u$ \tcp*{dangling mass}
  $\beta \gets \alpha\, s + (1-\alpha)$ \tcp*{redistributed via teleport}
  $x \gets y + \beta\, v$ \tcp*{$\mathbf{1}^\top x = 1$ automatically}
}
\Return $x$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{complexitybox}
Each iteration scans every edge once and every vertex a constant number of times, using $\Th(n + m)$ time and $\Th(n)$ working space for $x$ and $y$. Theorem~\ref{ch36:thm-converge} below shows that reaching $\ell_1$ error $\eps$ takes $\Oh\!\big(\log(1/\eps) / \log(1/\alpha)\big)$ iterations, \emph{independent of $n$}. At $\alpha = 0.85$, each step shrinks the error by a factor of $0.85$, so $50$--$100$ iterations give $4$--$7$ accurate decimal digits on a graph of any size (reaching double-precision machine accuracy near $10^{-16}$ takes about $230$ iterations). Total time: $\Oh\!\big((n+m)\,\log(1/\eps)/\log(1/\alpha)\big)$.
\end{complexitybox}

\subsection{The convergence rate is the damping factor}

The reason iteration count does not grow with $n$ is that teleportation shrinks every non-stationary eigenvalue of $\mathbf{P}'$ by a factor of $\alpha$, creating a spectral gap of at least $1-\alpha$ regardless of the graph's link structure. Here the \emph{subdominant eigenvalue} means the eigenvalue of largest modulus after $\lambda_1 = 1$.

\begin{theorem}[Subdominant eigenvalue]
\label{ch36:thm-eigen}
Let $\lambda_1 = 1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_n$ be the eigenvalues of the column-stochastic matrix $\mathbf{P}'$, ordered so $1 \ge \abs{\lambda_2}\ge\dots\ge\abs{\lambda_n}$. Then the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{G} = \alpha\mathbf{P}' + (1-\alpha)v\mathbf{1}^\top$ are $1$ together with $\alpha\lambda_2, \alpha\lambda_3, \dots, \alpha\lambda_n$. In particular, the subdominant eigenvalue of $\mathbf{G}$ has modulus at most $\alpha$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $W=\{w \in \R^n : \mathbf{1}^\top w=0\}$ be the $(n-1)$-dimensional subspace of zero-sum vectors. Column-stochasticity gives $\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{P}'=\mathbf{1}^\top$, so for any $w \in W$ we have $\mathbf{1}^\top(\mathbf{P}'w) = \mathbf{1}^\top w = 0$, meaning $\mathbf{P}'$ maps $W$ into $W$. Moreover, on $W$ the rank-one teleport matrix vanishes:
\[
  \mathbf{G}w \;=\; \alpha\mathbf{P}'w + (1-\alpha)v\,(\mathbf{1}^\top w) \;=\; \alpha\mathbf{P}'w \in W.
\]
Choose any eigenvector $\pi'$ of $\mathbf{P}'$ for eigenvalue $1$ normalized so $\mathbf{1}^\top \pi' = 1$ (one exists because $\mathbf{P}'$ is column-stochastic and therefore has at least one stationary probability distribution). Complete $\pi'$ with any basis $w_2,\dots,w_n$ of $W$, and let $\mathbf{B} \in \R^{(n-1)\times(n-1)}$ be the matrix representing the restriction of $\mathbf{P}'$ to $W$ in that basis. Because $\mathbf{P}'\pi' = \pi'$ and $\mathbf{G}\pi'$ has coordinate sum $\mathbf{1}^\top(\mathbf{G}\pi') = \mathbf{1}^\top \pi' = 1$ (so $\mathbf{G}\pi' = 1\cdot\pi' + u_0$ for some $u_0 \in W$), the matrices of $\mathbf{P}'$ and $\mathbf{G}$ in the basis $\{\pi', w_2, \dots, w_n\}$ take the block forms
\[
  [\mathbf{P}'] \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 & \mathbf{0}^\top \\
    \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{B}
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  [\mathbf{G}] \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 & \mathbf{0}^\top \\
    u & \alpha\mathbf{B}
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The determinant of a block-triangular matrix is the product of the determinants of its diagonal blocks: $\det(\lambda\mathbf{I} - [\mathbf{P}']) = (\lambda - 1)\det(\lambda\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{B})$ has roots $1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_n$, so $\mathbf{B}$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_2,\dots,\lambda_n$; therefore $\det(\lambda\mathbf{I} - [\mathbf{G}]) = (\lambda - 1)\det(\lambda\mathbf{I} - \alpha\mathbf{B})$ has roots $1, \alpha\lambda_2, \dots, \alpha\lambda_n$. Because this argument uses a single basis for $W$ rather than a full basis of eigenvectors, it holds even when $\mathbf{P}'$ is not diagonalizable (does not possess $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors). Finally, $\abs{\lambda_i} \le 1$ for every eigenvalue of the column-stochastic matrix $\mathbf{P}'$, giving $\abs{\alpha\lambda_i} \le \alpha$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Power-iteration convergence rate]
\label{ch36:thm-converge}
With $x^{(k)} = \mathbf{G}^k v$ and $\pi$ the PageRank vector,
\[
  \norm{x^{(k)} - \pi}_1 \;\le\; 2\,\alpha^{k}.
\]
Thus $\Oh(\log(1/\eps)/\log(1/\alpha))$ iterations suffice to achieve $\norm{x^{(k)} - \pi}_1 \le \eps$. Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-converge} plots this bound for three values of $\alpha$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write the step-$k$ error vector as $e^{(k)} = x^{(k)} - \pi$. Because $\mathbf{G}\pi = \pi$, we have $e^{(k+1)} = \mathbf{G}x^{(k)} - \mathbf{G}\pi = \mathbf{G}e^{(k)}$, so $e^{(k)} = \mathbf{G}^k e^{(0)}$. Both $x^{(k)}$ and $\pi$ are probability vectors, so $\mathbf{1}^\top e^{(k)} = 1 - 1 = 0$, placing every $e^{(k)}$ in the zero-sum subspace $W = \{w : \mathbf{1}^\top w = 0\}$.

On $W$, $\mathbf{G}w = \alpha\mathbf{P}'w$. Applying $\mathbf{P}'$ never increases the $\ell_1$ norm of any vector:
\begin{align*}
  \norm{\mathbf{P}'w}_1
  &= \sum_{i=1}^n \Big\lvert\sum_{j=1}^n \mathbf{P}'_{ij} w_j\Big\rvert \\
  &\le \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n \mathbf{P}'_{ij}\abs{w_j} && \text{(triangle inequality)} \\
  &= \sum_{j=1}^n \Big(\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{P}'_{ij}\Big)\abs{w_j} \;=\; \norm{w}_1 && \text{(each column of $\mathbf{P}'$ sums to $1$).}
\end{align*}
Therefore $\norm{\mathbf{G}w}_1 = \alpha\norm{\mathbf{P}'w}_1 \le \alpha\norm{w}_1$ for every $w \in W$. Applying this contraction $k$ times gives $\norm{e^{(k)}}_1 \le \alpha^k \norm{e^{(0)}}_1 \le 2\alpha^k$, since $\norm{x^{(0)}-\pi}_1 \le \norm{x^{(0)}}_1 + \norm{\pi}_1 = 2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{whybox}
The contraction $\norm{\mathbf{G}w}_1 \le \alpha\norm{w}_1$ on zero-sum vectors has a direct probabilistic meaning via \emph{coupling}: running two surfers step by step using the same biased coin flip and the same random teleport page whenever they teleport. Recall that the \emph{total-variation distance} between two distributions $p$ and $q$ is $\norm{p - q}_{\mathrm{TV}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\norm{p - q}_1$, the largest difference in probability they assign to any set of pages. On each step, with probability $1-\alpha$ both coupled surfers teleport to the \emph{same} page drawn from $v$ and move identically from then on, erasing a $1-\alpha$ share of their disagreement; with probability $\alpha$ both follow links, which cannot increase $\ell_1$ distance. Thus each step retains at most an $\alpha$ fraction of the remaining distance, without requiring $\mathbf{G}$ to be diagonalizable.
\end{whybox}

\begin{trapbox}
Raising $\alpha$ (say to $0.95$) makes PageRank follow the link structure over longer walks, at three costs: convergence slows because $0.95^k$ decays slowly, the problem becomes more \emph{ill-conditioned} (small link changes produce larger swings in $\pi$; compare condition numbers in Chapter~31), and link-spam farms gain stronger amplification (Section~\ref{ch36:spam}). Lowering $\alpha$ (say to $0.70$) converges rapidly and blunts spam, at the cost of flattening scores toward the teleport vector $v$. Choosing $\alpha = 0.85$ is an engineering trade-off rather than a mathematical constant.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{The same thing as a linear solve}
\label{ch36:linsolve}

Power iteration is not the only way to compute $\pi$. Because $\mathbf{1}^\top \pi = 1$, the stationary equation $\pi = \mathbf{G}\pi = \alpha\mathbf{P}'\pi + (1-\alpha)v(\mathbf{1}^\top \pi)$ simplifies to $\pi = \alpha\mathbf{P}'\pi + (1-\alpha)v$, which rearranges into a sparse linear system:
\[
  (\mathbf{I} - \alpha\mathbf{P}')\,\pi \;=\; (1-\alpha)\,v.
\]
In every column $u$ of $\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{P}'$, the diagonal entry is $1 - \alpha\mathbf{P}'_{uu}$ while the absolute values of the off-diagonal entries sum to $\sum_{w \ne u}\alpha\mathbf{P}'_{wu} = \alpha(1 - \mathbf{P}'_{uu})$, which is strictly smaller than $1 - \alpha\mathbf{P}'_{uu}$ by $1 - \alpha > 0$. A matrix in which every column's diagonal entry exceeds the sum of its absolute off-diagonal entries is called \emph{strictly diagonally dominant by columns}; any such matrix is invertible, and its condition number stays bounded as long as $\alpha$ is bounded away from $1$.

Viewing PageRank as $(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{P}')\pi = (1-\alpha)v$ connects it to standard iterative linear solvers:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Richardson iteration} splits the equation as $\pi = \alpha\mathbf{P}'\pi + (1-\alpha)v$ and updates $x^{(k+1)} = \alpha\mathbf{P}'x^{(k)} + (1-\alpha)v$. Whenever $\mathbf{1}^\top x^{(k)} = 1$, this update equals $\mathbf{G}x^{(k)}$, so power iteration \emph{is} Richardson iteration on $(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{P}')\pi = (1-\alpha)v$.
  \item \textbf{Gauss--Seidel iteration} updates the entries of $x$ one vertex at a time in place, immediately using newly computed values within the same sweep, which often cuts iteration counts roughly in half.
  \item \textbf{Krylov subspace methods} --- such as generalized minimal residual (GMRES) or biconjugate gradient stabilized (BiCGSTAB) --- search for the best approximate solution inside the subspace spanned by $\{b, \mathbf{M}b, \mathbf{M}^2b, \dots, \mathbf{M}^{k-1}b\}$ generated by repeated matrix--vector products with $\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{P}'$ and $b = (1-\alpha)v$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{whybox}
When is a linear solver worth using instead of power iteration? First, if $\alpha$ is pushed close to $1$ (say $0.99$), Richardson/power iteration slows down proportionally to $1/(1-\alpha)$, whereas Krylov methods exploit clustering among the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{P}'$ and can converge in far fewer steps, especially with \emph{preconditioning} (multiplying the system by an inexpensive approximate inverse to reduce its condition number). Second, existing scientific-computing libraries already optimize sparse linear solves. The drawback at web scale is memory and communication: Krylov solvers store multiple length-$n$ vectors and compute global inner products at each step, whereas power iteration needs just one streaming pass over the edges per step, making it straightforward to partition across thousands of machines.
\end{whybox}

\begin{connectionbox}
In linear-operator terminology, $(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{P}')^{-1}$ is called the \emph{resolvent} of the walk matrix. On a connected undirected graph, $\mathbf{P} = \Adjm\mathbf{D}^{-1}$, so multiplying $\mathbf{I} - \alpha\mathbf{P}$ on the right by the degree matrix $\mathbf{D}$ gives
\[
  (\mathbf{I} - \alpha\mathbf{P})\mathbf{D} \;=\; \mathbf{D} - \alpha\Adjm \;=\; (1-\alpha)\mathbf{D} + \alpha\Lap,
\]
a positive diagonal matrix plus a scaled graph Laplacian. Consequently, the near-linear-time Laplacian solvers of Chapter~41 solve undirected PageRank systems in $\tilde\Oh(m)$ time (where $\tilde\Oh$ hides logarithmic factors in $n$ and $1/\eps$, as in Chapter~35) even as $\alpha \to 1$.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Tracing PageRank by hand}
\label{ch36:trace}

Run Algorithm~\ref{ch36:alg-pagerank} on the four-page graph of Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-web4} with $\alpha = 0.85$ and uniform teleport $v = \tfrac14(1,1,1,1)^\top$. Because no vertex is dangling ($D = \emptyset$), we have $s = 0$ and $\beta = 1-\alpha = 0.15$ at every step, so $\beta v = 0.15 \cdot \tfrac14\mathbf{1} = 0.0375\,\mathbf{1}$ and each iteration computes
\[
  x^{(k+1)} \;=\; 0.85\,\mathbf{P}x^{(k)} + 0.0375\,\mathbf{1}.
\]
Start from $x^{(0)} = (0.25,\,0.25,\,0.25,\,0.25)^\top$. Recall from Example~\ref{ch36:ex-Pmatrix} that $A$ splits equally to $B,C$; $B$ splits equally to $A,D$; $C$ sends everything to $D$; and $D$ splits equally to $B,C$.

\textbf{Step 1.} Compute $\mathbf{P}x^{(0)}$ coordinate by coordinate:
\begin{align*}
(\mathbf{P}x^{(0)})_A &= \tfrac12 x^{(0)}_B = 0.125, &
(\mathbf{P}x^{(0)})_B &= \tfrac12 x^{(0)}_A + \tfrac12 x^{(0)}_D = 0.25,\\
(\mathbf{P}x^{(0)})_C &= \tfrac12 x^{(0)}_A + \tfrac12 x^{(0)}_D = 0.25, &
(\mathbf{P}x^{(0)})_D &= \tfrac12 x^{(0)}_B + x^{(0)}_C = 0.375.
\end{align*}
Scale by $0.85$ and add $0.0375$ to every coordinate:
\[
  x^{(1)} \;=\; 0.85\,(0.125,\, 0.25,\, 0.25,\, 0.375)^\top + 0.0375\,\mathbf{1}
  \;=\; (0.14375,\; 0.25,\; 0.25,\; 0.35625)^\top.
\]
(Sanity check: $0.14375 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 0.35625 = 1.0$.)

\textbf{Step 2.} Multiply $x^{(1)}$ by $\mathbf{P}$:
\begin{align*}
(\mathbf{P}x^{(1)})_A &= \tfrac12(0.25) = 0.125, &
(\mathbf{P}x^{(1)})_B &= \tfrac12(0.14375) + \tfrac12(0.35625) = 0.25,\\
(\mathbf{P}x^{(1)})_C &= \tfrac12(0.14375) + \tfrac12(0.35625) = 0.25, &
(\mathbf{P}x^{(1)})_D &= \tfrac12(0.25) + 0.25 = 0.375.
\end{align*}
Applying $0.85\,\mathbf{P}x^{(1)} + 0.0375\,\mathbf{1}$ gives
\[
  x^{(2)} \;=\; (0.14375,\; 0.25,\; 0.25,\; 0.35625)^\top \;=\; x^{(1)}.
\]
Iteration has reached the exact fixed point after a single step. Why did this four-node example finish in one step instead of fifty? The initial error $e^{(0)} = x^{(0)} - \pi = (0.10625,\, 0,\, 0,\, -0.10625)^\top$ happens to lie in the \emph{null space} of $\mathbf{P}$ (the set of vectors $z$ with $\mathbf{P}z = \mathbf{0}$), because $x_A$ and $x_D$ enter $\mathbf{P}x$ only through their sum $\tfrac12(x_A + x_D)$, which is already $0.25$ in $x^{(0)}$. Thus $e^{(1)} = \alpha\mathbf{P}e^{(0)} = \mathbf{0}$. Starting from $x^{(0)} = (1,0,0,0)^\top$ instead excites the subdominant eigenvalue $\alpha\lambda_2 = -0.85$ and decays as $0.85^k$. Rounding to four decimal places, the PageRank vector is
\[
  \pi \;\approx\; (0.1438,\; 0.2500,\; 0.2500,\; 0.3563)^\top.
\]

Inspect the ranking: $D$ scores highest ($0.356$) because it receives links from both $B$ and $C$, and $C$ funnels \emph{all} of its outgoing link weight into $D$. Pages $B$ and $C$ tie at $0.250$, while $A$ ranks lowest ($0.144$) because its sole incoming link comes from $B$, which splits its endorsement between $A$ and $D$. Notice that $A$, $B$, and $D$ all have out-degree $2$: adding outgoing links from your own page does not raise your score; incoming links from high-scoring pages do.

Try re-running the calculation with $\alpha = 0.50$ to verify the blue bars $(0.1875, 0.25, 0.25, 0.3125)^\top$ in Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-trace-bars}. Then add a fifth, dangling page $E$ with a link $A \to E$ and no outgoing links of its own, and watch $s = x_E$ feed back into $\beta$ at each step.
\end{labbox}

\begin{marginnotebox}
Sergey Brin and Larry Page introduced PageRank in 1998, naming it after both ``web page'' and Larry Page. Mathematically, it applies power iteration (studied by Richard von Mises in 1929) to eigenvector centrality; their decisive practical addition was the teleportation term, which guarantees a unique answer and fast convergence while keeping every iteration sparse.
\end{marginnotebox}

\section{Personalized PageRank}
\label{ch36:ppr}

Nothing in the linear system $(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{P}')\pi = (1-\alpha)v$ or Theorem~\ref{ch36:thm-converge} required the teleport vector $v$ to be uniform (strict column diagonal dominance guarantees a unique solution for any probability vector $v$, even when some entries of $v$ are zero). Changing $v$ biases the surfer's resets toward a chosen set of pages. \emph{Personalized PageRank} (PPR) sets $v = e_s$, the standard basis vector for a single source vertex $s$ (having $1$ in coordinate $s$ and $0$ everywhere else): every time the surfer teleports, they jump straight back \emph{home} to $s$. The stationary distribution $\mathrm{ppr}_s$ then measures importance \emph{relative to $s$}, giving high scores to vertices reachable from $s$ by short, multi-path walks:
\[
  \boxed{\;\mathrm{ppr}_s \;=\; (1-\alpha)\,\big(\mathbf{I} - \alpha\mathbf{P}'\big)^{-1} e_s.\;}
\]
Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-global-vs-ppr} compares global PageRank against $\mathrm{ppr}_A$ and $\mathrm{ppr}_D$ on our four-page web: seeding at $A$ lifts $\mathrm{ppr}_A(A)$ from $0.144$ to $0.248$, whereas seeding at $D$ raises $\mathrm{ppr}_D(D)$ to $0.443$ and drops $A$ to $0.098$.

\begin{whybox}
Where does the formula $\mathrm{ppr}_s = (1-\alpha)(\mathbf{I} - \alpha\mathbf{P}')^{-1}e_s$ come from, and what does it mean probabilistically? Rearranging the stationary equation $x = \alpha\mathbf{P}'x + (1-\alpha)e_s$ gives $(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{P}')x = (1-\alpha)e_s$. Just as a scalar $r$ with $\abs{r} < 1$ satisfies $(1-r)^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty r^k$, any square matrix $\mathbf{M}$ with spectral radius $\rho(\mathbf{M}) < 1$ satisfies $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{M})^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \mathbf{M}^k$, called the \emph{Neumann series} (verified by telescoping $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{M})\sum_{k=0}^K \mathbf{M}^k = \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{M}^{K+1} \to \mathbf{I}$). Because $\rho(\alpha\mathbf{P}') = \alpha < 1$, expanding $(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{P}')^{-1}$ gives
\[
  \mathrm{ppr}_s \;=\; (1-\alpha)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha^k\,(\mathbf{P}')^{k} e_s.
\]
In the $k$-th term, $(\mathbf{P}')^k e_s$ is the surfer's distribution after $k$ link steps starting from $s$, and its weight $(1-\alpha)\alpha^k$ is the probability of taking $k$ link steps in a row ($\alpha^k$) before teleporting on the next flip ($1-\alpha$). A random count $K \in \{0,1,2,\dots\}$ with $\Pr[K = k] = (1-\alpha)\alpha^k$ is said to follow a \emph{geometric distribution} with parameter $1-\alpha$; its average length is $\alpha/(1-\alpha)$ steps (about $5.7$ steps when $\alpha = 0.85$). Thus $\mathrm{ppr}_s(u)$ is the probability that a random walk of geometric$(1-\alpha)$ length started at $s$ ends at $u$.
\end{whybox}

\begin{connectionbox}
On an undirected graph, the Neumann series for $(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{P})^{-1}$ is the damped counterpart of the Laplacian pseudoinverse $\Lap^+$ from Chapter~35 (often called the discrete Green's function). Andersen, Chung, and Lang showed that sweeping vertices in decreasing order of $\mathrm{ppr}_s(u)/\deg(u)$ finds a low-conductance cluster around $s$ without examining the rest of the graph, giving a \emph{local} version of Cheeger's inequality (Chapter~33). The same PPR vectors drive ``who to follow'' recommendations in social networks and propagation layers in graph neural networks (such as approximate personalized propagation of neural predictions, APPNP).
\end{connectionbox}

\subsection{Computing PPR locally: push}

When we ask for vertices close to a single seed $s$, most of a billion-vertex graph has $\mathrm{ppr}_s(u) \approx 0$; computing the entire $n$-entry vector wastes work. The Andersen--Chung--Lang \emph{push} algorithm computes an accurate local approximation around $s$ in time independent of $n$. It maintains two sparse vectors (stored as hash maps containing only nonzero entries): an estimate $p$ of probability mass already settled into PPR scores, and a \emph{residual} vector $r$ of probability mass waiting at vertices to be walked further. Given a per-edge accuracy threshold $\tau > 0$, the algorithm repeatedly picks any vertex $u$ whose waiting mass satisfies $r_u \ge \tau\,\deg^+(u)$, settles a $(1-\alpha)$ share of $r_u$ into $p_u$, and pushes the remaining $\alpha$ share equally along $u$'s outgoing links.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Approximate Personalized PageRank by push (Andersen--Chung--Lang)}
\label{ch36:alg-push}
\KwIn{source $s$; damping $\alpha$; threshold $\tau$; out-adjacency of $\Gph$, every out-degree $\ge 1$}
\KwOut{estimate $p \approx \mathrm{ppr}_s$ with bounded residual}
$p \gets \mathbf{0}$; \quad $r \gets e_s$\;
\While{$\exists\, u$ with $r_u \ge \tau\,\deg^+(u)$}{
  $p_u \gets p_u + (1-\alpha)\, r_u$ \tcp*{settle the keep-fraction at $u$}
  $m \gets \alpha\, r_u$; \quad $r_u \gets 0$\;
  \ForEach{$u \to w$}{ $r_w \gets r_w + m / \deg^+(u)$ \tcp*{spread the walk-fraction}}
}
\Return $p$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{whybox}
\emph{Correctness invariant.} For any vector $r$, write $\mathrm{ppr}_r = (1-\alpha)(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{P}')^{-1}r = \sum_u r_u\,\mathrm{ppr}_u$ by linearity of matrix multiplication. Before the loop starts, $p + \mathrm{ppr}_r = \mathbf{0} + \mathrm{ppr}_{e_s} = \mathrm{ppr}_s$. Because $\mathrm{ppr}_u = (1-\alpha)e_u + \alpha \sum_{u \to w} \tfrac{1}{\deg^+(u)}\mathrm{ppr}_w$ (one step from $u$ either teleports immediately with probability $1-\alpha$ or steps to a random out-neighbor $w$ and continues from $w$), each push at $u$ replaces $r_u\,\mathrm{ppr}_u$ in $\mathrm{ppr}_r$ with $(1-\alpha)r_u e_u$ in $p$ plus $\tfrac{\alpha r_u}{\deg^+(u)}\mathrm{ppr}_w$ at each neighbor $w$, preserving
\[
  \mathrm{ppr}_s \;=\; p + \mathrm{ppr}_r
\]
at every step. When the loop terminates, $0 \le r_u < \tau\,\deg^+(u)$ for all $u$. On an undirected graph, let $\mathbf{d}$ be the vector of vertex degrees $\deg(u)$; since $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{d}$, we have $\mathrm{ppr}_{\mathbf{d}} = \mathbf{d}$, and nonnegativity of $(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\mathbf{P})^{-1}$ implies $0 \le \mathrm{ppr}_s(u) - p_u = \mathrm{ppr}_r(u) \le \tau\,\mathrm{ppr}_{\mathbf{d}}(u) = \tau\deg(u)$ at every vertex $u$. On a directed graph, no degree vector is universally stationary, so termination bounds the total unsettled mass $\norm{r}_1$ rather than per-vertex error divided by out-degree.

\emph{Running time.} Pushing vertex $u$ requires $r_u \ge \tau\,\deg^+(u)$, so line~3 permanently adds $(1-\alpha)r_u \ge (1-\alpha)\tau\,\deg^+(u)$ to $\mathbf{1}^\top p$. Because $\mathbf{1}^\top p \le \mathbf{1}^\top \mathrm{ppr}_s = 1$, the sum of $\deg^+(u)$ across all pushes ever executed is at most
\[
  \sum_{\text{pushes at } u} \deg^+(u) \;\le\; \frac{1}{(1-\alpha)\tau}.
\]
Each push at $u$ does $\Oh(\deg^+(u))$ work, so total running time is $\Oh\!\big(\tfrac{1}{(1-\alpha)\tau}\big)$, \emph{independent of $n$ and $m$}.
\end{whybox}

\begin{applicationbox}
Localized PPR powers interactive graph recommendations: Twitter's ``Who To Follow'' ran personalized walks on the follow graph, Pinterest's Pixie system serves related-pin recommendations from short biased walks in milliseconds, and e-commerce ``customers also viewed'' queries rank nearby items the same way. Because push touches at most $1/((1-\alpha)\tau)$ edges, a query finishes in microseconds even when the full graph has billions of edges.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Spam and robustness}
\label{ch36:spam}

Because anyone can publish web pages and add outgoing links, an attacker who controls many pages can wire them together to boost a target page's score. Manipulating link structure for rank gain is called \emph{link spam}, and fake pages or identities created by one operator to mimic independent endorsements are called \emph{Sybil} nodes.

\subsection{The link farm}

Suppose an attacker creates a set $S \subseteq V$ of cheap Sybil pages, points them all at a target page $T \in S$, and links $T$ back into $S$ so probability recirculates. How much PageRank can $S$ accumulate? Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-farm} diagrams the four probability flows into, inside, and out of $S$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Uniform teleportation guarantees every page on the web at least $(1-\alpha)/n$ of PageRank. Pointing $f - 1$ dummy pages at $T$ funnels roughly $\alpha (f - 1)(1-\alpha)/n$ of score into $T$ from teleportation alone, and linking $T$ back into the farm recirculates that score around a loop. What caps the loop is the same damping factor: every pass around the farm loses at least a $1-\alpha$ share of its mass to global teleportation, limiting internal amplification to a geometric-series factor of $1/(1-\alpha)$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{theorem}[Spam mass is bounded by the leak]
\label{ch36:thm-spam}
Let $S \subseteq V$ be a set of pages controlled by an adversary, and suppose teleportation is uniform ($v = \tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}$) and no page outside $S$ is dangling. Let
\[
  \mathrm{in}(S) \;=\; \sum_{u \notin S} \pi_u \sum_{\substack{w \in S \\ (u,w) \in E}} \frac{1}{\deg^+(u)}
\]
be the link probability flowing into $S$ from outside pages. Then the total PageRank $\pi(S) = \sum_{w \in S}\pi_w$ captured by $S$ satisfies
\[
  \pi(S) \;\le\; \frac{(1-\alpha)\,\abs{S}/n \;+\; \alpha\,\mathrm{in}(S)}{1-\alpha}
  \;=\; \frac{\abs{S}}{n} \;+\; \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,\mathrm{in}(S).
\]
In particular, with no outside links into the farm ($\mathrm{in}(S)=0$), $\pi(S) \le \abs{S}/n$: an isolated farm of $f$ pages can never capture more total PageRank than $f$ disconnected pages would receive from uniform teleportation alone.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Sum the stationary equation $\pi_w = (\mathbf{G}\pi)_w$ over all $w \in S$. At each step, mass arriving in $S$ comes from three sources: uniform teleportation landing in $S$, which contributes $(1-\alpha)\abs{S}/n$; link steps from $u \notin S$ into $S$, which contribute $\alpha\,\mathrm{in}(S)$ (since no $u \notin S$ is dangling); and $\alpha\mathbf{P}'$ steps starting inside $S$ that stay inside $S$, which contribute at most $\alpha\,\pi(S)$ (at most $100\%$ of each $u \in S$ stays in $S$). Therefore
\begin{align*}
  \pi(S) &\;\le\; (1-\alpha)\frac{\abs{S}}{n} \;+\; \alpha\,\mathrm{in}(S) \;+\; \alpha\,\pi(S), \\
  (1-\alpha)\pi(S) &\;\le\; (1-\alpha)\frac{\abs{S}}{n} \;+\; \alpha\,\mathrm{in}(S).
\end{align*}
Dividing by $1-\alpha > 0$ gives the bound, and setting $\mathrm{in}(S)=0$ gives $\pi(S) \le \abs{S}/n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{whybox}
Theorem~\ref{ch36:thm-spam} shows why link spam is an economic problem rather than a purely algebraic exploit. Rearranging internal links inside $S$ cannot push $\pi(S)$ past $\abs{S}/n$; to beat that baseline, the spammer must either pay to host millions of pages (growing $\abs{S}$) or buy/trick links from reputable external pages so $\mathrm{in}(S) > 0$, which then gets amplified by at most $\alpha/(1-\alpha)$ ($\approx 5.7$ at $\alpha = 0.85$). Raising $\alpha$ toward $1$ inflates $\alpha/(1-\alpha)$, which is the second practical reason search engines keep $\alpha$ well below $1$.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{Defenses}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{TrustRank.} Replace uniform teleportation with a vector $v$ supported only on a human-reviewed seed set of reputable sites (universities, major libraries, established news sources). Trust flows outward along links for a few steps; link farms that receive no path of links from the seed set get zero teleport mass and near-zero TrustRank.
  \item \textbf{Anti-TrustRank / BadRank.} Reverse every edge in $\Gph$ and run personalized PageRank from known spam pages; a high score means a page links heavily toward known spam and can be demoted.
  \item \textbf{Spam-mass estimation.} Compare a page's uniform-teleport PageRank $\pi_u$ against its trusted-seed score $t_u$; a large relative gap $(\pi_u - t_u)/\pi_u$ flags pages whose rank comes mostly from untrusted link farms (Gy\"ongyi, Garcia-Molina, and Pedersen).
  \item \textbf{Multi-signal ranking.} Modern search engines combine graph scores with hundreds of query-text, anchor-text, and user-behavior features inside machine-learning models trained on human-rated search results, reducing the reward of gaming link structure alone.
\end{itemize}

\begin{marginnotebox}
In the early 2000s, search engines faced both commercial link farms and ``Google bombing'' (coordinated campaigns linking thousands of pages with the same anchor phrase to a target URL). Gy\"ongyi, Garcia-Molina, and Pedersen introduced TrustRank in 2004, showing that seeding $v$ on a few hundred vetted sites sharply cuts link-farm scores across web crawls.
\end{marginnotebox}

\section{The linear-algebra and Markov views, side by side}
\label{ch36:twoviews}

Every Markov-chain statement in this chapter translates directly into a matrix statement:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.46\linewidth}p{0.46\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Markov-chain view} & \textbf{Linear-algebra view}\\
\midrule
Surfer's long-run fraction of steps $\pi$ & Positive right eigenvector $\mathbf{G}\pi = \pi$\\
Ergodic chain (irreducible and aperiodic) & $\mathbf{G}_{ij} > 0$ (primitive; Perron--Frobenius)\\
Coupling shrinks total variation by $\alpha$ & Contraction $\norm{\mathbf{G}w}_1 \le \alpha\norm{w}_1$ on $\mathbf{1}^\top w = 0$\\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{aestheticbox}
Instead of simulating one random surfer for millions of steps and counting visits (\emph{Monte Carlo} estimation, meaning estimation by repeated random sampling), power iteration advances the entire probability distribution deterministically one step at a time until the $1-\alpha$ contraction collapses all starting error. One vector $\pi$ simultaneously represents a matrix fixed point ($\mathbf{G}\pi = \pi$), a long-run visit frequency, and a network centrality score.
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{Historical Context and Controversies}

\begin{historybox}
The mathematics behind PageRank grew out of a 1906 dispute in probability theory. Andrey Markov introduced chains of dependent random variables to refute Pavel Nekrasov's claim that the law of large numbers required independent events, hand-tallying $20{,}000$ consecutive vowels and consonants in Alexander Pushkin's verse novel \emph{Eugene Onegin} to demonstrate convergence to stationary frequencies. Between 1907 and 1912, Oskar Perron and Georg Frobenius proved that positive and irreducible nonnegative matrices possess a unique positive dominant eigenvector.

For decades Markov chains were used mainly in statistical physics and queueing theory (the mathematical study of waiting lines). When the World Wide Web expanded in the mid-1990s, early search engines ranked pages by counting how often a query word appeared on the page, which site owners defeated by repeating popular keywords hundreds of times in tiny or white-on-white text. Raw in-degree counts failed just as quickly against dummy pages. In 1998 two link-analysis proposals appeared side by side: Jon Kleinberg's HITS (Hyperlink-Induced Topic Search), which alternated matrix multiplications on a query subgraph to separate \emph{authorities} (pages with strong content on the topic) from \emph{hubs} (directory pages curating links to authorities), and Sergey Brin and Larry Page's PageRank, built for their Stanford search prototype \emph{BackRub} (1996--1998), which became Google. Computing a single query-independent global vector $\pi$ offline let Google score pages at crawl time rather than solving an eigenvector problem per search query. Designing ranking rules that stay transparent and manipulation-resistant on fast-changing social and web streams remains an active problem.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{What We Can Now Do and Its Limitations}
\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{What we can now compute:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A unique, strictly positive global importance score $\pi$ on any directed graph in $\Oh\!\big((n+m)\log(1/\eps)/\log(1/\alpha)\big)$ time using sparse power iteration.
  \item Source-specific proximity scores $\mathrm{ppr}_s$ around any seed vertex $s$, approximated locally in $\Oh\!\big(1/((1-\alpha)\tau)\big)$ time by the push algorithm without reading the entire graph.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Limitations:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Coordinated link spam}: Although Theorem~\ref{ch36:thm-spam} caps isolated farms at $\abs{S}/n$, attackers who buy links on reputable external pages ($\mathrm{in}(S) > 0$) still gain up to a $1/(1-\alpha)$ boost, requiring defenses such as TrustRank and spam-mass filtering.
  \item \emph{Query blindness}: Global PageRank measures structural prominence, not whether a page answers a specific question. General reference homepages receive high PageRank on every topic, so search engines must combine graph scores with text matching and relevance models.
  \item \emph{Cost of global updates}: As millions of links appear and disappear continuously, recomputing global PageRank across billions of vertices requires large-scale distributed computation (a workload that helped motivate early cluster frameworks such as Google's MapReduce and the graph-processing system Pregel).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Where we go next:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item In Chapter~37 (NP-Hard Graph Problems and Reductions), we study graph problems for which no polynomial-time algorithm is known. Shortest paths, flows, Laplacians, and PageRank all run in polynomial time because linear systems, greedy trees, and augmenting paths have global optimality certificates; problems such as Hamiltonian cycle, minimum vertex cover, graph coloring, and maximum cut are $\mathbf{NP}$-hard.
  \item In Chapter~38 (Approximation Algorithms on Graphs), we design polynomial-time algorithms that are guaranteed to return solutions within a proven factor of optimal on these $\mathbf{NP}$-hard problems, including geometric relaxations that assign each vertex a unit vector on a sphere and partition the sphere with a random plane.
\end{itemize}
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch36:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Trace with teleportation changed]
Recompute the PageRank of Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-web4} with $\alpha = 0.5$ and uniform teleport $v = \tfrac14\mathbf{1}$. Carry out two power-iteration steps by hand from $x^{(0)} = v$ and report $x^{(2)}$. Confirm that every coordinate moved closer to $0.25$ compared with the $\alpha = 0.85$ result, and explain why in one sentence.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Add a dangling node]
Add a fifth page $E$ to Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-web4} with a single link $A \to E$ (so $A$ now links to $\{B,C,E\}$) and no outgoing links from $E$. Write the sparse update rule including the dangling sum $s = x_E$, then compute two power-iteration steps from uniform $x^{(0)} = \tfrac15\mathbf{1}$ at $\alpha = 0.85$. What fraction of total mass sits on $E$ at stationarity, and where does $E$'s mass go on the next step?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Eigenvalue check]
For the $4\times4$ matrix $\mathbf{P}$ of Example~\ref{ch36:ex-Pmatrix}, compute the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{P}$ and of $\mathbf{G} = 0.85\mathbf{P} + 0.15\cdot\tfrac14\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top$ (by hand or with a linear-algebra library). Verify that the subdominant eigenvalue of $\mathbf{G}$ has modulus $\le 0.85$, consistent with Theorem~\ref{ch36:thm-eigen}, and explain why $\abs{\lambda_2(\mathbf{G})} = 0.85$ holds with equality on this graph.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Prove the $\ell_1$ contraction by splitting signs]
Let $w \in \R^n$ satisfy $\mathbf{1}^\top w = 0$. Write $w = w^+ - w^-$ where $w^+_i = \max(w_i, 0)$ and $w^-_i = \max(-w_i, 0)$, so $w^+, w^- \ge 0$ satisfy $\mathbf{1}^\top w^+ = \mathbf{1}^\top w^- = \tfrac12\norm{w}_1$. Track $\mathbf{G}w^+$ and $\mathbf{G}w^-$ to prove directly that the $(1-\alpha)v(\mathbf{1}^\top w^\pm)$ terms cancel and $\norm{\mathbf{G}w}_1 \le \alpha\norm{w}_1$, deducing Theorem~\ref{ch36:thm-converge} without using eigenvalues.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Personalized PageRank as a walk]
For a source vertex $s$, prove that $\mathrm{ppr}_s(t) = (1-\alpha)\sum_{k\ge0}\alpha^k \Pr[\text{$k$-step walk from $s$ ends at $t$}]$ satisfies the stationary equation $x = \alpha\mathbf{P}'x + (1-\alpha)e_s$. Show by example that $\mathrm{ppr}_s(t) = \mathrm{ppr}_t(s)$ need \emph{not} hold on directed graphs, then prove that on an undirected graph without isolated vertices, $\deg(t)\,\mathrm{ppr}_s(t)$ and $\deg(s)\,\mathrm{ppr}_t(s)$ are equal: $\mathrm{ppr}_s(t)/\deg(t) = \mathrm{ppr}_t(s)/\deg(s)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Push correctness]
Verify algebraically that one iteration of Algorithm~\ref{ch36:alg-push} at vertex $u$ preserves the invariant $\mathrm{ppr}_s = p + \sum_w r_w\,\mathrm{ppr}_w$. Then prove the $\Oh\!\big(1/((1-\alpha)\tau)\big)$ bound on total work by charging each edge push against the $(1-\alpha)r_u$ mass permanently added to $\mathbf{1}^\top p$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Spam bound]
Write out the exact balance equation in the proof of Theorem~\ref{ch36:thm-spam}, accounting explicitly for links from $S$ that leave $S$ versus links (or dangling nodes) in $S$ that remain in $S$. Then construct a farm $S$ of $f$ pages on an $n$-page web receiving one incoming link from an outside page $u \notin S$ of PageRank $\pi_u = p_0$ whose sole out-link enters $S$ (with all links from $S$ staying inside $S$), and solve for $\pi(S)$ in terms of $\alpha, f, n, p_0$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Break it, then fix it]
Design a directed graph on at most $8$ vertices in which a $3$-vertex link farm $S$ captures more than twice its baseline share $3/n$ of uniform-teleport PageRank ($\alpha = 0.85$) by combining one legitimate incoming link with internal recirculation. Then recompute scores using a TrustRank teleport vector seeded uniformly on two legitimate vertices that cannot reach $S$, and verify how much $\pi(S)$ drops.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Monte Carlo PageRank]
Estimate $\pi_u$ without matrix--vector products by running $R$ independent surfers for $T$ steps each on $\mathbf{G}$ from initial distribution $v$ and recording the fraction $\hat{\pi}_u$ of the $R$ surfers standing on $u$ at step $T$. Choose $T$ so $\abs{\Pr[X_T = u] - \pi_u} \le \eps/2$, then use Hoeffding's inequality --- for $R$ independent $\{0,1\}$ trials with mean $\mu$, the sample average $\hat{\pi}_u$ satisfies $\Pr[\abs{\hat{\pi}_u - \mu} \ge \eps/2] \le 2\exp(-R\eps^2/2)$ --- to find how large $R$ must be so $\abs{\hat{\pi}_u - \pi_u} \le \eps$ with probability at least $1-\delta$. Compare the total step count $R\cdot T$ against power iteration and state when Monte Carlo uses less work.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Periodicity without teleport]
Construct a small directed graph whose raw transition matrix $\mathbf{P}$ is periodic of period $2$ (for example, a directed bipartite cycle) and choose a starting vector $x^{(0)}$ for which $\mathbf{P}^k x^{(0)}$ oscillates forever without converging. Show that for any $\alpha < 1$, $\mathbf{G}^k x^{(0)}$ converges, and give the exact rate $\alpha^k$ at which the oscillation amplitude decays.
\end{exercise}
