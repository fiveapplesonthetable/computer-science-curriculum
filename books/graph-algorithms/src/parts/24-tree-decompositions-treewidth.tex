\chapter{Tree Decompositions and Treewidth}

Trees support fast queries and clean dynamic programs because deleting any edge
splits a tree into two disconnected pieces and ancestors order the subproblems.
Most networks, however, contain cycles. A \emph{tree decomposition} carries a
tree's divide-and-conquer structure over to graphs with cycles, and an integer
called \emph{treewidth} measures how far a graph deviates from a tree. On an
$n$-vertex graph of treewidth $k$, tree-style dynamic programming runs in time
exponential in $k$ rather than in $n$.

\begin{historybox}
Tree decompositions were introduced in 1984 by Neil Robertson and Paul Seymour in
Paper~II of their twenty-paper \emph{Graph Minors} project. That series
proved Wagner's Conjecture: every minor-closed family of finite graphs (a family where if $\Gph$ belongs to the family, so do all its minors)
is characterized by finitely many forbidden minors (graphs that cannot appear as minors of any member), where $H$ is a \emph{minor} of $\Gph$
if $H$ can be obtained from $\Gph$ by deleting vertices, deleting edges, and
contracting edges (merging an edge's two endpoints into a single vertex). Related ideas appeared independently in
Umberto Bertel{\'e} and Francesco Brioschi's non-serial dynamic programming (dynamic programs whose variables interact along general graphs rather than a line, 1972),
Rudolf Halin's $S$-functions (tree-like width invariants, 1976), and Stefan Arnborg and
Andrzej Proskurowski's partial $k$-trees (subgraphs of graphs built by repeatedly attaching a new vertex to $k$ mutually adjacent vertices, 1989). Robertson and Seymour needed tree decompositions to show that
any graph avoiding a fixed planar minor (a minor that can be drawn in the plane without edge crossings) decomposes into tree-like pieces -- the Excluded Grid Theorem.

Early attempts to extend tree algorithms to general graphs deleted feedback vertex
sets (sets of vertices whose removal leaves an acyclic graph). That strategy breaks down on meshes:
an $n\times n$ grid graph requires deleting $\Th(n^2)$ vertices to break all cycles, discarding
local structure even though every vertex has degree at most $4$. Splitting a graph recursively along arbitrary cuts also struggled: when cut vertices accumulate on the boundaries of pieces, the number of dynamic-programming states grows exponentially.

Robertson and Seymour arranged overlapping vertex subsets (``bags'') into a tree subject to the \emph{running
intersection property}: the bags containing any given vertex form a connected subtree, ensuring that
each bag separates the parts of the graph on different branches of the tree. In 1990, Bruno Courcelle
proved that every graph property expressible in Monadic Second-Order Logic ($\mathrm{MSO}_2$,
logic quantifying over vertices, edges, and sets of vertices or edges) can be decided in linear time
$f(k)\cdot n$ on graphs of treewidth at most $k$, for a function $f$ depending only on $k$ and the formula. In 1996, Hans Bodlaender showed that
deciding whether a graph has treewidth at most $k$ and constructing an optimal decomposition also takes
linear time $f(k)\cdot n$ for fixed $k$. Whether treewidth can be approximated within a
constant factor in time polynomial in $n$ alone remains an open problem.
\end{historybox}

\section{From Trees to Tree-Like Graphs}
\label{ch24:intro}

\begin{intuitionbox}
A tree is easy to work with for two reasons. First, a tree has one-vertex \emph{separators}:
delete one vertex and the tree falls into disconnected pieces, so a subproblem in
one piece interacts with the rest only through that single cut vertex. Second,
those separators are organized hierarchically -- the recursion tree of ``cut
here, recurse on the pieces'' is itself a tree, so a dynamic program can sweep it
once. A tree decomposition provides both properties for a general graph. It
groups the vertices into overlapping sets called \emph{bags}, arranges the bags
in a tree, and guarantees that each bag acts as a small separator between the
parts of the graph on either side of it. The largest bag size, minus one, is the
treewidth, which governs the cost of dynamic programming.
\end{intuitionbox}

Consider the $4$-vertex cycle $C_4$ on $\{1,2,3,4\}$. Writing $uv$ as shorthand for
the undirected edge $\{u,v\}$, its edges are $12, 23, 34, 41$. Because of its
cycle, $C_4$ is not a tree, yet deleting opposite vertices $1$ and $3$ splits the
remaining vertices $2$ and $4$ apart. Figure~\ref{ch24:fig-c4} covers $C_4$ with
two bags of size three joined by a single tree edge.

\section{The Definition and Why Each Clause Matters}
\label{ch24:defs}

\begin{definition}[Tree decomposition]
\label{ch24:def-td}
A \emph{tree decomposition} of a graph $\Gph=(V,E)$ is a pair $(\mathcal{T},
\{B_x\}_{x\in \mathcal{T}})$ where $\mathcal{T}$ is a tree and each node $x$ of
$\mathcal{T}$ carries a \emph{bag} $B_x\subseteq V$, subject to three conditions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item[\textbf{(V)}] \emph{Vertex cover:} $\bigcup_{x\in\mathcal{T}} B_x = V$.
        Every vertex belongs to at least one bag.
  \item[\textbf{(E)}] \emph{Edge cover:} for every edge $\{u,v\}\in E$ there is a
        node $x$ of $\mathcal{T}$ with $\{u,v\}\subseteq B_x$. Every edge has both
        endpoints together inside at least one bag.
  \item[\textbf{(C)}] \emph{Connectivity (coherence):} for every vertex $v\in V$,
        the set of nodes $\{x : v\in B_x\}$ forms a \emph{connected subtree} of
        $\mathcal{T}$ (equivalently, if $v\in B_x$ and $v\in B_y$, then $v\in B_z$
        for every node $z$ on the unique path between $x$ and $y$ in $\mathcal{T}$).
\end{itemize}
The \emph{width} of the decomposition is $\max_{x\in\mathcal{T}}\abs{B_x}-1$. The
\emph{treewidth} $\tw(\Gph)$ is the minimum width over all tree decompositions of $\Gph$.
\end{definition}

The ``$-1$'' is a convention chosen so that trees have treewidth $1$
rather than $2$ (Proposition~\ref{ch24:prop-trees}). To keep the two graphs
distinct, we reserve ``node'' for $\mathcal{T}$ and ``vertex'' for $\Gph$: nodes of
$\mathcal{T}$ carry bags, while vertices of $\Gph$ sit inside bags.
Figure~\ref{ch24:fig-bag-structure} illustrates all three conditions on a
$6$-vertex graph.

\begin{focusbox}
Conditions \textbf{(V)} and \textbf{(E)} ensure that every vertex and every edge of
$\Gph$ appears inside at least one bag, so no adjacency can hide outside the bags
(non-adjacent vertices may also share a bag, as $1$ and $3$ do in Figure~\ref{ch24:fig-c4}).
Condition \textbf{(C)} is what makes bags act as separators. Without \textbf{(C)},
a vertex could appear in two distant bags without appearing along the path between
them, creating dependencies across subtrees and breaking dynamic programming.
Condition \textbf{(C)} forces each vertex to occupy a single connected region of
$\mathcal{T}$ and never reappear outside it.
\end{focusbox}

\subsection{The separator property}

Deleting any edge of $\mathcal{T}$ turns the shared vertices of its two endpoint
bags into a vertex separator of $\Gph$.

\begin{lemma}[Bags are separators]
\label{ch24:lem-separator}
Let $(\mathcal{T},\{B_x\})$ be a tree decomposition of $\Gph$, and let
$\{x,y\}$ be an edge of $\mathcal{T}$. Deleting $\{x,y\}$ splits $\mathcal{T}$
into two subtrees: $\mathcal{T}_x$ containing $x$ and $\mathcal{T}_y$ containing $y$. Let
\[
  A = \Bigl(\bigcup_{z\in\mathcal{T}_x} B_z\Bigr)\setminus (B_x\cap B_y),
  \qquad
  C = \Bigl(\bigcup_{z\in\mathcal{T}_y} B_z\Bigr)\setminus (B_x\cap B_y).
\]
Then $S = B_x\cap B_y$ separates $A$ from $C$: no edge of $\Gph$ joins a vertex
of $A$ to a vertex of $C$ (and $A\cap C=\emptyset$).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
First, if a vertex $v$ occurred in both $\mathcal{T}_x$ and $\mathcal{T}_y$, then
by \textbf{(C)} the unique path in $\mathcal{T}$ between those two occurrences
would cross $\{x,y\}$, putting $v\in B_x\cap B_y = S$; thus $A$ and $C$ are disjoint.
Now suppose for contradiction that $\{a,c\}\in E$ with $a\in A$ and $c\in C$. By \textbf{(E)},
some bag $B_z$ contains both $a$ and $c$. The node $z$ lies in $\mathcal{T}_x$ or
in $\mathcal{T}_y$; suppose $z\in\mathcal{T}_y$ (the other case is symmetric). Since
$a\in A$, vertex $a$ appears in some bag of $\mathcal{T}_x$, and since $a\in B_z$, it also
appears in a bag of $\mathcal{T}_y$. As above, condition \textbf{(C)} forces $a\in B_x\cap B_y=S$,
contradicting $a\in A$.
\end{proof}

\begin{whybox}
Lemma~\ref{ch24:lem-separator} (Figure~\ref{ch24:fig-separator}) is why tree decompositions support dynamic programming. When a bottom-up pass crosses an edge $\{x,y\}$ of a width-$k$ decomposition $\mathcal{T}$, everything it needs to remember about the already-processed side is how a partial solution behaves on the separator $S=B_x\cap B_y$, a set of at most $k+1$ vertices. If each vertex in $S$ takes one of $c$ states -- such as ``chosen'' or ``not chosen'' ($2^{\abs{S}}$ combinations), or one of $q$ colors in a proper vertex coloring where adjacent vertices receive distinct colors ($q^{\abs{S}}$ combinations) -- the table size at each bag is at most $c^{k+1}$, independent of $n$.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{Trees, cycles, and cliques}

\begin{proposition}[Treewidth $1$ means forest]
\label{ch24:prop-trees}
$\tw(\Gph)\le 1$ if and only if $\Gph$ is a forest (acyclic). A graph with at
least one edge has $\tw(\Gph)\ge 1$, while an edgeless graph on $n\ge 1$ vertices
has $\tw(\Gph)=0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
($\Leftarrow$) If $\Gph$ has no edges, give each vertex its own $1$-vertex bag and connect the bags in a path; every bag has size $1$, giving width $0$. Otherwise root each tree component of the forest at a vertex $r$. Create a root node in $\mathcal{T}$ with singleton bag $\{r\}$, and for each non-root vertex $u$ create a node with bag $\{u,\operatorname{parent}(u)\}$ whose parent in $\mathcal{T}$ is the node created for $\operatorname{parent}(u)$ (Figure~\ref{ch24:fig-tree-tw1}); if the forest has several tree components, link their root nodes into a path so $\mathcal{T}$ is connected. Every bag has size at most $2$, so the width is at most $1$. Conditions \textbf{(V)} and \textbf{(E)} hold by construction, and \textbf{(C)} holds because each vertex $u$ appears only in its own node and in the nodes of its children in $\Gph$, which form a connected star in $\mathcal{T}$.

($\Rightarrow$) Let $(\mathcal{T},\{B_x\})$ be a decomposition of $\Gph$ of width at most $1$, so every bag has size at most $2$. We prove $\Gph$ is a forest by induction on $\abs{V}$ plus the number of nodes of $\mathcal{T}$. If $\mathcal{T}$ has one node, $\Gph$ has at most two vertices and is a forest. Otherwise pick a leaf $x$ of $\mathcal{T}$ with neighbor $y$. If $B_x\subseteq B_y$, deleting node $x$ from $\mathcal{T}$ leaves a valid width-$\le 1$ decomposition of $\Gph$ with one fewer node. Otherwise some vertex $v\in B_x$ is absent from $B_y$. By \textbf{(C)}, the subtree of $\mathcal{T}$ containing $v$ includes the leaf $x$ and avoids $x$'s only neighbor $y$, so $v$ appears in $B_x$ alone. By \textbf{(E)}, every edge incident to $v$ must lie in $B_x$, which holds at most one other vertex, so $\deg(v)\le 1$. Deleting $v$ from $\Gph$ and from $B_x$ gives a width-$\le 1$ decomposition of $\Gph-v$ (the graph left after removing $v$ and its incident edge, if any), which is a forest by induction. Reattaching a vertex of degree at most $1$ cannot create a cycle, so $\Gph$ is a forest. Finally, if $\Gph$ has at least one edge $\{u,v\}$, condition \textbf{(E)} requires a bag of size at least $2$, so $\tw(\Gph)\ge 1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Cliques are unavoidable]
\label{ch24:prop-clique}
If $K\subseteq V$ forms a \emph{clique} in $\Gph$ (every two vertices in $K$ are adjacent), then some bag contains all of $K$.
Consequently $\tw(\Gph)\ge \omega(\Gph)-1$, where $\omega(\Gph)$ is the clique number (the size of the largest clique in $\Gph$),
and $\tw(K_n)=n-1$ for the complete graph $K_n$ on $n\ge 1$ vertices (the graph in which all $\binom{n}{2}$ pairs are adjacent).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We argue by induction on the number of nodes in $\mathcal{T}$. If $\mathcal{T}$ has one node $x$, condition \textbf{(V)} gives $K\subseteq V = B_x$. Otherwise let $x$ be a leaf of $\mathcal{T}$ with unique neighbor $y$. If $K\subseteq B_x$, we are done. Otherwise some vertex $u\in K$ satisfies $u\notin B_x$. For every other vertex $v\in K\setminus\{u\}$, the clique edge $\{u,v\}\in E$ lies in some bag $B_z$ by \textbf{(E)}, and $z\ne x$ because $u\notin B_x$. Thus every vertex of $K$ appears in $\mathcal{T}-x$. Moreover, if $v\in K$ also lies in $B_x$, then $v\in B_x\cap B_z$, and because the path in $\mathcal{T}$ from the leaf $x$ to $z\ne x$ passes through $y$, condition \textbf{(C)} gives $v\in B_y$. Hence $K\cap B_x\subseteq B_y$: deleting node $x$ from $\mathcal{T}$ preserves \textbf{(V)}, \textbf{(E)}, and \textbf{(C)} on $K$, so by induction some bag in $\mathcal{T}-x$ contains $K$. (This argument proves a case of the \emph{Helly property} for trees: any finite family of subtrees of a tree that intersect pairwise shares at least one common node.)

Because $K\subseteq B_x$, we have $\abs{B_x}\ge\abs{K}$ and width at least $\abs{K}-1$. For $K_n$ this gives $\tw(K_n)\ge n-1$, and a single bag containing all $n$ vertices achieves width $n-1$.
\end{proof}

\section{Nice Tree Decompositions}
\label{ch24:nice}

In an arbitrary tree decomposition, moving across one edge of $\mathcal{T}$ can add
and remove several vertices at once, and a node may have any number of children. A
\emph{nice} tree decomposition breaks those transitions into elementary steps so
dynamic programs have only four simple node types to handle.

\begin{definition}[Nice tree decomposition]
\label{ch24:def-nice}
A tree decomposition $(\mathcal{T},\{B_x\})$ is \emph{nice} if $\mathcal{T}$ is
rooted, the root bag and every leaf bag are empty ($\emptyset$), and every non-leaf
node $x$ of $\mathcal{T}$ has one of three types:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Introduce $v$}: $x$ has one child $y$, and $B_x = B_y\cup\{v\}$ for a
        single vertex $v\notin B_y$.
  \item \textbf{Forget $v$}: $x$ has one child $y$, and $B_x = B_y\setminus\{v\}$ for a
        single vertex $v\in B_y$.
  \item \textbf{Join}: $x$ has two children $y$ and $z$, with $B_x = B_y = B_z$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

Any tree decomposition can first be trimmed to at most $n$ nodes by repeatedly
contracting any tree edge $\{x,y\}$ with $B_x\subseteq B_y$. From there, converting
to a nice decomposition preserves the width and multiplies the node count by at
most $\Oh(k)$.

\begin{proposition}[Converting to a nice decomposition]
\label{ch24:prop-nice}
Any width-$k$ tree decomposition with $\Oh(n)$ nodes can be converted to a nice
tree decomposition of width $k$ with $\Oh(k\cdot n)$ nodes in $\Oh(k^2 n)$ time.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Root $\mathcal{T}$ arbitrarily. Whenever a node $x$ has $d\ge 2$ children, replace
$x$ by a binary tree of $d-1$ join nodes, all carrying the bag $B_x$, and attach
one child subtree to each slot. Next, on every edge from parent $x$ to child $y$
where $B_x\ne B_y$, insert a chain of forget nodes removing $B_y\setminus B_x$ one
vertex at a time followed by introduce nodes adding $B_x\setminus B_y$ one vertex
at a time; this takes $\abs{B_y\setminus B_x}+\abs{B_x\setminus B_y}\le 2(k+1)$
steps. Finally, attach a chain of forget nodes above the root up to an empty root
bag, and hang a chain of introduce nodes below each leaf down to an empty leaf bag.
Every inserted bag is a subset of an existing bag, so the width stays $k$, and the
total number of nodes is $\Oh(kn)$ (with only $\Oh(n)$ join nodes). Copying bags
of size at most $k+1$ across $\Oh(kn)$ nodes takes $\Oh(k^2 n)$ time.
\end{proof}

steps and a join node where two branches with identical bags merge. Arrows point
from child to parent (leaves at the bottom, root at the top), matching the
bottom-up order of dynamic programming.

\section{Dynamic Programming over a Tree Decomposition}
\label{ch24:dp}

At each node $x$ of a nice tree decomposition, we store a table indexed by the
possible states of its bag $B_x$. Let $V_x$ be the union of all bags in the
subtree of $\mathcal{T}$ rooted at $x$ (the vertices introduced at or below $x$).
Each table entry records the best value (or count, or feasibility) of a partial
solution on $V_x$ among those that match the given state on $B_x$. By
Lemma~\ref{ch24:lem-separator}, vertices in $V_x\setminus B_x$ have no edges to
vertices outside $V_x$, so the state on $B_x$ is the only information the rest of
the tree needs.

\subsection{Maximum weight independent set}

An \emph{independent set} is a set of pairwise non-adjacent vertices. Given vertex
weights $w(v)\ge 0$ and writing $w(I)=\sum_{v\in I} w(v)$ for the total weight of a
subset $I\subseteq V$, we want a maximum-weight independent set (MWIS). Finding an
MWIS is NP-hard on general graphs (the term was introduced in Chapter~4;
Chapter~\ref{ch:np-hard} covers NP-hardness in detail). On a nice tree decomposition, dynamic
programming solves MWIS in time linear in $n$ for fixed width.

At node $x$ with bag $B_x$, a state is any subset $S\subseteq B_x$. Define
\[
  \mathrm{dp}[x][S] \;=\;
  \max\Bigl\{\; w(I) \;:\; I\subseteq V_x \text{ is independent in }\Gph \text{ and }
  I\cap B_x = S \;\Bigr\},
\]
with $\mathrm{dp}[x][S]=-\infty$ when no such $I$ exists (which happens precisely
when $S$ itself contains an edge of $\Gph$). The four node rules are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Leaf} ($B_x=\emptyset$): the only subset is $S=\emptyset$, with
        $\mathrm{dp}[x][\emptyset]=0$.
  \item \textbf{Introduce $v$} (child $y$, $B_x=B_y\cup\{v\}$): for each $S\subseteq B_x$,
        \[
          \mathrm{dp}[x][S] \;=\;
          \begin{cases}
            \mathrm{dp}[y][S] & \text{if } v\notin S,\\[3pt]
            \mathrm{dp}[y][S\setminus\{v\}] + w(v) & \text{if } v\in S \text{ and } S \text{ is independent in }\Gph,\\[3pt]
            -\infty & \text{otherwise}.
          \end{cases}
        \]
  \item \textbf{Forget $v$} (child $y$, $B_x=B_y\setminus\{v\}$): for each $S\subseteq B_x$,
        \[
          \mathrm{dp}[x][S] \;=\; \max\bigl(\mathrm{dp}[y][S],\;
          \mathrm{dp}[y][S\cup\{v\}]\bigr),
        \]
        keeping the better of leaving $v$ out or putting $v$ in.
  \item \textbf{Join} (children $y,z$ with $B_x=B_y=B_z$): for each $S\subseteq B_x$,
        \[
          \mathrm{dp}[x][S] \;=\;
          \begin{cases}
            \mathrm{dp}[y][S]+\mathrm{dp}[z][S]-w(S) & \text{if } S \text{ is independent in }\Gph,\\[3pt]
            -\infty & \text{otherwise}.
          \end{cases}
        \]
        Both child subtrees include $S$, so adding their values counts $w(S)$
        twice; subtracting $w(S)$ corrects the overlap (Figure~\ref{ch24:fig-mwis-join}).
\end{itemize}

At the empty root $r$, $\mathrm{dp}[r][\emptyset]$ is the maximum weight of an
independent set in $\Gph$. Algorithm~\ref{ch24:alg-mwis} processes $\mathcal{T}$ in
post-order (visiting children before their parent).

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{MWIS} on a nice tree decomposition}
\label{ch24:alg-mwis}
\KwIn{nice tree decomposition $(\mathcal{T},\{B_x\})$ rooted at $r$, weights $w$}
\KwOut{maximum weight of an independent set of $\Gph$}
\ForEach{node $x$ of $\mathcal{T}$ in post-order}{
  \uIf{$x$ is a leaf}{$\mathrm{dp}[x][\emptyset]\gets 0$\;}
  \uElseIf{$x$ introduces $v$ over child $y$}{
    \ForEach{$S\subseteq B_x$}{
      \uIf{$v\in S$ and $S$ is independent in $\Gph$}{
        $\mathrm{dp}[x][S]\gets \mathrm{dp}[y][S\setminus\{v\}]+w(v)$\;}
      \uElseIf{$v\notin S$}{$\mathrm{dp}[x][S]\gets \mathrm{dp}[y][S]$\;}
      \Else{$\mathrm{dp}[x][S]\gets -\infty$\;}
    }
  }
  \uElseIf{$x$ forgets $v$ over child $y$}{
    \ForEach{$S\subseteq B_x$}{
      $\mathrm{dp}[x][S]\gets\max\bigl(\mathrm{dp}[y][S],\,\mathrm{dp}[y][S\cup\{v\}]\bigr)$\;}
  }
  \Else(\tcp*[h]{join with children $y,z$}){
    \ForEach{$S\subseteq B_x$}{
      \uIf{$S$ is independent in $\Gph$}{
        $\mathrm{dp}[x][S]\gets \mathrm{dp}[y][S]+\mathrm{dp}[z][S]-w(S)$\;}
      \Else{$\mathrm{dp}[x][S]\gets -\infty$\;}
    }
  }
}
\Return $\mathrm{dp}[r][\emptyset]$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{whybox}
Why does the join rule only add the two child values and subtract $w(S)$? By
Lemma~\ref{ch24:lem-separator}, $B_x$ separates the private vertices
$V_y\setminus B_x$ from $V_z\setminus B_x$. Once $S\subseteq B_x$ is fixed, any
independent set $I\subseteq V_x$ with $I\cap B_x=S$ splits uniquely into
$I_y = I\cap V_y$ and $I_z = I\cap V_z$, each intersecting $B_x$ in $S$. Because no
edge crosses between $V_y\setminus B_x$ and $V_z\setminus B_x$, any valid $I_y$ and
$I_z$ combine freely, and $w(I_y\cup I_z) = w(I_y) + w(I_z) - w(S)$.
\end{whybox}

\begin{theorem}[Correctness of Algorithm~\ref{ch24:alg-mwis}]
\label{ch24:thm-mwis}
For every node $x$ and every $S\subseteq B_x$, Algorithm~\ref{ch24:alg-mwis}
computes $\mathrm{dp}[x][S]$ equal to the maximum weight of an independent set
$I\subseteq V_x$ with $I\cap B_x=S$ (or $-\infty$ if none exists). In particular,
$\mathrm{dp}[r][\emptyset]$ is the MWIS weight of $\Gph$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We induct on the post-order of $\mathcal{T}$.
\emph{Leaf:} $V_x=\emptyset$, so only $I=\emptyset$ qualifies, with weight $0$.
\emph{Introduce $v$:} since $v\in B_x$ and $v\notin B_y$, condition \textbf{(C)}
implies $v\notin V_y$: if any node in $y$'s subtree contained $v$, the path to $x$
would pass through $y$ and force $v\in B_y$. Likewise, if $\{u,v\}\in E$ for some
$u\in V_y$, condition \textbf{(E)} places $\{u,v\}$ in a bag outside $y$'s subtree,
and condition \textbf{(C)} on $u$ then forces $u\in B_y\subseteq B_x$. Thus every
neighbor of $v$ in $V_x$ lies in $B_x$. If $v\in S$ and $S$ is independent, an
independent set $I\subseteq V_x$ with $I\cap B_x=S$ is formed by adding $v$ to any
independent $I'\subseteq V_y$ with $I'\cap B_y=S\setminus\{v\}$, giving weight
$\mathrm{dp}[y][S\setminus\{v\}]+w(v)$. If $v\notin S$, any $I\subseteq V_x$ with
$I\cap B_x=S$ satisfies $I\subseteq V_y$ and $I\cap B_y=S$ (when $S$ itself is not
independent, $\mathrm{dp}[y][S]=-\infty$ by induction).
\emph{Forget $v$:} here $V_x=V_y$, and an independent set $I\subseteq V_x$ with
$I\cap B_x=S$ either contains $v$ ($I\cap B_y=S\cup\{v\}$) or excludes $v$
($I\cap B_y=S$), so taking the maximum of the two child entries gives
$\mathrm{dp}[x][S]$.
\emph{Join:} by Lemma~\ref{ch24:lem-separator}, $V_y\setminus B_x$ and
$V_z\setminus B_x$ are disjoint with no edges between them, so $I\subseteq V_x$
with $I\cap B_x=S$ is independent if and only if $S$ is independent, $I\cap V_y$ is
independent on $V_y$, and $I\cap V_z$ is independent on $V_z$. Maximizing each side
independently and subtracting $w(S)$ yields the optimum.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Dominating set: a $3$-state DP}

A \emph{dominating set} $D\subseteq V$ is a vertex subset such that every vertex of
$\Gph$ either belongs to $D$ or has a neighbor in $D$. Finding a minimum-size
dominating set is NP-hard on general graphs. Unlike independent set, two states per
bag vertex are not enough: a vertex outside $D$ may already have a neighbor in $D$
from below, or it may still be waiting for a neighbor introduced higher in the
tree.

Each vertex $v\in B_x$ therefore receives one of three labels:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textcolor{sourceblue}{$\mathbf{1}$} (\emph{black}): $v\in D$.
  \item \textcolor{targetgreen}{$\mathbf{0}$} (\emph{white-dominated}): $v\notin
        D$ and $v$ is already adjacent to some vertex of $D$ in $V_x$.
  \item \textcolor{quotientorange}{$\hat{\mathbf{0}}$} (\emph{white-pending}):
        $v\notin D$ and $v$ is \emph{not yet} dominated -- it still needs a
        neighbor in $D$ from bags above $x$.
\end{itemize}
A state at node $x$ is a labeling $\phi:B_x\to\{1,0,\hat 0\}$. Define
$\mathrm{dp}[x][\phi]$ as the minimum size $\abs{D}$ of a subset $D\subseteq V_x$
such that (i)~$D\cap B_x=\{v\in B_x : \phi(v)=1\}$, (ii)~every forgotten vertex
$u\in V_x\setminus B_x$ is dominated by $D$, and (iii)~every vertex $v\in B_x$
with $\phi(v)=0$ is already dominated by $D$ (if no such $D$ exists,
$\mathrm{dp}[x][\phi]=+\infty$). The transitions minimize $\abs{D}$:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Leaf} ($B_x=\emptyset$): $\mathrm{dp}[x][()]=0$ for the empty labeling $()$.
  \item \textbf{Introduce $v$} (child $y$, $B_x=B_y\cup\{v\}$): $v$ has no
        forgotten neighbors in $V_y\setminus B_y$. From each child state $\psi$ on
        $B_y$, placing $v\in D$ ($\phi(v)=1$) adds $1$ to the cost and upgrades
        every neighbor $u\in B_y$ of $v$ with $\psi(u)=\hat 0$ to $\phi(u)=0$
        (taking the minimum when multiple $\psi$ map to the same $\phi$). Leaving
        $v\notin D$ adds $0$ to the cost and sets $\phi(v)=0$ if some neighbor of
        $v$ in $B_y$ has $\psi(u)=1$, or $\phi(v)=\hat 0$ otherwise.
  \item \textbf{Forget $v$} (child $y$, $B_x=B_y\setminus\{v\}$): once $v$ leaves
        the bag, it can never gain new neighbors, so states with $v$ still pending
        ($\hat 0$) are discarded:
        \[
          \mathrm{dp}[x][\phi] \;=\; \min\bigl(\mathrm{dp}[y][\phi\cup\{v\mapsto 1\}],\;
          \mathrm{dp}[y][\phi\cup\{v\mapsto 0\}]\bigr).
        \]
  \item \textbf{Join} (children $y,z$ with $B_x=B_y=B_z$): child states $\phi_y$
        and $\phi_z$ are compatible with parent state $\phi$ when they choose the
        same black vertices (each $u$ has $\phi_y(u)=1$ if and only if $\phi_z(u)=1$, matching $\phi(u)=1$) and, on
        every non-black vertex $u$, $\phi(u)=0$ when at least one of $\phi_y(u),
        \phi_z(u)$ is $0$, while $\phi(u)=\hat 0$ when $\phi_y(u)=\phi_z(u)=\hat 0$.
        Minimizing over compatible pairs $(\phi_y,\phi_z)$ gives
        \[
          \mathrm{dp}[x][\phi] \;=\; \min_{(\phi_y,\phi_z)\to\phi}
          \Bigl(\mathrm{dp}[y][\phi_y] + \mathrm{dp}[z][\phi_z]
          - \abs{\{u\in B_x : \phi(u)=1\}}\Bigr).
        \]
\end{itemize}

At the empty root $r$, $\mathrm{dp}[r][()]$ is the minimum dominating-set size.

\begin{trapbox}
Collapsing white-$0$ and white-$\hat0$ into a single ``not in $D$'' state breaks
the algorithm: the forget rule can no longer tell whether a vertex leaving the bag
was ever dominated, so the dynamic program produces sets that miss vertices. Keep
``not in $D$ and already dominated'' ($0$) separate from ``not in $D$ and still
awaiting a dominator'' ($\hat 0$). A second trap is the join rule: domination acts
as a logical OR across the two subtrees, so white-$0$ in the parent requires
white-$0$ in \emph{at least one} child, whereas $\hat 0$ requires $\hat 0$ in
\emph{both} children.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{complexitybox}
At each node of a width-$k$ nice decomposition, MWIS stores $2^{\abs{B_x}}\le
2^{k+1}$ states; checking independence at an introduce node takes $\Oh(k)$ time per
state (or $\Oh(1)$ bitmask operations when $k$ fits in a machine word), giving
$\Oh(2^k\cdot k^2 n)$ time overall ($\Oh(2^k\cdot kn)$ with bitmasks) -- linear in
$n$ for fixed $k$. Dominating set stores $3^{\abs{B_x}}\le 3^{k+1}$ states;
scanning all compatible child pairs at a join node takes $\Oh(5^k)$ time (since
each coordinate has $5$ valid $(y,z)$ combinations: $(1,1)$, $(\hat 0,\hat 0)$,
$(0,0)$, $(0,\hat 0)$, $(\hat 0,0)$), and merging the logical-OR coordinates one
bag vertex at a time reduces each of the $\Oh(n)$ join nodes to $\Oh(3^k\cdot k)$,
giving $\Oh(3^k\cdot kn)$ overall. In general, a problem with $c$ states per bag
vertex runs in $\Oh(c^k\cdot\poly(k)\cdot n)$ time, where $\poly(k)$ denotes a
polynomial in $k$: exponential in treewidth, linear in $n$.
\end{complexitybox}

\section{Computing and Approximating Treewidth}
\label{ch24:compute}

Where does the tree decomposition itself come from? Deciding whether
$\tw(\Gph)\le k$ when $k$ is part of the input is NP-complete
(Arnborg--Corneil--Proskurowski, 1987; no polynomial-time algorithm is known for
general $k$), yet we have exact algorithms when $k$ is small, constant-factor
approximations, and fast practical heuristics.

\subsection{Exact, when treewidth is small: Bodlaender's theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Bodlaender, 1996]
\label{ch24:thm-bodlaender}
There is an algorithm that, given $\Gph$ and $k$, runs in $2^{\Oh(k^3)}\cdot n$
time and either outputs a tree decomposition of width at most $k$ or reports
$\tw(\Gph)>k$.
\end{theorem}

This running time is \emph{fixed-parameter tractable} (FPT): polynomial (here,
linear) in $n$ for each fixed $k$, with the exponential growth confined to the
factor $2^{\Oh(k^3)}$. Because $2^{\Oh(k^3)}$ is prohibitively large even for
moderate $k$, Theorem~\ref{ch24:thm-bodlaender} is mainly a theoretical benchmark
showing that finding a decomposition does not break linear-in-$n$ FPT bounds.
Practical solvers rely instead on constant-factor approximations or elimination
heuristics.

\subsection{Constant-factor approximation via balanced separators}

A recursive algorithm due to Robertson and Seymour builds a decomposition of width
$\Oh(k)$ whenever $\tw(\Gph)\le k$, using the fact that small-treewidth graphs
always contain small \emph{balanced separators}. Recall from Definition~\ref{ch01:def-subgraph} that $\Gph[X]$ is the
\emph{induced subgraph} on $X\subseteq V$ and $\Gph-S=\Gph[V\setminus S]$.

\begin{lemma}[Balanced separator from treewidth]
\label{ch24:lem-balanced-sep}
If $\tw(\Gph)\le k$, then for any nonnegative vertex weights $\mathrm{wt}:V\to\R_{\ge 0}$
there is a bag $B_x$ of an optimal tree decomposition that is a
$\tfrac12$-balanced separator: every connected component of $\Gph-B_x$ has
total weight at most $\tfrac12\mathrm{wt}(V)$. In particular, giving vertices of a
subset $W\subseteq V$ weight $1$ and all other vertices weight $0$ yields a set
$S=B_x$ of size at most $k+1$ such that every component of $\Gph-S$
contains at most $\tfrac12\abs{W}$ vertices of $W$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
Take an optimal decomposition $\mathcal{T}$ of width at most $k$. Orient each tree
edge $\{x,y\}$ toward whichever side ($\mathcal{T}_x$ or $\mathcal{T}_y$) has
strictly more than $\tfrac12\mathrm{wt}(V)$ weight among vertices outside
$B_x\cap B_y$; by Lemma~\ref{ch24:lem-separator} the two sides are disjoint, so at
most one side exceeds half. Because $\mathcal{T}$ is a finite tree with fewer
edges than nodes, some node $x$ has no outgoing oriented edge. Removing $B_x$
restricts each component of $\Gph-B_x$ to one side of $\mathcal{T}-x$, so
every component carries weight at most $\tfrac12\mathrm{wt}(V)$.
\end{proof}

More generally, call $S\subseteq U\cup W$ \emph{$\alpha$-balanced for $W$} in
$\Gph[U\cup W]$ if every connected component of $\Gph[U\cup W]-S$ contains
at most $\alpha\abs{W}$ vertices of $W$. Any components that each hold at most
$\tfrac12\abs{W}$ vertices of $W$ can be grouped into two sides that each hold at
most $\tfrac23\abs{W}$ vertices of $W$. Relaxing $\tfrac12$ to $\tfrac23$ lets us
\emph{find} such an $S$ without knowing an optimal decomposition: when
$\abs{W}\le 3k+6$, we can test all $3^{\abs{W}}=2^{\Oh(k)}$ ways to partition $W$
into $(W_1, S\cap W, W_2)$ with $\abs{W_1},\abs{W_2}\le \tfrac23\abs{W}$ and run a
minimum vertex-separator flow computation (Chapter~18) between $W_1$ and $W_2$.

Algorithm~\ref{ch24:alg-approx} decomposes an induced subgraph $\Gph[U\cup W]$,
where $U$ is the current \emph{interior} yet to be decomposed and $W$ is a
\emph{boundary} interface of size at most $3k+6$ that must sit inside the returned
root bag. Here $N(\Gamma)$ denotes the set of vertices outside $\Gamma$ that have
at least one neighbor in $\Gamma$ (Figure~\ref{ch24:fig-approx-decomp}).

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{ApproxDecompose}$(U,W)$ -- decomposition of interior $U$ with boundary $W$}
\label{ch24:alg-approx}
\KwIn{disjoint vertex sets $U$ (interior) and $W$ (boundary) with $\abs W\le 3k+6$}
\KwOut{a tree decomposition of $\Gph[U\cup W]$ of width $\le 4k+7$ whose root bag contains $W$}
\If{$\abs{U\cup W}\le 4k+8$}{\Return single bag $U\cup W$\;}
find $S\subseteq U\cup W$ with $\abs S\le k+1$ that is $\tfrac{2}{3}$-balanced for $W$ in $\Gph[U\cup W]$\;
\tcp{exists by Lemma~\ref{ch24:lem-balanced-sep} when $\tw(\Gph)\le k$; else report $\tw(\Gph)>k$}
add one vertex of $U\setminus S$ to $S$\tcp*{ensures $\abs{\Gamma}<\abs{U}$; now $\abs S\le k+2$}
\ForEach{connected component $\Gamma$ of $\Gph[U]-S$}{
  $W_\Gamma\gets N(\Gamma)\cap (W\cup S)$\tcp*{boundary inherited by $\Gamma$}
  $\mathcal{T}_\Gamma\gets$\textsc{ApproxDecompose}$(\Gamma,\,W_\Gamma)$\;
}

\Return the assembled tree\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[$\Oh(1)$-approximation of treewidth]
\label{ch24:thm-approx}
If $\tw(\Gph)\le k$, Algorithm~\ref{ch24:alg-approx} called with $U=V$ and
$W=\emptyset$ returns a tree decomposition of width at most $4k+7=\Oh(k)$. With a
refined separator routine, Bodlaender et al.\ (2016) obtain width at most $5k+4$
in $2^{\Oh(k)}\cdot n$ time.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
Why does the invariant $\abs W\le 3k+6$ hold at every recursive call? Take any
child component $\Gamma$ of $\Gph[U]-S$. Because $\Gamma$ together with
its neighbors in $W\setminus S$ is connected and avoids $S$, it sits inside a
single component of $\Gph[U\cup W]-S$, which holds at most
$\tfrac23\abs W$ vertices of $W$. Every remaining vertex of $W_\Gamma$ comes from
$S$, so
\begin{align*}
  \abs{W_\Gamma}
  &\le \abs{N(\Gamma)\cap (W\setminus S)} + \abs{S} \\
  &\le \tfrac23(3k+6) + (k+2) = 3k+6.
\end{align*}
The boundary cap therefore reproduces itself down the tree, and every bag $W\cup S$
has size at most $(3k+6)+(k+2)=4k+8$, giving width at most $4k+7$. Adding one
vertex of $U\setminus S$ to $S$ guarantees $\abs{\Gamma}<\abs{U}$, so the recursion
terminates.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{Practical heuristics: elimination orderings}

Practical solvers compute decompositions via \emph{vertex elimination}. To
eliminate a vertex $v$, delete $v$ from the current graph and add an edge between
every pair of its remaining neighbors that are not yet adjacent, turning those
remaining neighbors into a clique; these added edges are called \emph{fill-in}
edges. An \emph{elimination ordering} $\pi=(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ eliminates all vertices
one by one. If $N_\pi(v_i)$ denotes the set of remaining neighbors of $v_i$ at the
moment $v_i$ is eliminated, the \emph{elimination width} of $\pi$ is
$\max_i \abs{N_\pi(v_i)}$ (equivalently, the maximum size of the closed clique
$\{v_i\}\cup N_\pi(v_i)$ minus one).

\begin{theorem}[Treewidth equals minimum elimination width]
\label{ch24:thm-elim}
$\tw(\Gph)$ equals the minimum over all vertex orderings $\pi$ of the elimination
width of $\pi$. Moreover, an ordering of width $k$ yields a tree decomposition of
width $k$ (and vice versa) in linear time.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Why the two notions coincide]
Given a width-$k$ decomposition, repeatedly pick a leaf bag $B_x$. If
$B_x\subseteq B_y$ for its neighbor $y$, delete leaf $x$; otherwise some vertex $v$
appears \emph{only} in $B_x$ by \textbf{(C)}. Every current neighbor of $v$ also
sits in $B_x$ by \textbf{(E)}, so eliminating $v$ touches at most
$\abs{B_x}-1\le k$ remaining neighbors and every fill-in edge between them already
lies inside $B_x$. Repeating produces an elimination ordering of width at most $k$.

Conversely, given an elimination ordering $\pi$ of width $k$, create one bag
$B_v = \{v\}\cup N_\pi(v)$ of size at most $k+1$ for each vertex $v$. Connect $B_v$
to $B_w$, where $w$ is the earliest-eliminated vertex in $N_\pi(v)$ (if
$N_\pi(v)=\emptyset$, attach $B_v$ to any later bag). Because eliminating $v$ made
$N_\pi(v)$ a clique, all other vertices of $N_\pi(v)\setminus\{w\}$ are adjacent to
$w$ when $w$ is eliminated and therefore belong to $B_w$; this propagation
guarantees \textbf{(C)}, while \textbf{(V)} and \textbf{(E)} hold directly, giving
a tree decomposition of width $k$.
\end{proof}

Heuristics choose the next vertex \emph{greedily}, making the locally cheapest
choice at each step without looking ahead:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Min-degree:} always eliminate a vertex of smallest current degree.
        Updates are fast using bucket queues, and the resulting width is close to
        optimal on many sparse networks.
  \item \textbf{Min-fill:} always eliminate a vertex whose elimination adds the
        \emph{fewest} new fill-in edges. Each step does more work to count missing
        neighbor pairs, and usually produces smaller width than min-degree.
\end{itemize}

Neither heuristic guarantees an optimal ordering -- by Theorem~\ref{ch24:thm-elim},
finding a minimum-width ordering is NP-hard -- yet on structured graphs from
circuits, compilers, and graphical models they regularly match or nearly match the
true treewidth.

\begin{labbox}
Run min-degree elimination on our seven-vertex running graph (Figure~\ref{ch24:fig-running-graph}).
Initial degrees are $\deg(a)=\deg(f)=\deg(g)=2$, $\deg(b)=\deg(d)=3$, and $\deg(c)=\deg(e)=4$.
Eliminate $a$ first: its remaining neighbors $N_\pi(a)=\{b,c\}$ are already adjacent, so $0$ fill-in and bag $\{a,b,c\}$ (size $3$).
Next eliminate $f$ ($N_\pi(f)=\{e,g\}$, already adjacent, bag $\{e,f,g\}$ of size $3$), then $g$ ($N_\pi(g)=\{e\}$, bag $\{e,g\}$ of size $2$).
On the remaining four vertices $\{b,c,d,e\}$, the current degrees are $\deg(b)=\deg(e)=2$ and $\deg(c)=\deg(d)=3$.
Min-degree eliminates $b$ next ($N_\pi(b)=\{c,d\}$, already adjacent, $0$ fill-in, bag $\{b,c,d\}$ of size $3$), leaving the triangle $\{c,d,e\}$ where $d$, $c$, and $e$ eliminate with degrees $2$, $1$, and $0$.
The maximum remaining degree encountered is $\max_v \abs{N_\pi(v)}=2$ (largest bag size $3$, width $3-1=2$), matching our hand decomposition.
(Had we eliminated $d$ instead of $b$ when $\deg(d)=3$, $N_\pi(d)=\{b,c,e\}$ would have added fill-in edge $be$ and raised the width to $3$.)
\end{labbox}

\section{Treewidth and Fixed-Parameter Tractability}
\label{ch24:fpt}

The dynamic programs in Section~\ref{ch24:dp} are instances of a general theorem,
which Chapter~25 places in the broader setting of parameterized algorithms.

\begin{theorem}[Courcelle, 1990]
\label{ch24:thm-courcelle}
Every graph property expressible by a formula $\varphi$ in \emph{monadic
second-order logic} ($\mathrm{MSO}_2$: logic allowing quantification over vertices,
edges, and sets of vertices or edges, using adjacency and vertex-edge incidence)
can be decided in time $f(\tw(\Gph),\,\abs{\varphi})\cdot n$ on an $n$-vertex graph
$\Gph$ given with a tree decomposition of width $\tw(\Gph)$, where $\abs{\varphi}$
is the length of $\varphi$ and $f$ is a computable function.
\end{theorem}

Independent set (``there exists a vertex set $I$ such that no edge has both
endpoints in $I$''), dominating set, $3$-colorability (coloring vertices with three
colors so no edge has equal-colored endpoints), and Hamiltonicity (having a cycle
that visits every vertex once) can all be written in $\mathrm{MSO}_2$, so each runs
in linear time on bounded-treewidth graphs. Courcelle's proof compiles $\varphi$
into a finite-state machine that runs bottom-up on a nice tree decomposition -- the
same table-passing pattern as our dynamic programs, though problem-specific tables
have far smaller constant factors than the generic construction.

\begin{connectionbox}
The bag-as-separator idea links several parts of this book. Planar graphs on $n$
vertices have treewidth $\Oh(\sqrt n)$ (Chapter~27), turning tree-decomposition
dynamic programming into $2^{\Oh(\sqrt n)}$-time subexponential algorithms instead
of $2^{\Th(n)}$ brute force. Heavy-light and centroid decompositions (Chapters~19
and~20) are specialized restructurings of treewidth-$1$ graphs for fast path and
subtree queries. In numerical linear algebra, Gaussian elimination on a sparse
symmetric matrix $M$ (whose nonzero off-diagonal entries $M_{uv}\ne 0$ define a
graph) performs vertex elimination directly: pivoting on variable $v$ creates new
nonzero entries between all remaining variables coupled to $v$, matching fill-in.
Minimizing treewidth is minimizing matrix fill-in, the guiding question of sparse
direct solvers and of nested dissection (recursively ordering small separators last
when solving linear systems $\Lap x=b$, where $\Lap$ is the graph Laplacian matrix
of Chapter~31). When the companion \emph{Linear Algebra} volume factors a symmetric
positive-definite matrix into $LL^\top$ (Cholesky factorization), its fill-in and
elimination tree are treewidth in matrix language.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\emph{Probabilistic inference} in Bayesian networks and Markov random fields
(graphical models representing joint probability distributions) runs dynamic
programming over a tree decomposition of the model graph, known as the
\emph{junction tree} algorithm; its running time is exponential in the treewidth,
so inference engines use min-degree and min-fill to choose elimination orders.
\emph{Register allocation} in a compiler builds an \emph{interference graph} whose
vertices are program variables, with an edge between two variables whenever both are
live at the same instruction, and assigns CPU registers by coloring this graph; for
programs in static single-assignment form, the interference graph is \emph{chordal}
(every cycle of length at least $4$ has a shortcut edge, equivalent to admitting a
zero-fill elimination ordering) and can be colored optimally in linear time.
\emph{Constraint satisfaction} and SQL \emph{query optimization} bound join-evaluation
cost by the treewidth (or, on hypergraphs where a single edge can join more than
two vertices, the \emph{hypertree width}) of the query graph. Many
\emph{road-network} and very-large-scale integration (\emph{VLSI}) chip-layout
graphs also have small treewidth relative to their size, enabling fast
preprocessing-based shortest-path and routing engines.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Pathwidth, Branchwidth, and the Wider Family}
\label{ch24:family}

A \emph{path decomposition} is a tree decomposition whose underlying tree
$\mathcal{T}$ is a path; the minimum width over all path decompositions of $\Gph$
is its \emph{pathwidth} $\operatorname{pw}(\Gph)\ge\tw(\Gph)$. Pathwidth models a
linear sweep with a sliding vertex window. Our four-bag decomposition of the
seven-vertex running graph (Figure~\ref{ch24:fig-running-graph}) is already a path,
so that graph has $\operatorname{pw}(\Gph)=\tw(\Gph)=2$. On branching trees,
however, forcing the bags into a single line can raise the width
(Figure~\ref{ch24:fig-pathwidth-vs-treewidth}; Exercise~10 shows the gap reaches
$\Th(\log n)$ on complete binary trees).

A second relative is \emph{branchwidth}, written $\operatorname{bw}(\Gph)$. A
branch decomposition places the edges of $\Gph$ at the leaves of an unrooted
\emph{ternary tree} (a tree in which every internal node has degree $3$); deleting
any tree edge splits $E$ into two edge sets, and the cut's order is the number of
vertices of $\Gph$ incident to at least one edge on both sides. Minimizing the
maximum cut order over all ternary trees gives $\operatorname{bw}(\Gph)$, which tracks
treewidth within a factor of $\tfrac32$ on every graph with at least one edge:
\[
  \max\bigl(\operatorname{bw}(\Gph),\,2\bigr)
  \;\le\; \tw(\Gph)+1
  \;\le\; \tfrac{3}{2}\max\bigl(\operatorname{bw}(\Gph),\,2\bigr).
\]
All three width parameters are tied to a concrete geometric obstruction by the
\emph{Grid Minor Theorem} of Robertson and Seymour: every graph of large treewidth
contains a large grid as a minor, and grids themselves have large treewidth. Up to
polynomial factors, treewidth measures the largest grid minor inside $\Gph$ -- a
duality between width and grid obstructions that underpins
\emph{bidimensionality theory} (designing subexponential FPT algorithms on planar
and minor-free graph families by branching or dynamic programming depending on grid
size).

\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{What This Made Possible:}
Treewidth extends linear-time tree dynamic programming to general graphs. When a
graph has bounded treewidth ($k = \Oh(1)$), NP-hard problems such as Maximum
Independent Set, $3$-Coloring, Dominating Set, and Hamiltonian Cycle run in linear
time $2^{\Oh(k)}\cdot n$ or $3^{\Oh(k)}\cdot n$. This yields exact algorithms on
networks with small separator structure, including control-flow graphs of structured
programs (which record how execution branches and loops), telecommunication
backbones, and RNA base-pairing graphs.

\textbf{Limitations:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Exponential growth in width:} Running times and table sizes scale as
        $2^{\Oh(k)}$ or $3^{\Oh(k)}$. Once $k \ge 30$, memory and transition costs
        exceed practical limits.
  \item \emph{Grids, planar graphs, and expanders have unbounded width:} An
        $n\times n$ grid has treewidth $n$, and general $n$-vertex planar graphs can
        have treewidth $\Th(\sqrt{n})$. Expander graphs (sparse graphs in which
        every vertex subset up to half the graph has proportionally many outgoing
        edges, Chapter~34) and complete graphs have treewidth $\Th(n)$, reducing the
        dynamic program to $2^{\Th(n)}$ time.
  \item \emph{Computing treewidth is NP-hard:} Deciding whether $\tw(\Gph)\le k$ is
        NP-complete when $k$ is part of the input, and the $2^{\Oh(k^3)}$ factor in
        Bodlaender's exact linear-time algorithm is already prohibitive for $k \ge 5$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Where We Go Next:}
When treewidth is large, we can parameterize instead by the size of the solution we
seek: \textbf{Chapter~25 (Fixed-Parameter Tractability on Graphs)} develops
polynomial-time input reduction and bounded-depth search trees. For planar graphs
whose treewidth reaches $\Th(\sqrt{n})$, \textbf{Chapter~26 (Planar Graphs)} and
\textbf{Chapter~27 (Separators)} develop planar embeddings and balanced separator
theorems directly.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch24:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Treewidth of small graphs]
Compute the treewidth of: (a)~the cycle $C_n$ ($n\ge 3$), (b)~the complete
bipartite graph $K_{2,n}$ (two vertices on one side adjacent to all $n\ge 2$
vertices on the other), (c)~the $3$-dimensional hypercube $Q_3$ (vertices
$\{0,1\}^3$, with edges between $3$-bit strings differing in one coordinate), and
(d)~a triangle with a path of length $5$ attached at one vertex. Prove both the
upper bound (by exhibiting a decomposition) and the lower bound (using
Proposition~\ref{ch24:prop-trees}, Proposition~\ref{ch24:prop-clique}, or the first
eliminated vertex in Theorem~\ref{ch24:thm-elim}).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The separator lemma, sharpened]
Test the converse of Lemma~\ref{ch24:lem-separator}: if a tree of bags satisfies
\textbf{(V)} and \textbf{(E)}, and for every tree edge $\{x,y\}$ the intersection
$B_x\cap B_y$ separates $A$ from $C$ as in Lemma~\ref{ch24:lem-separator}, must
condition \textbf{(C)} hold? Prove it or construct a counterexample.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Implement MWIS]
Implement Algorithm~\ref{ch24:alg-mwis} end to end: a min-degree heuristic to build
an elimination ordering, conversion to a nice tree decomposition, and the dynamic
program. Test your solver against brute force on random $12$-vertex graphs and on a
$4\times 4$ grid (treewidth $4$), and report how the runtime scales with $2^k$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Reconstruct the witness]
Algorithm~\ref{ch24:alg-mwis} returns only the optimum weight. Augment it to return
an actual maximum-weight independent set by recording which child choice achieved
each maximum and walking back down from the root. Prove that your reconstruction
returns a valid independent set of optimal weight.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Dominating set transitions in full]
Write out the complete introduce, forget, and join transition formulas for the
dominating-set DP of Section~\ref{ch24:dp}, including the rule that upgrades a bag
neighbor from $\hat 0$ to $0$ when a black vertex is introduced. Trace your rules
on the $4$-vertex path $P_4$ and verify that the minimum dominating set has size $2$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Three-coloring]
Design a dynamic program on a nice tree decomposition of width $k$ that decides
$3$-colorability in $\Oh(3^k\cdot\poly(k)\cdot n)$ time. Take each state at node $x$
to be a proper $3$-coloring of $\Gph[B_x]$ using $\{1,2,3\}$; state the introduce,
forget, and join rules, and prove the join rule correct via
Lemma~\ref{ch24:lem-separator}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Break a fake decomposition]
Construct three bags $B_1,B_2,B_3$ on a path $B_1-B_2-B_3$ that satisfy \textbf{(V)}
and \textbf{(E)} for some graph $\Gph$ while violating \textbf{(C)}, and exhibit an
edge of $\Gph$ for which Lemma~\ref{ch24:lem-separator} fails.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Elimination $=$ fill-in]
Carry out both directions of Theorem~\ref{ch24:thm-elim} on a $6$-vertex graph of
your choice: pick an elimination ordering, form the fill-in graph and compute its
elimination width, then construct the corresponding tree decomposition and verify
\textbf{(V)}, \textbf{(E)}, and \textbf{(C)}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Series-parallel graphs]
A two-terminal graph $(G,s,t)$ is \emph{series-parallel} if it is built from single
edges $(s,t)$ by \emph{series composition} (gluing $(G_1,s_1,t_1)$ and
$(G_2,s_2,t_2)$ by merging $t_1=s_2$, keeping terminals $(s_1,t_2)$) and
\emph{parallel composition} (merging $s_1=s_2$ and $t_1=t_2$). Prove that every
simple series-parallel graph has treewidth at most $2$, and conversely that every
graph of treewidth at most $2$ is a subgraph of a series-parallel graph. (Hint: use
Theorem~\ref{ch24:thm-elim} to eliminate a vertex of degree at most $2$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Pathwidth gap]
Exhibit a family of trees ($\tw=1$) whose pathwidth grows as $\Th(\log n)$. Prove
that the complete binary tree of depth $d$ (a rooted binary tree with $n=2^{d+1}-1$
vertices in which every internal node has two children and every leaf lies at
distance $d$ from the root) has pathwidth $\ceil{d/2}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Grid lower bound]
Prove that the $n\times n$ grid graph has treewidth at least $n$. (Hint: show that
any vertex set whose removal leaves no component with more than $\tfrac12 n^2$
vertices must have size at least $n$, then apply the contrapositive of
Lemma~\ref{ch24:lem-balanced-sep}.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Treewidth under operations]
Determine how treewidth can change under: adding one edge ($\tw$ increases by at
most $1$), contracting an edge ($\tw$ does not increase), and taking the disjoint
union of two graphs with no edges between them ($\tw$ is the maximum of the two).
Use these facts to determine the treewidth of $K_n$ ($n$ even) minus a perfect
matching ($n/2$ vertex-disjoint edges).
\end{exercise}
