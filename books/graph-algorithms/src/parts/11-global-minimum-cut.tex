\chapter{Global Minimum Cut}

The previous chapter found bridges --- single edges whose deletion cuts a connected network in two.
A network with no bridges can still be disconnected by removing a small set of edges.
Instead of cutting a single edge or specifying two fixed endpoints in advance, we ask a broader,
global question:

\begin{quote}
What is the cheapest set of edges whose removal disconnects the graph into two nonempty pieces?
\end{quote}

This is the \emph{global} minimum cut. It is the weakest point of a network: the smallest amount
of capacity you would have to sever to split it in two, no matter where the split happens to fall.
With so many possible splits, the problem looks hard, but it can be solved by working directly on the
graph. We will see two methods. The first, due to Stoer and Wagner, makes no random choices: it
repeatedly visits the vertices in a carefully chosen order and merges the last two. The second, due
to Karger, merges the endpoints of randomly chosen edges until only two groups of vertices remain;
it can fail, but we can prove how rarely. A refinement by Karger and Stein makes the random method
much faster by repeating only the risky final part of the process.

\chapterline

\section{The problem, precisely}

Let $\Gph=(V,E)$ be a connected undirected graph with $n=\abs{V}$ vertices and $m=\abs{E}$ edges, and let each edge $e$ carry a nonnegative weight $w(e)\ge 0$ (for unweighted graphs read $w(e)=1$). A \emph{cut} is a partition of the vertices into two nonempty sets $(S,\bar S)$ with $\bar S = V\setminus S$ and $\emptyset \neq S \neq V$. The \emph{weight} (or capacity) of the cut is the total weight of the edges crossing it:
\[
  w(S,\bar S) \;=\; \sum_{\substack{e=\{u,v\}\in E\\ u\in S,\ v\in\bar S}} w(e).
\]

\begin{definition}[Global minimum cut]\label{ch11:def-mincut}
The \emph{global minimum cut} of $\Gph$ is a cut of minimum weight over all partitions $(S,\bar S)$. Its value is
\[
  \lambda(\Gph) \;=\; \min_{\emptyset\neq S\subsetneq V} w(S,\bar S).
\]
For unweighted graphs $\lambda(\Gph)$ is the \emph{edge connectivity}: the fewest edges whose deletion disconnects $\Gph$.
\end{definition}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Picture the graph as a sheet of fabric and each edge as a thread of a certain thickness $w(e)$. A cut is a pair of scissors slicing the fabric into two nonempty pieces; its cost is the total thickness of the threads you sever. The global min-cut is the place where the fabric is most flimsy. The $s$--$t$ version (defined in the next paragraph) forces you to keep two named points on opposite sides of the slice; the global version lets the scissors fall anywhere, so $\lambda(\Gph) \le w(S,\bar S)$ for \emph{every} cut, in particular every $s$--$t$ cut.
\end{intuitionbox}

\paragraph{Fixed-endpoint cuts versus global cuts.}
An \emph{$s$--$t$ cut} is a cut with two named vertices $s$ and $t$ on opposite sides, and an \emph{$s$--$t$ min-cut} is the cheapest such cut. In Chapter~\ref{ch12:networks} we will compute $s$--$t$ min-cuts using \emph{flows} (amounts of a commodity pushed through the edges from $s$ to $t$). If you had an algorithm for $s$--$t$ min-cuts, you could find the global cut by fixing an arbitrary vertex $s$ and finding the cheapest $s$--$t$ cut over all $n-1$ possible choices of $t$:
\[
  \lambda(\Gph) \;=\; \min_{t\neq s}\; \big(\text{$s$--$t$ min-cut}\big).
\]
Whichever side of the true global cut contains $s$, the opposite side contains at least one vertex $t$, so running $n-1$ $s$--$t$ min-cut computations is guaranteed to locate the global minimum. The two algorithms in this chapter are faster than this approach and compute no flows at all.

\begin{focusbox}
Three facts we will lean on repeatedly:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\lambda(\Gph)$ is at most the minimum weighted degree $\delta = \min_v \sum_{e\ni v} w(e)$, because isolating a single minimum-degree vertex is a cut. (The \emph{weighted degree} of $v$ is the total weight $\sum_{e\ni v} w(e)$ of the edges $e$ that touch $v$.)
  \item Every cut $(S,\bar S)$ separates \emph{some} pair $u\in S$, $v\in \bar S$. So the global cut equals $\min_{u,v}$ of the $u$--$v$ min-cut over all $\binom{n}{2}=n(n-1)/2$ pairs, but we will never need all pairs.
  \item Contracting an edge $\{u,v\}$ (merging $u$ and $v$ into one vertex, summing parallel edges, and dropping the self-loop) can only destroy cuts that separated $u$ from $v$. Any cut that kept $u$ and $v$ on the same side survives with its crossing edge weights unchanged. Contraction shrinks the vertex count while preserving all unseparated cuts.
\end{enumerate}
\end{focusbox}

\begin{historybox}
The global min-cut problem is old, but its direct combinatorial algorithms are relatively recent. Through the 1980s the standard route was $n-1$ max-flow computations; Hao and Orlin (1992) showed how to share work across them. In 1992 Nagamochi and Ibaraki found the first deterministic algorithm that uses no flows, and Stoer and Wagner (1994; journal version 1997) simplified it to the algorithm in this chapter. Meanwhile David Karger, as a Stanford graduate student, proposed random edge contraction in 1993; with Clifford Stein (1993; journal version 1996) he refined it to an $\Oh(n^2\log^3 n)$ algorithm. Karger's 2000 tree-packing algorithm reached $\Oh(m\log^3 n)$ time, only logarithmic factors more than the time needed to read the edges, and work continued into the 2020s with deterministic near-linear algorithms (Henzinger--Rao--Wang, Li). This chapter covers the two foundational algorithms: Stoer--Wagner and Karger(--Stein).
\end{historybox}

\section{Stoer--Wagner: greedy orderings find a cut}

The Stoer--Wagner algorithm is \emph{deterministic}: it makes no random choices, so every run on the same input does the same work and gives the same answer. It runs in $\Oh(nm + n^2\log n)$ time and has a short implementation. Its main subroutine, the \emph{minimum cut phase}, makes one greedy sweep over the vertices and produces a cut that isolates the final vertex visited. The sweep is also called a \emph{maximum adjacency search} (MA-search), because it always visits next the vertex with the most edge weight into the visited part. A lemma guarantees that this cut is an $s$--$t$ min-cut between the final two vertices $s$ and $t$.

\subsection{The minimum cut phase}

Start from an arbitrary vertex and grow a set $A$ one vertex at a time. At each step add the vertex \emph{most tightly connected} to the current $A$: the vertex $v\notin A$ maximizing
\[
  w(A,v) \;=\; \sum_{\substack{e=\{a,v\}\in E\\ a\in A}} w(e),
\]
the total weight of edges from $v$ into $A$. Ties are broken arbitrarily. Continue until all vertices are absorbed. Let $s$ and $t$ be the \emph{second-to-last} and \emph{last} vertices added. Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-phase-mechanics} shows a step in the middle and the end of a phase. The phase returns:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the \emph{cut-of-the-phase}: the cut $(\{t\},\,V\setminus\{t\})$ that isolates the last vertex. Its weight is $w(A_{n-1},t)$, where $A_{n-1}=V\setminus\{t\}$ is the set just before $t$ was added; this is the weighted degree of $t$;
  \item the pair $(s,t)$, which it then \emph{merges} (contracts) for the next phase.
\end{itemize}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{MinimumCutPhase}$(\Gph,w,a)$ --- one MA-search}
\label{ch11:alg-phase}
\DontPrintSemicolon
\KwIn{weighted graph $\Gph=(V,E,w)$, start vertex $a$}
\KwOut{cut-of-the-phase value, and the last two vertices $s,t$ in MA-order}
$A \gets \{a\}$\;
\For{each $v\in V\setminus\{a\}$}{ $\textit{key}[v]\gets w(\{a\},v)$ \tcp*{edge weight from $v$ to $a$} }
\textit{order} $\gets [a]$\;
\While{$\abs{A} < \abs{V}$}{
  pick $z\notin A$ with maximum $\textit{key}[z]$ \tcp*{most tightly attached}
  add $z$ to $A$; append $z$ to \textit{order}\;
  \For{each $v\notin A$ with edge $\{z,v\}\in E$}{
    $\textit{key}[v] \mathrel{+}= w(\{z,v\})$\;
  }
}
$s\gets$ second-to-last of \textit{order};\quad $t\gets$ last of \textit{order}\;
\Return $\big(\textit{key}[t]=w(A\setminus\{t\},t),\ s,\ t\big)$\;
\end{algorithm}

This is the same loop as Prim's MST algorithm and Dijkstra's algorithm, with a different priority: Prim keeps the \emph{minimum} attaching edge and Dijkstra the minimum tentative distance, while MA-search keeps the \emph{maximum} total attachment $\textit{key}[v]$, adding edge weights up rather than taking a minimum. With a binary heap each phase costs $\Oh(m\log n)$; with a Fibonacci heap, $\Oh(m + n\log n)$.

\subsection{The full algorithm}

Run $n-1$ phases. After each phase, record the cut-of-the-phase value, then contract $s$ and $t$ into a single super-vertex (parallel edges to a common neighbor are summed). Each contraction drops the vertex count by one, so after $n-1$ phases a single vertex remains. The global min-cut is the smallest cut-of-the-phase ever recorded.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{StoerWagner}$(\Gph,w)$}
\label{ch11:alg-sw}
\DontPrintSemicolon
\KwIn{connected weighted graph $\Gph=(V,E,w)$, $n=\abs V \ge 2$}
\KwOut{the global minimum cut value $\lambda(\Gph)$ and one cut achieving it}
$\textit{best}\gets +\infty$;\quad $\textit{bestSet}\gets\varnothing$\;
fix an arbitrary start vertex $a\in V$\;
\While{$\abs V > 1$}{
  $(\textit{cutval}, s, t)\gets \textsc{MinimumCutPhase}(\Gph,w,a)$\;
  \If{$\textit{cutval} < \textit{best}$}{
    $\textit{best}\gets \textit{cutval}$\;
    $\textit{bestSet}\gets$ set of original vertices currently merged into $t$\;
  }
  merge $s$ and $t$ into one super-vertex (sum weights of parallel edges, drop loops)\;
}
\Return $(\textit{best}, \textit{bestSet})$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Why should isolating the \emph{last} vertex of a maximum-adjacency sweep give a min-cut for the last \emph{two}? Every vertex that went before $t$ was, at its turn, at least as strongly attached to the visited set as $t$ was at that moment. So when a cut tries to separate $t$ from $s$, the vertices on $t$'s side bring along edges into the other side that weigh at least as much as $t$'s own attachments. Such a cut therefore costs at least as much as chopping off $t$ alone. The lemma below makes this precise.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Correctness}

Two claims combine into correctness: each phase returns a legitimate $s$--$t$ min-cut, and looping over contractions never loses the global optimum.

\begin{lemma}[Cut-of-the-phase is an $s$--$t$ min-cut]\label{ch11:lem-phase}
Let an MA-search on $(\Gph,w)$ visit vertices in order $v_1,v_2,\dots,v_n$, and let $s=v_{n-1}$, $t=v_n$. Then the cut $(\{t\}, V\setminus\{t\})$ is a minimum-weight cut among all cuts that separate $s$ from $t$. Its value equals $w(A_{n-1}, t)$, the weighted degree of $t$, where $A_{n-1}=\{v_1,\dots,v_{n-1}\}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Consider any cut $C=(X,\bar X)$ that separates $s$ from $t$; we show $w(C)\ge w(\{t\},V\setminus\{t\})$, i.e.\ $w(C)\ge \textit{key}[t]$ at the moment $t$ is absorbed.

Call a vertex $v_i$ (for $i\ge 2$) \emph{active} (with respect to $C$) if $v_i$ lands on the opposite side of $C$ from the vertex $v_{i-1}$ that immediately precedes it in MA-order. Because $C$ separates $s=v_{n-1}$ from $t=v_n$, the last vertex $t$ is active. Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-active} shows an example. For each vertex $v_i$ write $A_i = \{v_1,\dots,v_i\}$ and let $w(A_{i-1}, v_i)$ be the key of $v_i$ when it is added. Let $C_i$ be the cut $C$ restricted to $A_i$: keep only the vertices of $A_i$ and the edges between them, and split them as $C$ does. Its weight $w(C_i)$ counts the edges of $C$ with both endpoints in $A_i$. We prove by induction over the active vertices: for every active $v_i$,
\[
  w(A_{i-1}, v_i) \;\le\; w(C_i).
\tag{$\star$}\label{ch11:star}
\]

\emph{Base case.} Let $v_i$ be the first active vertex. Then $v_1,\dots,v_{i-1}$ all lie on one side of $C$ and $v_i$ on the other, so every edge from $v_i$ into $A_{i-1}$ crosses $C$. Hence $w(A_{i-1},v_i)=w(C_i)$, giving \eqref{ch11:star}.

\emph{Inductive step.} Let $v_i$ be active and $v_j$ the previous active vertex ($j<i$). Split the attachment of $v_i$ into edges hitting $A_{j-1}$ and edges hitting the block $\{v_j,\dots,v_{i-1}\}$:
\[
  w(A_{i-1}, v_i) \;=\; w(A_{j-1}, v_i) + w(\{v_j,\dots,v_{i-1}\}, v_i).
\]
\emph{The block term.} Because no vertex strictly between $v_j$ and $v_i$ is active, the block $v_j,v_{j+1},\dots,v_{i-1}$ never switches sides of $C$; and because $v_i$ is active, $v_i$ sits on the opposite side of $C$ from $v_{i-1}$, and therefore from every vertex in $\{v_j,\dots,v_{i-1}\}$. Every edge between $v_i$ and $\{v_j,\dots,v_{i-1}\}$ thus crosses $C$. These edges have both endpoints in $A_i$, so they are counted in $w(C_i)$, but not in $w(C_j)$, because $v_i\notin A_j$. Hence
\[
  w(\{v_j,\dots,v_{i-1}\}, v_i) \;\le\; w(C_i)-w(C_j).
\]
\emph{The earlier term.} Because $v_j$ was chosen before $v_i$, the greedy rule tells us that $v_j$ had an attachment key at least as large as $v_i$ when $v_j$ was added:
\[
  w(A_{j-1}, v_i) \;\le\; w(A_{j-1}, v_j) \;\le\; w(C_j),
\]
the last inequality being the inductive hypothesis \eqref{ch11:star} applied to the active vertex $v_j$. Adding the two bounds:
\begin{align*}
  w(A_{i-1}, v_i) &= w(A_{j-1}, v_i) + w(\{v_j,\dots,v_{i-1}\}, v_i) && \text{(split the edges)}\\
                  &\le w(C_j) + \bigl(w(C_i)-w(C_j)\bigr) && \text{(the two bounds)}\\
                  &= w(C_i),
\end{align*}
which proves \eqref{ch11:star} for $v_i$. Applying \eqref{ch11:star} to the last vertex $t=v_n$ (which is active) gives $\textit{key}[t]=w(A_{n-1},t)\le w(C_n)=w(C)$. Thus the cut isolating $t$ is no heavier than any $s$--$t$ cut $C$, certifying it as an $s$--$t$ min-cut.
\end{proof}

\begin{whybox}
The induction only ever compares an active vertex $v_i$ to the previous active vertex $v_j$. The key inequality is $w(A_{j-1},v_i)\le w(A_{j-1},v_j)$, which says that the maximum-adjacency rule preferred $v_j$ over $v_i$ at step $j$. The rest of the algebra checks that crossing edges only accumulate as $A_i$ grows. Informally, the visiting order ensures that the last vertex cannot be separated from the one before it without cutting at least as much weight as isolating the last vertex itself.
\end{whybox}

\begin{theorem}[Stoer--Wagner correctness]\label{ch11:thm-sw}
Algorithm~\ref{ch11:alg-sw} returns $\lambda(\Gph)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Induction on $n=\abs V$. For $n=2$ the only cut separates the two vertices and equals their connecting weight; the single phase reports exactly that.

Assume correctness for graphs with fewer than $n$ vertices. Run the first phase, producing $s,t$ and the cut-of-the-phase. There are two cases for the true global min-cut $C^\ast$ of $\Gph$, drawn in Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-sw-cases}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{$C^\ast$ separates $s$ from $t$.} By Lemma~\ref{ch11:lem-phase} the cut-of-the-phase is an $s$--$t$ min-cut, so its value is $\le w(C^\ast)$. Since no cut beats the global min-cut, the cut-of-the-phase value equals $w(C^\ast)=\lambda(\Gph)$, and the algorithm records it.
  \item \textbf{$C^\ast$ keeps $s$ and $t$ together.} Then $C^\ast$ survives merging $s$ and $t$: it is a cut of the contracted graph $\Gph/\{s,t\}$ of the same weight, and $\Gph/\{s,t\}$ has no cut cheaper than $\lambda(\Gph)$ (contraction only removes cuts, never creates lighter ones). By induction the recursion on $\Gph/\{s,t\}$ returns $\lambda(\Gph)$.
\end{itemize}
Either way some phase records $\lambda(\Gph)$. Every recorded value is the weight of a real cut of $\Gph$, so none is below $\lambda(\Gph)$, and the minimum over all phases is exactly $\lambda(\Gph)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{trapbox}
Two classic implementation bugs.
\emph{(1) Running on the wrong graph.} The start vertex $a$ of each phase is arbitrary (you may keep the same one or pick a new one each phase), but you must run MA-search on the \emph{current contracted} graph, not the original. If you forget to contract, every phase repeats the first one.
\emph{(2) Recovering the actual cut.} \texttt{bestSet} must be the set of \emph{original} vertices merged into $t$ at the phase that achieved the minimum, not the single super-vertex label. Keep, for each super-vertex, the list of original vertices it represents, and copy it when you update \texttt{best}. It is common to get the value right and the side wrong.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Complexity}

Each phase is one MA-search. With a Fibonacci heap, extracting the max-key vertex $n$ times and relaxing each edge once costs $\Oh(m + n\log n)$. There are $n-1$ phases, and contraction is absorbed into the next phase's adjacency build, so the total is
\[
  \Oh\!\big((n-1)(m + n\log n)\big) \;=\; \Oh(nm + n^2\log n).
\]
A binary heap gives $\Oh(nm\log n)$, and a dense $\Oh(n^2)$-per-phase array implementation (scan for the max, no heap) gives $\Oh(n^3)$, which is often the simplest to code and competitive when $m=\Th(n^2)$.

\section{Karger's random contraction}

Stoer--Wagner is greedy and exact. Karger's algorithm is \emph{randomized}: it makes a random choice at every step and keeps no priority queue. A single run finds the minimum cut only with some probability, but we can prove that probability is at least about $2/n^2$, small enough to need repeating but large enough that a polynomial number of repeats makes failure very unlikely. The algorithm repeats one step: pick an edge \emph{uniformly at random} (every edge of the current multigraph equally likely) and merge its two endpoints into a single super-vertex, until only two super-vertices remain. Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-contract} shows a run.

\subsection{Contraction}

To \emph{contract} an edge $e=\{u,v\}$: replace $u$ and $v$ by a single composite vertex $uv$. Every edge previously incident to $u$ or $v$ now connects to $uv$. Keep all \emph{multi-edges}: if both $u$ and $v$ had edges to a third vertex $x$, the super-vertex $uv$ now has two parallel edges to $x$. Parallel edges are kept because each one stands for an original edge between the two groups of vertices. Self-loops formed by copies of $e$ itself are discarded. Each contraction decreases the vertex count by exactly one. When only two super-vertices remain, the multiset of edges spanning them forms a cut of the original graph: the two super-vertices define the vertex partition.

\begin{lemma}[Contraction preserves untouched cuts]\label{ch11:lem-contract}
Let $\Gph'=\Gph/e$ be $\Gph$ with edge $e=\{u,v\}$ contracted. There is a weight-preserving bijection (a one-to-one matching) between cuts of $\Gph'$ and cuts of $\Gph$ that do \emph{not} separate $u$ from $v$. In particular, the min-cut of $\Gph'$ is at least the min-cut of $\Gph$: $\lambda(\Gph')\ge\lambda(\Gph)$, with equality unless every min-cut of $\Gph$ separates $u$ and $v$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A cut $(S',\bar S')$ of $\Gph'$ assigns the super-vertex $uv$ entirely to one side. Expand it to $\Gph$ by replacing $uv$ with both $u$ and $v$ on that same side; this is a cut of $\Gph$ that keeps $u,v$ together, and its crossing edges are in bijection with the crossing edges of $(S',\bar S')$ (the only edges that disappeared under contraction were copies of $e$, which never cross a cut keeping $u,v$ together). The map is reversible, hence a weight-preserving bijection. Cuts of $\Gph$ that separated $u$ from $v$ have no preimage, so $\Gph'$ has a subset of $\Gph$'s cuts; the minimum over a subset is no smaller.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{KargerContract}$(\Gph)$ --- one trial (unweighted multigraph)}
\label{ch11:alg-karger}
\DontPrintSemicolon
\KwIn{connected multigraph $\Gph=(V,E)$, $n=\abs V$}
\KwOut{a cut of $\Gph$ (its value is the number of surviving edges)}
\While{$\abs V > 2$}{
  pick an edge $e$ uniformly at random from the current edge multiset $E$\;
  $\Gph \gets \Gph / e$ \tcp*{contract: merge endpoints, keep multi-edges, drop loops}
}
\Return the cut given by the two remaining super-vertices (value $=\abs E$)\;
\end{algorithm}

For \emph{weighted} graphs, ``pick a uniformly random edge'' becomes ``pick edge $e$ with probability proportional to $w(e)$,'' and the surviving cut value is the total surviving weight. The analysis below is for the unweighted (multigraph) case; the weighted case follows by treating weight as parallel-edge multiplicity (or directly with the weighted sampling probabilities, see Exercise~\ref{ch11:ex-weighted}).

\begin{intuitionbox}
A min-cut has few edges. A uniformly random edge is therefore \emph{unlikely} to be one of them: most edges connect vertices on the same side of the cut. Each contraction of a non-cut edge merges two vertices that the min-cut keeps together, leaving the min-cut intact (Lemma~\ref{ch11:lem-contract}). If we never choose a min-cut edge across all $n-2$ contractions, the two surviving super-vertices are the two sides of the min-cut. The analysis below gives a lower bound on the probability of this event.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Success probability}

Fix one particular minimum cut $C^\ast$ of weight $k=\lambda(\Gph)$ (there may be several; we only need one to survive). The trial \emph{succeeds for $C^\ast$} if it never contracts an edge of $C^\ast$.

\begin{lemma}[Min-degree bound]\label{ch11:lem-mindeg}
At every stage of contraction, a multigraph on $r$ super-vertices whose min-cut is still $\ge k$ has at least $rk/2$ edges; hence each vertex has degree $\ge k$ and a uniformly random edge crosses $C^\ast$ with probability $\le k/(rk/2)=2/r$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Contraction never decreases the min-cut (Lemma~\ref{ch11:lem-contract}), so throughout the run every super-vertex has degree at least $k$ (isolating it is a cut, which costs $\ge k$). Each edge has two endpoints, so summing degrees counts every edge twice:
\[
  2\abs{E} \;=\; \sum_v \deg v \;\ge\; rk, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \abs{E} \;\ge\; \frac{rk}{2}.
\] The cut $C^\ast$ contributes exactly $k$ edges to the current multigraph as long as none has been contracted, so the chance the random edge is one of them is $k/\abs E \le k/(rk/2)=2/r$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Karger success probability]\label{ch11:thm-karger}
A single run of Algorithm~\ref{ch11:alg-karger} returns the fixed min-cut $C^\ast$ with probability at least
\[
  \Pr[\text{success}] \;\ge\; \binom{n}{2}^{-1} \;=\; \frac{2}{n(n-1)}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $E_i$ be the event ``the $i$-th contraction does not destroy $C^\ast$,'' i.e.\ the chosen edge is not a $C^\ast$-edge. We write $\Pr[E_i \mid E_1\cap\cdots\cap E_{i-1}]$ for the \emph{conditional probability} of $E_i$ given that $E_1,\dots,E_{i-1}$ all happened (so $C^\ast$ is still intact before step $i$). Before the $i$-th contraction there are $r=n-i+1$ vertices, so by Lemma~\ref{ch11:lem-mindeg},
\[
  \Pr[E_i \mid E_1\cap\cdots\cap E_{i-1}] \;\ge\; 1-\frac{2}{n-i+1}\;=\;\frac{n-i-1}{n-i+1}.
\]
The run succeeds if and only if all $n-2$ events occur. The probability that they all occur is the product of these conditional probabilities (the chain rule of probability):
\begin{align*}
  \Pr[\text{success}] &\ge \prod_{i=1}^{n-2}\frac{n-i-1}{n-i+1}\\
                      &= \frac{n-2}{n}\cdot\frac{n-3}{n-1}\cdot\frac{n-4}{n-2}\cdots\frac{2}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{3}.
\end{align*}
Each numerator cancels the denominator two factors later: the numerators $n-2,\dots,3$ cancel the denominators $n-2,\dots,3$ (Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-telescope} shows this for $n=8$). Only the last two numerators and the first two denominators survive:
\[
  \Pr[\text{success}] \;\ge\; \frac{2\cdot 1}{n(n-1)} \;=\; \binom{n}{2}^{-1}.
\]
\end{proof}

This proof uses only conditional probability and the chain rule
\[
  \Pr\Bigl[\,\bigcap_{i} E_i\Bigr] \;=\; \prod_{i} \Pr[E_i\mid E_1\cap\cdots\cap E_{i-1}],
\]
both developed in the companion probability book. The product telescopes: each factor's numerator cancels a later factor's denominator. Theorem~\ref{ch11:thm-karger} also gives the corollary below: an upper bound on the number of distinct min-cuts, obtained from the probability bound without listing any cuts.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{corollary}[At most $\binom{n}{2}$ minimum cuts]\label{ch11:cor-count}
A graph on $n$ vertices has at most $\binom{n}{2}$ distinct minimum cuts.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For each distinct min-cut $C_j$, the events ``a single Karger run outputs $C_j$'' are disjoint (a run outputs exactly one cut). Each has probability $\ge\binom{n}{2}^{-1}$ by Theorem~\ref{ch11:thm-karger}. Disjoint events have probabilities summing to at most $1$, so the number of distinct min-cuts is at most $\binom{n}{2}$. This bound is tight: the cycle $C_n$ has exactly $\binom{n}{2}$ min-cuts (cut any two of its $n$ edges).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Amplification}

A success probability of about $2/n^2$ is small, but repeating the trial with fresh random choices each time (so the trials are \emph{independent}) drives the failure probability down quickly. Run the trial $T$ times and keep the lightest cut found. The probability that \emph{all} $T$ trials miss $C^\ast$ is at most $\big(1-\binom{n}{2}^{-1}\big)^T$. Using $1-x\le e^{-x}$,
\[
  \Pr[\text{all }T\text{ fail}] \;\le\; \Big(1-\tbinom{n}{2}^{-1}\Big)^{T}
  \;\le\; \exp\!\Big(-T\big/\tbinom{n}{2}\Big).
\]
Choosing $T = \binom{n}{2}\ln n \approx \tfrac12 n^2\ln n$ drives the failure probability below $e^{-\ln n}=1/n$; choosing $T=c\,\binom{n}{2}\ln n$ gives failure $\le n^{-c}$. An algorithm whose failure probability is at most $1/n^c$ for a constant $c\ge 1$ of our choosing is said to succeed \emph{with high probability} (w.h.p.). Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-amplify} plots how fast the failure bound falls as the number of trials grows.

\begin{complexitybox}
One trial does $n-2$ contractions; a careful adjacency-matrix implementation makes each contraction $\Oh(n)$, so a trial is $\Oh(n^2)$. Amplifying to failure $\le 1/n$ needs $\Oh(n^2\log n)$ trials, for a total of
\[
  \Oh(n^2)\cdot\Oh(n^2\log n) \;=\; \Oh(n^4\log n).
\]
That is worse than Stoer--Wagner. Karger--Stein improves it by noticing that the early contractions are safe and only the late ones are dangerous, so we should not redo the safe early part in every trial.
\end{complexitybox}

\section{Karger--Stein: Branching on the Dangerous Tail}

When $r$ vertices remain, a contraction keeps the min-cut with probability at least $1-2/r$. When $r$ is large, each contraction is safe: with $r=1000$ vertices, the chance of destroying the min-cut is at most $2/1000 = 0.2\%$. As $r$ shrinks the risk grows: at $r=4$, the chance of severing the min-cut on that single step is up to $2/4 = 50\%$. Almost every failed trial loses the minimum cut during the final few contractions.

Restarting the algorithm from scratch on every failure throws away the safe early contractions. Karger and Stein instead contract down to a size at which the min-cut has survived with probability at least $1/2$, and then \emph{branch}: continue from there with two independent copies. Because the work doubles each time the graph shrinks by a factor of $\sqrt{2}$, most of the repeated effort goes to the late stages, where the min-cut is most likely to be lost.

\subsection{The partial-contraction guarantee}

\begin{lemma}[Surviving to $t$ vertices]\label{ch11:lem-survive}
Starting from $n$ vertices and contracting random edges until $t$ vertices remain, the fixed min-cut $C^\ast$ survives with probability at least
\[
  \Pr[\text{survive to }t] \;\ge\; \frac{\binom{t}{2}}{\binom{n}{2}} \;=\; \frac{t(t-1)}{n(n-1)}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Same telescoping product as Theorem~\ref{ch11:thm-karger}, but stop after $n-t$ contractions (when $t$ vertices remain):
\begin{align*}
  \prod_{i=1}^{n-t}\Bigl(1-\frac{2}{n-i+1}\Bigr)
    &= \prod_{r=t+1}^{n}\frac{r-2}{r} && \text{(write $r=n-i+1$)}\\
    &= \frac{(t-1)\,t}{n(n-1)} && \text{(the product telescopes)}\\
    &= \frac{\binom{t}{2}}{\binom{n}{2}}.
\end{align*}
\end{proof}

Choosing $t=\lceil 1+n/\sqrt2\rceil$ makes $\binom{t}{2}/\binom{n}{2}\ge \tfrac12$: contracting down to a $1/\sqrt2$ fraction of the vertices keeps the min-cut alive with probability at least one half. Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-survival} plots this bound for $n=50$. It stays high for most of the run and collapses only in the last few steps.

\subsection{The recursion}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{KargerStein}$(\Gph)$}
\label{ch11:alg-ks}
\DontPrintSemicolon
\KwIn{multigraph $\Gph$ with $n=\abs V$ vertices}
\KwOut{a cut of $\Gph$; it is a min-cut with probability $\Om(1/\log n)$ (Theorem~\ref{ch11:thm-ks})}
\If{$n \le 6$}{
  \Return a min-cut of $\Gph$ found exactly (by brute force or Stoer--Wagner)\;
}
$t \gets \big\lceil 1 + n/\sqrt2\,\big\rceil$\;
$\Gph_1 \gets$ contract random edges of $\Gph$ down to $t$ vertices\;
$\Gph_2 \gets$ contract random edges of $\Gph$ down to $t$ vertices \tcp*{independent of $\Gph_1$}
\Return $\min\big(\textsc{KargerStein}(\Gph_1),\ \textsc{KargerStein}(\Gph_2)\big)$\;
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Running time}

Let $T(n)$ be the time for one call. Contracting from $n$ to $t\approx n/\sqrt2$ vertices costs $\Oh(n^2)$ (each of $\Oh(n)$ contractions is $\Oh(n)$), done twice, and there are two recursive calls on graphs of size $\approx n/\sqrt2$:
\[
  T(n) = 2\,T\!\Big(\tfrac{n}{\sqrt2}+1\Big) + \Oh(n^2).
\]
To solve this, look at the recursion tree. At depth $i$ there are $2^i$ calls, each on about $n/(\sqrt2)^i$ vertices, so each costs $\Oh(n^2/2^i)$ and the whole level costs $\Oh(n^2)$. The graph reaches the base case after about $\log_{\sqrt2} n = 2\log_2 n$ levels, so the tree has $2^{2\log_2 n}=n^2$ leaves and
\[
  T(n) = \Oh(n^2\log n).
\]
(This is the balanced case of the \emph{master theorem}, the standard rule for recurrences $T(n)=a\,T(n/b)+f(n)$: here $a=2$, $b=\sqrt2$, and the per-level work $n^2=n^{\log_b a}$ is the same at every level.)

\subsection{Success probability of the recursion}

\begin{theorem}[Karger--Stein success]\label{ch11:thm-ks}
Algorithm~\ref{ch11:alg-ks} finds the min-cut with probability $\Om\!\big(1/\log n\big)$, that is, at least $c/\log n$ for some constant $c>0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $P(n)$ lower-bound the probability that a call on $n$ vertices finds $C^\ast$. Each of the two children succeeds if (a) the partial contraction to $t$ vertices preserves $C^\ast$ --- probability $\ge 1/2$ by the choice of $t$ --- and (b) the recursive call then finds it --- probability $\ge P(t)$. So a single child succeeds with probability $\ge \tfrac12 P(t)$, and the parent succeeds if \emph{either} child does:
\[
  P(n) \;\ge\; 1 - \Big(1 - \tfrac12 P(t)\Big)^2, \qquad t\approx \tfrac{n}{\sqrt2}.
\]
Index the recursion by depth: let $p_d$ be a lower bound on the success probability at a node $d$ levels above the leaves, with $p_0=1$ (the brute-force base case is exact). Because $x\mapsto 1-(1-\tfrac12 x)^2 = x - \tfrac14 x^2$ is strictly increasing on $[0,1]$, the recurrence yields
\[
  p_d \;\ge\; p_{d-1} - \tfrac14 p_{d-1}^2 \;=\; p_{d-1}\bigl(1-\tfrac14 p_{d-1}\bigr).
\]
Put $q_d=1/p_d$ and write $p'=p_{d-1}\in(0,1]$. Taking reciprocals,
\begin{align*}
  q_d \;=\; \frac{1}{p_d} &\le \frac{1}{p'(1-p'/4)} \;=\; \frac{4}{p'(4-p')}\\
      &= \frac{1}{p'} + \frac{1}{4-p'} && \text{(split the fraction)}\\
      &\le q_{d-1} + \frac13 && \text{(since $p'\le 1$)}.
\end{align*}
Therefore $q_d \le q_0 + d/3 = 1 + d/3$. The depth is $d=\Oh(\log n)$, so $q_d=\Oh(\log n)$, giving $p_d = \Om(1/\log n)$.
\end{proof}

So a \emph{single} Karger--Stein run succeeds with probability at least $c/\ln n$ for some constant $c>0$, and fails with probability at most $1-c/\ln n\le e^{-c/\ln n}$. Repeating it $T=(\ln^2 n)/c$ times with independent random choices and keeping the best cut gives
\[
  \Pr[\text{all $T$ runs fail}] \;\le\; \bigl(e^{-c/\ln n}\bigr)^{(\ln^2 n)/c} \;=\; e^{-\ln n} \;=\; \frac1n.
\]
That is $\Oh(\log^2 n)$ runs, for a total of
\[
  \Oh(n^2\log n)\cdot \Oh(\log^2 n) \;=\; \Oh(n^2\log^3 n).
\]

\begin{complexitybox}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Algorithm & Time & Guarantee \\
\midrule

\end{complexitybox}

\begin{trapbox}
A frequent implementation mistake is \emph{merging parallel edges} during contraction. If you collapse parallel edges into single edges without weights, you lose the multiplicities that make a random edge land across the min-cut with probability $k/\abs E$; the probability analysis assumes uniform sampling over the multiset of edges. Represent the multigraph with an adjacency \emph{matrix of integer counts} (or edge weights) and add them; never deduplicate edges. A second trap: in Karger--Stein, the two recursive branches must contract \emph{independently} from the parent graph. Reusing a single contracted graph for both branches throws away the second chance and pushes the success probability back toward the $1/n^2$ of plain Karger.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Tracing Karger by hand}

Take a tiny example where we can compute everything: the $4$-cycle $C_4$ with vertices $1,2,3,4$ and edges $12,23,34,41$, all weight $1$. Its min-cut is $2$ (removing $12$ and $34$ separates $\{1,4\}$ from $\{2,3\}$, for instance). By Corollary~\ref{ch11:cor-count} there are at most $\binom42=6$ min-cuts; for $C_4$ every pair of edges removed disconnects it, so there are exactly $6$, hitting the bound.

One trial: $n=4$, we contract twice.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Step 1} ($r=4$, $4$ edges). Pick a uniform edge, say $23$. Contract: super-vertex $23$, edges become $1{-}23$ (the old $12$), $23{-}4$ (the old $34$), $4{-}1$. Now $3$ vertices $\{1, 23, 4\}$ forming a triangle, $3$ edges.
  \item \textbf{Step 2} ($r=3$, $3$ edges). Pick a uniform edge, say $4{-}1$. Contract: super-vertex $14$. Two vertices $\{14, 23\}$ with edges $14{-}23$: the old $1{-}23$ and $23{-}4$, so a \emph{double} edge. Surviving cut value $=2$. Success --- this equals $\lambda=2$, separating $\{1,4\}$ from $\{2,3\}$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Applications}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Network reliability.} The edge connectivity $\lambda(\Gph)$ is the number of link failures a network can tolerate before splitting. Telecom and data-center networks are designed so $\lambda$ exceeds a target; the global min-cut shows the weakest place, where links should be added first. Karger's contraction view also yields the fastest known algorithms for estimating all-terminal network \emph{reliability} (the probability that the network stays connected when each link fails independently at random), because near-minimum cuts account for most of the failure probability and Corollary~\ref{ch11:cor-count} extends to bound the number of \emph{near}-minimum cuts (Exercise~\ref{ch11:ex-near}).

\textbf{Image segmentation and clustering.} Treat pixels (or data points) as vertices and put a heavy edge between similar neighbors. A light global cut separates dissimilar regions, and this is a starting point for graph-based segmentation. The min-cut often just slices off a few pixels, though; when you want two sides of comparable size you switch to the \emph{normalized} and \emph{sparsest} cuts of the later spectral chapters, which divide the cut weight by a measure of the sides' sizes. The plain global min-cut remains the exact, polynomial-time baseline.

\textbf{Chip design and partitioning.} Splitting an electronic circuit across two chips while cutting the fewest wires is a min-cut problem on the \emph{netlist}, the graph whose vertices are components and whose edges are wires. The unconstrained version is global min-cut. Requiring the two parts to have roughly equal size makes it NP-hard (no polynomial-time algorithm is known; see Chapter~4 for the term), which is why heuristics take over in practice.

\textbf{A structural bound.} Corollary~\ref{ch11:cor-count} (at most $\binom n2$ min-cuts) is used on its own in other algorithms: for example, in \emph{cut sparsification} (replacing a dense graph by a sparser weighted one that has nearly the same cut weights), and in the fact that all min-cuts of a graph can be stored together in $\Oh(n)$ space.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Global min-cut connects to several other parts of this book.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Max-flow (Chapter~\ref{ch12:networks}).} The $n-1$ flow computations above connect the global cut to $s$--$t$ cuts and the max-flow min-cut theorem; Stoer--Wagner is what you use to \emph{avoid} flows entirely.
  \item \textbf{Spectral graph theory.} Later chapters study the Laplacian matrix $\Lap$ of a graph. Its second-smallest eigenvalue measures how well the graph splits into two \emph{balanced} pieces with few edges between them (Cheeger's inequality), where the min-cut ignores balance. Random contraction and random walks rest on the same intuition: most edges lie inside the pieces, not between them.
  \item \textbf{Probability book.} Theorem~\ref{ch11:thm-karger} and Lemma~\ref{ch11:lem-survive} are standard chain-rule arguments, and the amplification step uses independence and the bound $(1-p)^T\le e^{-pT}$, which appear throughout randomized algorithms.
\end{itemize}
\end{connectionbox}

\section*{Summary}

Global min-cut asks for the cheapest split of a graph with no fixed endpoints. You can pay for $n-1$ max-flow computations, but two direct algorithms do better. Stoer--Wagner grows maximum-adjacency orderings; the trailing two vertices of each ordering give a true $s$--$t$ min-cut (Lemma~\ref{ch11:lem-phase}), and contracting them across $n-1$ phases sweeps out the global optimum deterministically in $\Oh(nm+n^2\log n)$. Karger contracts a uniformly random edge until two vertices remain; a fixed min-cut survives with probability $\ge\binom n2^{-1}$ (Theorem~\ref{ch11:thm-karger}), which both gives a randomized algorithm and proves there are at most $\binom n2$ min-cuts. Karger--Stein spends two independent recursive continuations on the risky tail of the contraction, reaching $\Oh(n^2\log^3 n)$ with high probability.

The next chapter asks a related question: how much of a commodity can we send from a source to a sink without exceeding the edges' capacities? It introduces maximum flow and proves the max-flow min-cut theorem, which says the largest flow equals the smallest $s$--$t$ cut.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Implement Stoer--Wagner]\label{ch11:ex-implsw}
Implement the dense $\Oh(n^3)$ version with an $n\times n$ weight matrix and a boolean ``merged'' array. Run it on Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-running} and confirm it returns $4$ with side $\{3,4,7,8\}$. Then add cut recovery: maintain for each super-vertex the list of original vertices it absorbed, and output the actual partition.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Implement Karger and Karger--Stein]\label{ch11:ex-implkarger}
Implement Karger with a count-matrix contraction in $\Oh(n^2)$ per trial. Empirically measure the success rate over many trials on $C_n$ for $n=5,\dots,20$ and compare to $\binom n2^{-1}$. Then implement Karger--Stein and verify the success rate is $\Om(1/\log n)$ per run.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The greedy lemma is local]\label{ch11:ex-tight}
In Lemma~\ref{ch11:lem-phase} we only proved the cut-of-the-phase is an $s$--$t$ min-cut for the \emph{last two} vertices. Give a small weighted graph and an MA-order in which the first cut-of-the-phase is \emph{not} a global min-cut, so the algorithm needs more than one phase to find $\lambda$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Counting min-cuts is tight]\label{ch11:ex-cycle}
Prove the cycle $C_n$ has exactly $\binom n2$ distinct minimum cuts, each of value $2$, matching Corollary~\ref{ch11:cor-count}. Then exhibit a graph with strictly fewer than $\binom n2$ min-cuts, confirming the bound is an inequality in general.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Weighted Karger]\label{ch11:ex-weighted}
Extend Algorithm~\ref{ch11:alg-karger} to weighted graphs by sampling edge $e$ with probability $w(e)/\sum_f w(f)$. Prove that Theorem~\ref{ch11:thm-karger} still holds with $k=\lambda(\Gph)$ the weighted min-cut. (Hint: redo Lemma~\ref{ch11:lem-mindeg} with weighted degrees; the bound $2/r$ on the probability of cutting $C^\ast$ is unchanged.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Min-cut from max-flow, carefully]\label{ch11:ex-flow}
Show that $\lambda(\Gph)=\min_{t\neq s}(\text{$s$--$t$ min-cut})$ for any fixed $s$. Why is it correct to fix $s$ and vary only $t$, rather than ranging over all $\binom n2$ pairs? Then argue that on an undirected graph you need at most $n-1$ flow computations, and look up (or sketch) why a \emph{Gomory--Hu tree}, a weighted tree on the same vertices whose lightest edge on the path between $u$ and $v$ equals the $u$--$v$ min-cut, can be built from only $n-1$ flow computations and so encodes all $\binom n2$ pairwise min-cuts.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Break a buggy contraction]\label{ch11:ex-break}
Suppose a colleague's Karger implementation merges parallel edges (keeps each neighbor relation as a single weight-$1$ edge, discarding multiplicity). Construct a graph on which this bug makes the algorithm return the min-cut with probability bounded \emph{away} from the correct $\binom n2^{-1}$, or where it systematically returns the wrong value. Explain precisely which step of Lemma~\ref{ch11:lem-mindeg} fails.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Near-minimum cuts]\label{ch11:ex-near}
Generalize Lemma~\ref{ch11:lem-survive}: show that for any $\alpha\ge 1$, the number of cuts of value at most $\alpha\lambda(\Gph)$ is $\Oh(n^{2\alpha})$. (Hint: stop the contraction earlier, bound the survival probability of a fixed $\alpha$-near-minimum cut, then use the disjointness argument of Corollary~\ref{ch11:cor-count}.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Which cut does Stoer--Wagner find?]\label{ch11:ex-which}
Stoer--Wagner returns \emph{a} min-cut; which one if there are several? Run it on $C_6$ (six min-cuts) under different start vertices and tie-breaking rules, and characterize which of the six it reports. Does the choice of start vertex change the returned \emph{value}? Should it?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A lower-bound intuition]\label{ch11:ex-lower}
Argue informally why any correct deterministic global min-cut algorithm that only queries edge weights must, in the worst case, ``look at'' $\Om(m)$ edges, and why Stoer--Wagner's $\Oh(nm+n^2\log n)$ is therefore within a near-linear-in-$n$ factor of an obvious barrier. (This is intuition, not a formal lower bound --- formal lower bounds for min-cut are subtle.)
\end{exercise}
