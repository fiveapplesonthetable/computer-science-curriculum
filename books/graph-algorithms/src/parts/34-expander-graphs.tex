\chapter{Expander Graphs}

In Chapter~33, we searched for bottlenecks to partition graphs into clusters. An expander is the opposite extreme: a graph with no bottlenecks anywhere, where every cut is large and information spreads fast.

A well-connected road network has a useful property. No matter how you split its cities into two substantial halves, you have to cut many roads. There is no narrow waist you can sever with a few cuts to isolate a large region. Every set of cities, unless it contains almost everybody or almost nobody, has a thick boundary leading out to the rest. Networks like this tolerate failures: remove a handful of links and the graph stays connected; start a rumor anywhere and it reaches every vertex in $\Oh(\log n)$ rounds; run a \emph{random walk} (at each step moving to a randomly chosen neighbor) and within $\Oh(\log n)$ steps it is nearly uniform across the whole graph, forgetting where it started.

These are \emph{expander graphs}. They are sparse, having only $\Oh(n)$ edges on $n$ vertices, yet for routing paths, spreading a random walk evenly (\emph{mixing}), and diffusing information they behave like the complete graph $K_n$. Combining dense-graph connectivity with a linear edge budget makes them useful across computer science: in fault-tolerant networks, in \emph{derandomization} (reducing or removing randomness in probabilistic algorithms), in \emph{error-correcting codes} (adding redundancy to data so corrupted bits can be repaired), in the analysis of \emph{Markov chains} (random processes whose next state depends only on the current state), and inside fast algorithms for cuts and flows.

Expansion can be measured in three ways that prove quantitatively equivalent, building on the Laplacian and spectral tools of Chapters~31--33: combinatorially by counting edges that leave vertex subsets, spectrally by the gap between the first two Laplacian eigenvalues, and probabilistically by testing random sparse graphs in which every vertex has the same degree. Each view answers a different question: how to certify that no sparse cut exists, how closely edge counts between sets match those of a random graph, how to construct expanders deterministically, and why walks, random-bit reductions, and linear-time decoders work.

\section{Two ways to measure a bottleneck}
\label{ch34:two-measures}

Fix an undirected graph $\Gph=(V,E)$ with $n=\abs{V}$ vertices. For most of this chapter we take $\Gph$ to be \emph{$d$-regular}: every vertex has degree $d$, meaning exactly $d$ neighbors. Regularity keeps the algebra clean, puts eigenvalues on a uniform scale, and matches standard constructions. How hard is it to cut $\Gph$ into two substantial pieces?

\begin{intuitionbox}
Compare the cycle $C_n$ with the complete graph $K_n$ (Figure~\ref{ch34:fig-extremes}). To split the cycle in half you cut only two edges; the cycle has a thin waist everywhere. To split $K_n$ in half you must cut roughly $n^2/4$ edges, a quarter of all vertex pairs. The cycle is the worst expander; the complete graph is the best. An expander is a sparse graph ($\Oh(n)$ edges, like the cycle) that resists balanced cuts (cutting $\Om(n)$ edges, like a constant-density caricature of $K_n$).
\end{intuitionbox}

For a subset $S\subseteq V$, write $\bar S = V\setminus S$ for everything in $V$ outside $S$. The \emph{edge boundary} of $S$ is
\[
  \partial S \;=\; \set{(u,v)\in E : u\in S,\ v\in\bar S},
\]
the set of edges with one endpoint in $S$ and the other outside $S$. Severing $S$ from $\bar S$ requires cutting $\abs{\partial S}$ edges.

\begin{definition}[Edge expansion / Cheeger constant]
\label{ch34:def-edge-expansion}
The \emph{edge expansion} (or \emph{Cheeger constant}) of $\Gph$ is
\[
  h(\Gph)\;=\;\min_{\substack{S\subseteq V\\ 0<\abs{S}\le n/2}}\ \frac{\abs{\partial S}}{\abs{S}}.
\]
\end{definition}

The restriction $0 < \abs{S}\le n/2$ normalizes the cut by the size of the \emph{smaller} side; without it, $S = \emptyset$ makes $0/0$ undefined and $S = V$ gives $0/n = 0$. By this definition, $h(\Gph)$ is large precisely when \emph{every} nonempty set of at most $n/2$ vertices sends out many edges per vertex. A large $h(\Gph)$ rules out bottlenecks.

\begin{definition}[Combinatorial expander]
\label{ch34:def-expander}
A family $\set{\Gph_n}$ of $d$-regular graphs (with $n\to\infty$ and $d$ fixed) is a \emph{family of expanders} if there is a constant $\eps>0$ such that $h(\Gph_n)\ge \eps$ for all $n$.
\end{definition}

Any single finite connected graph has some positive $h(\Gph) > 0$. Definition~\ref{ch34:def-expander} is a statement about an \emph{infinite family}: $h(\Gph_n)$ must stay bounded \emph{away from zero} as $n$ grows, while the degree $d$ stays fixed. Even with only $dn/2$ edges in total, at least $\eps\abs{S}$ edges leave every set $S$ of size at most $n/2$.

\begin{focusbox}
Three normalizations appear in the literature, and mixing them up causes off-by-$d$ errors:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Edge expansion} $h(\Gph)=\min_{0<\abs{S}\le n/2} \abs{\partial S}/\abs{S}$ (boundary edges per vertex; Definition~\ref{ch34:def-edge-expansion}).
  \item \textbf{Conductance} $\phi(\Gph)=\min_{0<\abs{S}\le n/2} \abs{\partial S}/(d\abs{S})$ on $d$-regular graphs (boundary edges per unit \emph{volume}, where the volume $\operatorname{vol}(S)=\sum_{v\in S}\deg(v)$ from Chapter~32 equals $d\abs{S}$). Thus $\phi(\Gph) = h(\Gph)/d \in [0,1]$.
  \item \textbf{Vertex expansion} counts boundary \emph{vertices} rather than boundary edges (Definition~\ref{ch34:def-vertex-expansion}).
\end{itemize}
We use $h$ for combinatorial counting and $\phi$ when comparing graphs of different degrees or analyzing random walks. Always check which normalization a theorem uses.
\end{focusbox}

A second measure counts distinct outside neighbors instead of crossing edges. For $S\subseteq V$, let
\[
  N(S) \;=\; \set{v\in \bar S : (u,v)\in E \text{ for some } u\in S}
\]
be the \emph{vertex boundary} of $S$.

\begin{definition}[Vertex expansion]
\label{ch34:def-vertex-expansion}
For parameters $K\ge 1$ (the maximum set size tested) and $A>0$ (the expansion factor), $\Gph$ is a \emph{$(K,A)$ vertex expander} if every set $S\subseteq V$ with $\abs{S}\le K$ satisfies $\abs{N(S)}\ge A\abs{S}$. In a bipartite graph $\Gph=(L\cup R,E)$, we call $\Gph$ a \emph{$(K,A)$ left vertex expander} if $\abs{N(S)}\ge A\abs{S}$ holds for every left subset $S\subseteq L$ with $\abs{S}\le K$.
\end{definition}

Vertex expansion with $A>1$ requires small sets to have strictly more outside neighbors than members. Error-correcting codes in Section~\ref{ch34:codes} rely on bipartite left expanders of this kind. Edge and vertex expansion differ whenever several boundary edges land on the same outside vertex (Figure~\ref{ch34:fig-fanout}). In a $d$-regular graph, each vertex in $N(S)$ can absorb at most $d$ edges from $\partial S$, and each edge in $\partial S$ contributes at most one vertex to $N(S)$, so
\[
  \frac{\abs{\partial S}}{d} \;\le\; \abs{N(S)} \;\le\; \abs{\partial S}.
\]
For fixed $d$, edge expansion and small-set vertex expansion therefore agree up to a factor of $d$.

\begin{labbox}
Take the $3$-cube $Q_3$: vertices are $3$-bit strings $000,\dots,111$, and edges connect strings that differ in a single bit. It is $3$-regular on $8$ vertices. Pick $S=\set{000,001,010,011}$ (all strings starting with $0$). Its boundary edges are the four ``flip the first bit'' edges $000$--$100$, $001$--$101$, $010$--$110$, and $011$--$111$, giving $\abs{\partial S}=4$, $\abs{S}=4$, and ratio $4/4=1$. Next try $S=\set{000}$: $\abs{\partial S}=3$, ratio $3/1=3$. Try $S=\set{000,111}$ (an \emph{antipodal} pair, opposite in every bit and therefore not adjacent): each vertex contributes $3$ boundary edges with none shared, giving ratio $6/2=3$. The half-cube split achieves the minimum, so $h(Q_3)=1$. Check two other $4$-vertex sets (say, a $4$-cycle face) to confirm none drops below ratio $1$.
\end{labbox}

\section{The spectral viewpoint and Cheeger's inequality}
\label{ch34:spectral}

Computing $h(\Gph)$ directly requires minimizing over roughly $2^{n-1}$ nonempty subsets of size at most $n/2$, and the associated decision problem is NP-hard. A single eigenvalue of the graph Laplacian estimates $h(\Gph)$ to within a quadratic factor and can be computed in polynomial time.

Recall from Chapter~31 the Laplacian $\Lap=\mathbf{D}-\Adjm$, where $\mathbf{D}$ is the diagonal degree matrix and $\Adjm$ is the adjacency matrix. For a $d$-regular graph $\mathbf{D}=d\mathbf{I}$, where $\mathbf{I}$ is the $n\times n$ identity matrix, so $\Lap=d\mathbf{I}-\Adjm$. Its quadratic form on any vector $x\in\R^n$ is
\[
  x^\top \Lap x \;=\; \sum_{(u,v)\in E}\bigl(x_u-x_v\bigr)^2,
\]
the sum of squared differences across edges, which measures how much $x$ varies from neighbor to neighbor. Because $x^\top\Lap x\ge 0$ for all $x$, $\Lap$ is symmetric positive semidefinite and its eigenvalues are real and nonnegative:
\[
  0=\lambda_1\le \lambda_2\le\cdots\le\lambda_n.
\]
The all-ones vector $\mathbf{1}$ satisfies $\Lap\mathbf{1}=0$, giving $\lambda_1=0$. As proved in Chapter~31, the multiplicity of $0$ equals the number of connected components, so $\lambda_2>0$ if and only if $\Gph$ is connected. For regular graphs, $\lambda_2$ is called the \emph{spectral gap} (the algebraic connectivity or Fiedler value of Chapters~31 and~32). It is the single spectral parameter that governs expansion.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Why does $\lambda_2$ detect sparse cuts? Suppose $\Gph$ has a sparse cut $(S,\bar S)$. A step-like vector $x$ that takes one constant value $+1/\abs{S}$ on $S$ and another $-1/\abs{\bar S}$ on $\bar S$ is orthogonal to $\mathbf{1}$ (meaning $\sum_{v\in V}x_v = 0$), has zero difference $(x_u-x_v)^2=0$ on all internal edges, and pays only on the few crossing edges in $\partial S$. By the Courant--Fischer characterization (Chapter~31),
\[
  \lambda_2\;=\;\min_{x\perp\mathbf{1},\ x\ne 0}\frac{x^\top\Lap x}{x^\top x},
\]
so this low-energy test vector forces $\lambda_2$ to be small. Thus a sparse cut forces a small $\lambda_2$. Cheeger's inequality proves the harder converse: a small $\lambda_2$ \emph{forces} a sparse cut to exist, and sorting the second eigenvector finds one.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{theorem}[Cheeger's inequality, discrete form]
\label{ch34:thm-cheeger}
For any connected $d$-regular graph $\Gph$,
\[
  \frac{\lambda_2}{2}\;\le\; h(\Gph)\;\le\;\sqrt{2\,d\,\lambda_2}.
\]
Dividing by $d$ gives the conductance form $\phi(\Gph)=h(\Gph)/d$:
\[
  \frac{\lambda_2}{2d}\;\le\; \phi(\Gph)\;\le\;\sqrt{\frac{2\lambda_2}{d}}.
\]
\end{theorem}

The two inequalities play complementary roles. The left inequality bounds expansion from above by $\lambda_2$ and proves that a small cut forces a small $\lambda_2$. The right inequality (proved constructively by \emph{Cheeger rounding}) guarantees that if $\lambda_2$ is bounded below by a positive constant, then $h(\Gph)$ is bounded below as well, matching Definition~\ref{ch34:def-expander}:
\begin{center}
\emph{A family of $d$-regular graphs is a family of expanders if and only if $\lambda_2\ge \eps'$ for a constant $\eps'>0$.}
\end{center}
Up to the square-root loss on the right, a spectral gap and combinatorial expansion certify the same property.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Proof of the lower bound $h(\Gph)\ge \lambda_2/2$ (equivalently $\lambda_2\le 2h(\Gph)$).} Let $S\subseteq V$ achieve $h(\Gph)=\abs{\partial S}/\abs{S}$ with $0<\abs{S}\le n/2$. Define the test vector
\[
  x_v=\begin{cases}\ \ \abs{\bar S} & v\in S,\\[2pt] -\abs{S} & v\in\bar S.\end{cases}
\]
Then $\sum_{v\in V} x_v=\abs{S}\abs{\bar S}-\abs{\bar S}\abs{S}=0$, so $x\perp\mathbf{1}$. Any edge inside $S$ or inside $\bar S$ contributes $0$ to $x^\top\Lap x$, whereas each boundary edge in $\partial S$ contributes $(\abs{\bar S}-(-\abs{S}))^2=(\abs{\bar S}+\abs{S})^2=n^2$. Hence $x^\top\Lap x=\abs{\partial S}\,n^2$. For the squared norm,
\begin{align*}
  x^\top x &= \abs{S}\,\abs{\bar S}^2 + \abs{\bar S}\,\abs{S}^2 \\
           &= \abs{S}\abs{\bar S}\bigl(\abs{\bar S}+\abs{S}\bigr) \\
           &= \abs{S}\abs{\bar S}\,n.
\end{align*}
Substituting into the Courant--Fischer minimum gives
\begin{align*}
  \lambda_2 &\le \frac{x^\top\Lap x}{x^\top x} \\
            &= \frac{\abs{\partial S}\,n^2}{\abs{S}\abs{\bar S}\,n} \\
            &= \frac{\abs{\partial S}}{\abs{S}}\cdot\frac{n}{\abs{\bar S}}.
\end{align*}
Since $\abs{S}\le n/2$, we have $\abs{\bar S}=n-\abs{S}\ge n/2$, so $n/\abs{\bar S}\le 2$ and $\lambda_2\le 2\abs{\partial S}/\abs{S}=2h(\Gph)$. \hfill$\square$
\end{whybox}

The upper bound $h(\Gph)\le\sqrt{2d\lambda_2}$ takes a second eigenvector $x$ of $\Lap$ (a Fiedler vector), orders the vertices $x_{v_1}\le x_{v_2}\le\cdots\le x_{v_n}$, and tests only the $n-1$ prefix \emph{sweep cuts} $S_t=\set{v_1,\dots,v_t}$ (equivalently $\set{v : x_v \le \tau}$ for a threshold $\tau$ between $x_{v_t}$ and $x_{v_{t+1}}$; see Figure~\ref{ch34:fig-sweep}). As proved for general graphs in Chapter~32, choosing a random threshold on squared shifted coordinates cuts each edge $(u,v)$ with probability proportional to $\abs{x_u^2-x_v^2}$, and applying the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality ($\sum_e a_e b_e \le \sqrt{\sum_e a_e^2}\sqrt{\sum_e b_e^2}$) to
\[
  \sum_{(u,v)\in E}\abs{x_u^2-x_v^2} \;=\; \sum_{(u,v)\in E}\abs{x_u-x_v}\,\abs{x_u+x_v}
\]
proves that at least one prefix cut $S_t$ satisfies $\abs{\partial S_t}/\min(\abs{S_t},\abs{\bar S_t})\le\sqrt{2d\lambda_2}$. Algorithm~\ref{ch34:alg-cheeger} writes out this sweep procedure explicitly.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Spectral partitioning / Cheeger rounding}
\label{ch34:alg-cheeger}
\KwIn{connected $d$-regular graph $\Gph=(V,E)$ on $n$ vertices.}
\KwOut{a subset $S^\star\subseteq V$ with $0<\abs{S^\star}\le n/2$ and $\abs{\partial S^\star}/\abs{S^\star}\le\sqrt{2d\lambda_2}$.}
Form $\Lap=d\mathbf{I}-\Adjm$\;
Compute $\lambda_2$ and a unit eigenvector $x\perp\mathbf{1}$ (e.g.\ via the Lanczos method while projecting out $\mathbf{1}$ at each step)\;
Sort vertices so that $x_{v_1}\le x_{v_2}\le\cdots\le x_{v_n}$\;
$\mathit{best}\gets\infty$;\quad $S^\star\gets\emptyset$\;
\For{$t\gets 1$ \KwTo $n-1$}{
  $S_t\gets\set{v_1,\dots,v_t}$\;
  update $\abs{\partial S_t}$ incrementally (add edges from $v_t$ to $\bar S_t$, subtract edges from $v_t$ to $S_{t-1}$)\;
  $r\gets \abs{\partial S_t}/\min(\abs{S_t},n-\abs{S_t})$\;
  \If{$r<\mathit{best}$}{
    $\mathit{best}\gets r$\;
    \lIf{$t\le n/2$}{$S^\star\gets S_t$} \lElse{$S^\star\gets V\setminus S_t$}
  }
}
\Return $S^\star$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{complexitybox}
Computing the eigenvector dominates. Because $\Lap$ has at most $dn+n$ nonzero entries, a near-linear-time Laplacian solver (Chapter~41) approximates $\lambda_2$ and $x$ in $\widetilde\Oh(dn)$ time, where $\widetilde\Oh(\cdot)$ hides $\poly(\log n)$ factors; standard iterative eigensolvers take $\Oh(dn)$ work per matrix--vector product times the iteration count. Sorting takes $\Oh(n\log n)$, and the sweep over $t=1,\dots,n-1$ (Figure~\ref{ch34:fig-sweep}) takes $\Oh(dn)$ total time because each vertex enters the prefix once and each of its $d$ incident edges changes boundary status twice. The returned set $S^\star$ satisfies $\abs{\partial S^\star}/\abs{S^\star}\le\sqrt{2d\lambda_2}$.
\end{complexitybox}

\begin{trapbox}
Cheeger rounding does \emph{not} guarantee finding the exact sparsest cut; it finds a cut within a \emph{quadratic} factor of optimal. When the true $h(\Gph)$ is $\Th(1)$ on a bounded-degree graph, $\sqrt{2d\lambda_2}$ is also $\Th(1)$, so the bound loses only a constant factor. When $\lambda_2$ is tiny (for example $\lambda_2=\Th(1/n^2)$ on the cycle $C_n$), $\sqrt{2d\lambda_2}=\Th(1/n)$ while $\lambda_2/2=\Th(1/n^2)$, leaving an $\Oh(n)$ multiplicative gap between the lower and upper bounds. That worst-case loss is inherent to spectral rounding and motivates the semidefinite-programming (SDP) approximations for sparsest cut in Chapter~38.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{marginnotebox}
The discrete Cheeger inequality, adapted from Riemannian geometry by Dodziuk (1984) and by Alon and Milman (1985), turns $\lambda_2$ into a polynomial-time certificate of expansion. Alon's 1986 paper ``Eigenvalues and expanders'' established this spectral bridge for expander constructions and Markov chain mixing.
\end{marginnotebox}

\subsection{The adjacency spectrum and the second eigenvalue}
\label{ch34:adj-spectrum}

For $d$-regular graphs, an equivalent and widely used phrasing works with the eigenvalues of the \emph{adjacency} matrix $\Adjm$. Because $\Lap=d\mathbf{I}-\Adjm$, ordering the eigenvalues of $\Adjm$ downward as
\[
  d = \mu_1 \ge \mu_2 \ge \cdots \ge \mu_n \ge -d
\]
pairs each $\mu_i$ with the Laplacian eigenvalue $\lambda_i = d - \mu_i$. (Throughout this chapter, $\mu_i$ denotes an eigenvalue of $\Adjm$, not of the normalized Laplacian as in Chapter~32.) The top adjacency eigenvalue is $\mu_1=d$ with eigenvector $\mathbf{1}$, corresponding to $\lambda_1=0$. The Laplacian spectral gap $\lambda_2=d-\mu_2$ is large precisely when $\mu_2$ is well below $d$.

Define
\[
  \mu(\Gph)\;=\;\max\bigl(\abs{\mu_2},\abs{\mu_n}\bigr),
\]
the largest magnitude among all eigenvalues orthogonal to $\mathbf{1}$. Including $\abs{\mu_n}$ rules out eigenvalues near $-d$: as Exercise~\ref{ch34:ex-bipartite} verifies, $\mu_n=-d$ occurs when $\Gph$ is bipartite, where a random walk alternates between the two sides forever instead of mixing. A $d$-regular graph on $n$ vertices is called a \emph{one-sided $(n,d,\alpha)$-expander} if $\mu_2\le\alpha$, and a \emph{two-sided $(n,d,\alpha)$-expander} if $\mu(\Gph)\le\alpha$ with $\alpha$ much smaller than $d$. (Many authors write $\lambda$ for the bound $\alpha$; to keep $\lambda_i$ reserved for Laplacian eigenvalues, we write $\mu(\Gph)$ or $\mu$ in formulas.) Smaller $\mu(\Gph)/d$ means stronger expansion and faster mixing.

\begin{formalbox}
\textbf{How small can $\mu(\Gph)$ be?} Not arbitrarily close to zero. The \emph{Alon--Boppana bound} states that every infinite family of $d$-regular graphs satisfies
\[
  \mu_2(\Gph_n) \;\ge\; 2\sqrt{d-1} - o(1),
\]
where $o(1)$ denotes a quantity tending to $0$ as $n\to\infty$. Thus $2\sqrt{d-1}$ is the asymptotic floor for $\mu_2$. Connected $d$-regular graphs that meet this floor, satisfying $\mu_2\le 2\sqrt{d-1}$ (or $\mu(\Gph)\le 2\sqrt{d-1}$ in the strict two-sided sense), are called \emph{Ramanujan graphs}. Explicit infinite families exist (Lubotzky--Phillips--Sarnak, 1988; Margulis, 1988; Marcus--Spielman--Srivastava for bipartite graphs of every degree, 2013) and achieve the largest spectral gap a $d$-regular graph family can have.
\end{formalbox}

Figure~\ref{ch34:fig-ramanujan-spectrum} shows this layout on the interval $[-d,d]$: the all-ones vector gives $\mu_1=d$, while a two-sided Ramanujan graph confines $\mu_2,\dots,\mu_n$ to $[-2\sqrt{d-1},2\sqrt{d-1}]$, leaving wide gaps to both $+d$ and $-d$.

\section{The Expander Mixing Lemma}
\label{ch34:eml}

A small $\mu(\Gph)$ makes a deterministic graph \emph{behave like a random graph} on every pair of vertex subsets. In a random $d$-regular graph, each ordered pair $(u,v)\in S\times T$ is adjacent with probability roughly $d/n$, so we expect about $\frac{d}{n}\abs{S}\abs{T}$ crossing pairs. The Expander Mixing Lemma proves that \emph{every} $d$-regular graph approaches this ideal, with worst-case discrepancy proportional to $\mu(\Gph)$ (Figure~\ref{ch34:fig-eml}).

\begin{theorem}[Expander Mixing Lemma]
\label{ch34:thm-eml}
Let $\Gph=(V,E)$ be $d$-regular on $n$ vertices with $\mu=\max(\abs{\mu_2},\abs{\mu_n})$. For all subsets $S,T\subseteq V$,
\[
  \Bigl\lvert\,e(S,T)-\frac{d}{n}\abs{S}\,\abs{T}\,\Bigr\rvert\;\le\;\mu\sqrt{\abs{S}\,\abs{T}},
\]
where $e(S,T)=\abs{\set{(u,v)\in S\times T:(u,v)\in E}}$ counts ordered adjacent pairs $(u,v)$ with $u\in S$ and $v\in T$, so an undirected edge with both endpoints in $S\cap T$ is counted twice.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Proof.} Let $\mathbf{1}_S,\mathbf{1}_T\in\set{0,1}^n$ be the $0$--$1$ indicator vectors of $S$ and $T$ (so $(\mathbf{1}_S)_v = 1$ if $v\in S$ and $0$ otherwise). By definition of $\Adjm$, $e(S,T)=\mathbf{1}_S^\top\Adjm\,\mathbf{1}_T$. Decompose each indicator into its projection onto $\mathbf{1}$ and an orthogonal remainder:
\[
  \mathbf{1}_S=\frac{\abs{S}}{n}\mathbf{1} + s^\perp,\qquad \mathbf{1}_T=\frac{\abs{T}}{n}\mathbf{1} + t^\perp,
\]
with $s^\perp\perp\mathbf{1}$ and $t^\perp\perp\mathbf{1}$. Since $\Adjm\mathbf{1}=d\mathbf{1}$ and $\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{1}=n$,
\begin{align*}
  \mathbf{1}_S^\top\Adjm\,\mathbf{1}_T
  &= \frac{\abs{S}\abs{T}}{n^2}\,\mathbf{1}^\top\Adjm\mathbf{1} + (s^\perp)^\top\Adjm\,t^\perp \\
  &= \frac{d}{n}\abs{S}\abs{T} + (s^\perp)^\top\Adjm\,t^\perp,
\end{align*}
where both cross terms vanish because $\Adjm\mathbf{1}=d\mathbf{1}$ is orthogonal to $s^\perp$ and $t^\perp$. On the subspace orthogonal to $\mathbf{1}$, every eigenvalue of symmetric $\Adjm$ has magnitude at most $\mu$, so $\norm{\Adjm\,t^\perp}\le\mu\norm{t^\perp}$ in the Euclidean norm $\norm{v}=\sqrt{v^\top v}$. By Cauchy--Schwarz,
\[
  \abs{(s^\perp)^\top\Adjm\,t^\perp}\;\le\;\norm{s^\perp}\,\norm{\Adjm\,t^\perp}\;\le\;\mu\,\norm{s^\perp}\,\norm{t^\perp}.
\]
Because $\frac{\abs{S}}{n}\mathbf{1}$ and $s^\perp$ are orthogonal, the Pythagorean theorem gives
\begin{align*}
  \norm{s^\perp}^2 &= \norm{\mathbf{1}_S}^2 - \Bigl\lVert\frac{\abs{S}}{n}\mathbf{1}\Bigr\rVert^2 \\
                   &= \abs{S} - \frac{\abs{S}^2}{n} \;\le\; \abs{S},
\end{align*}
and similarly $\norm{t^\perp}^2\le\abs{T}$. Substituting $\norm{s^\perp}\le\sqrt{\abs{S}}$ and $\norm{t^\perp}\le\sqrt{\abs{T}}$ completes the proof. \hfill$\square$
\end{whybox}

Expressing $e(S,T)$ as the bilinear form $\mathbf{1}_S^\top\Adjm\,\mathbf{1}_T$ lets linear algebra do the counting: the $\mathbf{1}$-direction produces the random-graph average $\frac{d}{n}\abs{S}\abs{T}$, and orthogonality bounds the error by $\mu$.

\begin{labbox}
Check Theorem~\ref{ch34:thm-eml} numerically on the $3$-cube $Q_3$ ($d=3$, $n=8$; Figure~\ref{ch34:fig-eml}, right). Its adjacency eigenvalues are $3,1,1,1,-1,-1,-1,-3$, so $\mu=\max(\abs{1},\abs{-3})=3=d$: $Q_3$ fails two-sided expansion because it is bipartite. Let $S$ be the four even-weight strings (containing an even number of $1$s) and $T$ the four odd-weight strings (an odd number of $1$s). Every single-bit flip changes parity, so all $12$ edges of $Q_3$ cross between $S$ and $T$:
\[
  e(S,T) = 12, \qquad \frac{d}{n}\abs{S}\abs{T} = \frac{3}{8}\cdot 4\cdot 4 = 6, \qquad \Bigl\lvert e(S,T)-\frac{d}{n}\abs{S}\abs{T}\Bigr\rvert = 6.
\]
Theorem~\ref{ch34:thm-eml} bounds this discrepancy by $\mu\sqrt{\abs{S}\abs{T}}=3\cdot 4=12$, leaving a factor-of-two margin. That margin comes solely from relaxing $\norm{s^\perp}^2 = \abs{S}(1-\abs{S}/n) = 2$ to $\abs{S}=4$ in the proof: the unrelaxed bound $\mu\norm{s^\perp}\norm{t^\perp}=3\cdot\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{2}=6$ holds with equality, attained by the $\mu_8=-3$ bipartition eigenvector $(+1\text{ on }S,\,-1\text{ on }T)$. Bipartiteness ($\mu_n=-d$) prevents uniform mixing, which is why Theorem~\ref{ch34:thm-eml} requires $\max(\abs{\mu_2},\abs{\mu_n})$ rather than $\mu_2$ alone.
\end{labbox}

\begin{corollary}[Spectral expansion controls edge expansion]
\label{ch34:cor-eml-cut}
For $\abs{S}\le n/2$, setting $T=\bar S$ in Theorem~\ref{ch34:thm-eml} gives
\[
  \abs{\partial S}=e(S,\bar S)\;\ge\; \frac{d}{n}\abs{S}\abs{\bar S}-\mu\sqrt{\abs{S}\abs{\bar S}}.
\]
When $\mu$ is much smaller than $d$, this directly bounds balanced cuts: at $\abs{S}=\abs{\bar S}=n/2$ it gives $\abs{\partial S}\ge \bigl(\tfrac{d}{2}-\mu\bigr)\tfrac{n}{2}$. For a uniform lower bound across \emph{all} sets with $0<\abs{S}\le n/2$, the lower Cheeger inequality $\lambda_2\le 2h(\Gph)$ is sharper: since $\lambda_2 = d-\mu_2$ and $\mu_2\le\mu$,
\begin{align*}
  h(\Gph) &\ge \frac{\lambda_2}{2} \\
          &= \frac{d-\mu_2}{2} \\
          &\ge \frac{d-\mu}{2}.
\end{align*}
Any spectral gap $d-\mu$ bounded away from $0$ forces constant edge expansion.
\end{corollary}

\section{Random graphs are expanders}
\label{ch34:random}

Do sparse expanders exist for every large $n$? Almost every sparse regular graph is one. To sample a \emph{random $d$-regular graph} on $n$ labeled vertices (with $dn$ even), use the \emph{configuration} (or \emph{pairing}) \emph{model} shown in Figure~\ref{ch34:fig-pairing}: attach $d$ stub ``half-edges'' to each vertex, and pair the $dn$ half-edges uniformly at random into $dn/2$ edges. (Pairing two stubs on the same vertex or between the same pair creates self-loops or parallel edges; for fixed $d$, the graph has none of either with probability bounded away from $0$, so any property holding with probability $1-o(1)$ in the pairing model transfers directly to uniformly random simple $d$-regular graphs.)

We prove combinatorial expansion using two elementary probability tools: the \emph{union bound} (the probability that at least one event in a collection occurs is at most the sum of their individual probabilities) and its equivalent \emph{first-moment} phrasing (if the expected count $\mathbb{E}[X]$ of ``bad'' sets tends to $0$, then $\Pr[X\ge 1]\le\mathbb{E}[X]\to 0$, so no bad set exists with probability $1-o(1)$).

\begin{theorem}[A random regular graph expands]
\label{ch34:thm-random-expand}
Fix $d\ge 3$. There is a constant $\eps>0$ (depending only on $d$) such that a uniformly random $d$-regular graph on $n$ vertices satisfies $h(\Gph)\ge\eps$ with probability $1-o(1)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Proof sketch (first-moment / union bound).} Call a subset $S\subseteq V$ of size $\abs{S}=k\le n/2$ \emph{bad} if $\abs{\partial S}<\eps k$, meaning fewer than $\eps k$ edges leave $S$. We bound the expected number of bad sets across all $1\le k\le n/2$ and show it is $o(1)$ when $\eps>0$ is chosen small enough.

Fix $k$. There are $\binom{n}{k}$ sets $S$ of size $k$. For $S$ to be bad in the pairing model, at most $\eps k$ of its $dk$ half-edges may cross to $\bar S$; the remaining at least $(d-\eps)k$ half-edges must pair \emph{within} $S$. Expose these internal pairs one by one: at each step, an unmatched half-edge in $S$ finds its partner inside $S$ with probability at most $dk/(dn)=k/n$. Because each internal edge consumes \emph{two} half-edges of $S$, the $(d-\eps)k$ internal half-edges form $(d-\eps)k/2$ pairs. Accounting for the $\binom{dk}{\eps k}$ ways to choose which half-edges are allowed to cross, the probability that a fixed $S$ is bad is up to constants at most
\[
  \binom{dk}{\eps k}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{(d-\eps)k/2}.
\]
Using the standard binomial upper bound $\binom{m}{r}\le (em/r)^r$ (where $e\approx 2.718$ is Euler's number), we have $\binom{n}{k}\le (en/k)^k$ and $\binom{dk}{\eps k}\le c^k$ for a constant $c=(ed/\eps)^\eps$ that approaches $1$ as $\eps\to 0$. Multiplying by $\binom{n}{k}$, the expected number of bad sets of size $k$ is at most
\[
  \Bigl[c\,e\,(k/n)^{(d-\eps)/2-1}\Bigr]^{k}.
\]
Because $d\ge 3$, choosing $\eps<1/2$ makes the exponent $(d-\eps)/2-1 \ge 1/4 > 0$. Thus for $k\le\delta n$ with $\delta>0$ a small constant, the bracket is below $1/2$: for $1\le k\le\log_2 n$ each term is $n^{-\Om(1)}$, and for $\log_2 n < k \le \delta n$ each term is below $2^{-k}<1/n$, so their sum over $1\le k\le\delta n$ is $o(1)$. For the remaining balanced range $\delta n \le k \le n/2$ (where $k/n$ is a constant), counting exact pairings with at most $\eps k$ crossing edges (Bollob\'as, 1988) shows that for small enough $\eps$ the expected count is exponentially small in $n$. Summing over all $k$ gives $\mathbb{E}[\text{bad sets}]=o(1)$, so $h(\Gph)\ge\eps$ with probability $1-o(1)$. \hfill$\square$
\end{whybox}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Why does $d\ge 3$ succeed where $d=2$ fails? Look at the exponent $(d-\eps)/2 - 1$ in the proof: the $-1$ comes from the $\binom{n}{k}\approx (en/k)^k$ choices of $S$, while $(d-\eps)/2$ comes from the $(d-\eps)k/2$ independent internal edge events needed to trap $S$. When $d=2$, a random $2$-regular graph is a union of disjoint cycles, $(2-\eps)/2 - 1 < 0$, and expansion fails. As soon as $d\ge 3$, trapping $S$ costs $(d-\eps)k/2 > k$ improbable internal matches, overpowering the $\binom{n}{k}$ union bound. Expansion is the generic behavior of sparse graphs of degree at least $3$; cycles and grids are structured exceptions.
\end{intuitionbox}

Friedman (2008) proved Alon's conjecture, giving the matching spectral theorem: a random $d$-regular graph comes arbitrarily close to the Ramanujan bound.

\begin{theorem}[Friedman, informal]
\label{ch34:thm-friedman}
Fix $d\ge 3$. For every constant $\eps>0$, a uniformly random $d$-regular graph on $n$ vertices satisfies $\mu(\Gph)\le 2\sqrt{d-1}+\eps$ with probability $1-o(1)$.
\end{theorem}

Random graphs settle existence, but they do not supply a deterministic, pre-certified blueprint when wiring hardware or shipping an error-correcting code where even a rare bad draw or an $\Oh(n^2)$-space adjacency table is unacceptable. That motivates explicit constructions.

\section{Explicit constructions, conceptually}
\label{ch34:constructions}

A probabilistic proof certifies that expanders abound without naming one. Hardware interconnects, deterministic algorithms, and error-correcting codes require an \emph{explicit} family: a deterministic rule that constructs the $n$-vertex graph in $\poly(n)$ time---or, better still, \emph{strongly explicit}, computing the $i$-th neighbor of any $b$-bit vertex label in time polynomial in $b=\log_2 n$ without storing the whole graph---together with a once-and-for-all proof of its spectral or vertex expansion. Three main families achieve this.

\paragraph{Algebraic constructions.} The earliest explicit expanders come from group theory and number theory. In the \emph{Margulis} (1973) construction, vertices are pairs $(x,y)\in\Z_m\times\Z_m$ of integers modulo $m$, and each $(x,y)$ connects to $8$ affine neighbors (linear combinations plus shifts modulo $m$):
\[
  (x\pm y,\,y),\quad (x,\,y\pm x),\quad (x\pm y+1,\,y),\quad (x,\,y\pm x+1) \pmod{m}.
\]
Fourier analysis on $\Z_m\times\Z_m$ bounds the eigenvalues away from $d=8$. Stronger still are the \emph{Lubotzky--Phillips--Sarnak} (LPS, 1988) graphs, built as \emph{Cayley graphs}: given a finite group $\Gamma$ and a symmetric generating set $S\subset\Gamma$ of size $d$, the Cayley graph takes $\Gamma$ as its vertex set and connects each $g\in\Gamma$ to $g\cdot s$ for all $s\in S$, automatically producing a $d$-regular graph. LPS chose $\Gamma = \mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb{F}_q)$ ($2\times 2$ invertible matrices with entries modulo a prime $q$, up to scalar multiples) and proved the optimal bound $\mu(\Gph)\le 2\sqrt{d-1}$ via Pierre Deligne's theorem on Srinivasa Ramanujan's conjecture in number theory---the reason optimal spectral expanders are named \emph{Ramanujan graphs}.

\paragraph{The zig-zag product.} Reingold, Vadhan, and Wigderson (2002) gave a combinatorial construction requiring no group theory or number theory. Given a large $D$-regular graph $\Gph$ on $N$ vertices and a small $d$-regular ``cloud'' graph $H$ on $D$ vertices, their \emph{zig-zag product} $\Gph\mathbin{\circledcirc} H$ (often written with a circled $\mathrm{z}$) builds a $d^2$-regular graph on $ND$ vertices whose spectral gap remains bounded away from zero whenever $\Gph$ and $H$ both expand (Figure~\ref{ch34:fig-zigzag}).

\begin{intuitionbox}
Replace every vertex $u$ of $\Gph$ with a $D$-vertex copy (``cloud'') of $H$, identifying each vertex $(u,i)$ of the cloud with the $i$-th edge incident to $u$ in $\Gph$. Each edge of $\Gph\mathbin{\circledcirc} H$ corresponds to a three-part walk \emph{zig--zag--zig} (Figure~\ref{ch34:fig-zigzag}): take one step inside $u$'s cloud along an edge of $H$ (\emph{zig}, $d$ choices), cross the unique $\Gph$-edge attached to the reached port to land in a neighboring cloud $v$ ($1$ deterministic step), and take one step inside $v$'s cloud along $H$ (\emph{zag}, $d$ choices). Each vertex has only $d\cdot 1\cdot d = d^2$ neighbors, yet the short $H$-steps spread weight across all $D$ outgoing $\Gph$-edges so the global expansion of $\Gph$ carries through. Alternating graph squaring (replacing a graph by $2$-step walks, which squares degree while squaring $\mu/d$ down) with the zig-zag product (which restores degree back to $d^2$) builds an infinite expander family from a single constant-size gadget $H$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\paragraph{Lossless bipartite expanders.} Error-correcting codes (Section~\ref{ch34:codes}) need \emph{vertex} expansion almost equal to the degree $d$: every small left subset $S\subseteq L$ must satisfy $\abs{N(S)}\ge(1-\eps)d\abs{S}$, so nearly all $d\abs{S}$ outgoing edges land on distinct right-hand vertices. Eigenvalue bounds alone cannot certify this: Kahale (1995) proved that even optimal Ramanujan eigenvalues $\mu\le 2\sqrt{d-1}$ guarantee vertex expansion at most roughly $d/2$, not $(1-\eps)d$. Capalbo, Reingold, Vadhan, and Wigderson (2002) bypassed this spectral barrier with a bipartite generalization of the zig-zag product that tracks entropy growth directly, giving explicit \emph{lossless expanders} for arbitrarily small $\eps>0$.

\begin{marginnotebox}
Explicit expanders developed in four milestones: Margulis gave the first explicit construction in 1973, Gabber and Galil proved explicit eigenvalue bounds in 1981, and Lubotzky, Phillips, and Sarnak (independently Margulis) reached the Ramanujan bound in 1988. The combinatorial zig-zag product (2002) led to Reingold's 2005 theorem that $\mathsf{SL}=\mathsf{L}$: undirected $s$--$t$ connectivity (which defines symmetric logspace $\mathsf{SL}$) can be solved in deterministic logarithmic space $\mathsf{L}$, using $\Oh(\log n)$ bits of memory and zero randomness. In 2013, Marcus, Spielman, and Srivastava proved that bipartite Ramanujan graphs exist for every degree by analyzing averages of characteristic polynomials whose real roots interlace.
\end{marginnotebox}

\section{Why expanders matter}
\label{ch34:applications}

Each view of expansion---eigenvalue decay, large cuts, uniform walk visits, and lossless neighbor fan-out---drives a distinct algorithmic application.

\subsection{Fast mixing of random walks}
\label{ch34:mixing}

Run a \emph{lazy random walk} on a connected $d$-regular graph $\Gph$: from the current vertex, stay put with probability $1/2$, and otherwise step to one of its $d$ neighbors chosen uniformly at random. Represent the walk's distribution after $t$ steps as a probability vector $p_t\in\R^n$, starting at vertex $v$ from $p_0=e_v$ (the standard basis vector with $1$ at coordinate $v$ and $0$ elsewhere). One step multiplies by the symmetric transition matrix
\[
  W \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{I} + \frac{1}{2d}\Adjm, \qquad p_{t+1} = Wp_t.
\]
Because $\mu_i\in[-d,d]$, every eigenvalue $w_i = \frac{1}{2}+\frac{\mu_i}{2d}$ of $W$ lies in $[0,1]$: the $\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{I}$ self-loop prevents negative eigenvalues near $-1$ that would make the walk oscillate on bipartite graphs. A \emph{stationary distribution} is a probability vector $\pi$ satisfying $W\pi=\pi$; because every row and column of $W$ sums to $1$, the uniform vector $\pi=\mathbf{1}/n$ is stationary (corresponding to $w_1=1$).

To measure how close $p_t$ is to $\pi$, we use the $\ell_1$ norm $\norm{x}_1=\sum_{u\in V}\abs{x_u}$ and the \emph{total-variation distance} $\frac{1}{2}\norm{p_t-\pi}_1 = \max_{A\subseteq V}\abs{p_t(A)-\pi(A)}$, the largest probability discrepancy on any vertex event $A\subseteq V$. How many steps $t$ does it take for $p_t$ to become nearly uniform?

\begin{theorem}[Mixing time of an expander]
\label{ch34:thm-mixing}
Let $w_\star=\max_{i\ge 2}\abs{w_i}$ be the largest walk eigenvalue orthogonal to $\mathbf{1}$. Since $w_i=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\mu_i}{2d}\in[0,1]$ for all $i$, the maximum occurs at $\mu_2$:
\begin{align*}
  w_\star &= \frac{1}{2}+\frac{\mu_2}{2d} \\
          &\le \frac{1}{2}+\frac{\mu}{2d} \;<\; 1 \qquad (\text{for any connected } \Gph).
\end{align*}
For every start vertex $v$ and step count $t\ge 0$, the distribution $p_t = W^t e_v$ satisfies
\[
  \norm{p_t-\pi}_1 \;\le\; \sqrt{n}\cdot w_\star^{\,t}.
\]
Consequently, $\frac{1}{2}\norm{p_t-\pi}_1 \le 1/n$ holds once
\[
  t \;=\; \Oh\!\left(\frac{1}{1-w_\star}\log n\right) \;=\; \Oh\!\left(\frac{d}{d-\mu_2}\log n\right).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Proof.} Because $W$ is real and symmetric, it has an orthonormal eigenbasis $\psi_1=\mathbf{1}/\sqrt{n},\psi_2,\dots,\psi_n$ with eigenvalues $1=w_1 > w_2 \ge \cdots \ge w_n \ge 0$. Expand $p_0 = e_v = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i\psi_i$; the $i=1$ term is $(e_v^\top \psi_1)\psi_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\frac{\mathbf{1}}{\sqrt{n}} = \pi$, and by orthonormality $\sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2 = \norm{e_v}_2^2 = 1$, so $\sum_{i\ge 2} c_i^2 = 1 - 1/n \le 1$. Applying $W^t$ leaves $\pi$ fixed and scales $\psi_i$ by $w_i^t$:
\begin{align*}
  \norm{p_t-\pi}_2^2 &= \sum_{i\ge 2} c_i^2 w_i^{2t} \\
                     &\le w_\star^{2t}\sum_{i\ge 2} c_i^2 \\
                     &\le w_\star^{2t}.
\end{align*}
Applying Cauchy--Schwarz to $x = p_t-\pi$ converts the Euclidean $\ell_2$ norm $\norm{x}_2=\sqrt{\sum_u x_u^2}$ to $\ell_1$ via $\norm{x}_1 = \sum_u 1\cdot\abs{x_u} \le \sqrt{n}\,\norm{x}_2$, giving $\norm{p_t-\pi}_1\le\sqrt{n}\,w_\star^{\,t}$. Using $w_\star = 1-(1-w_\star)\le e^{-(1-w_\star)}$, setting $\sqrt{n}\,e^{-(1-w_\star)t}\le 1/n$ requires $t \ge \frac{3}{2(1-w_\star)}\ln n = \frac{3d}{d-\mu_2}\ln n$. \hfill$\square$
\end{whybox}

\begin{intuitionbox}
On the cycle $C_n$, a random walk drifts only $\Th(\sqrt{t})$ steps from its start in $t$ steps, so reaching the opposite side requires $\Th(n^2)$ steps---matching its spectral gap $d-\mu_2 = \Th(1/n^2)$. On an expander, every set of size at most $n/2$ sends a constant fraction of its edges outward, so probability mass doubles its reach each step and mixes in $\Th(\log n)$ steps. For a graph of $10^6$ vertices, $\log n$ versus $n^2$ is around $20$ steps versus $10^{12}$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
Fast mixing powers \emph{Markov chain Monte Carlo} (MCMC, Chapters~35 and~36): to sample from a combinatorially huge set (such as all matchings, proper colorings, or weighted spin configurations in statistical physics), you design a local random walk whose stationary distribution is the target and prove that its state graph has a spectral gap. The same eigenvalue gap governs card-shuffling times and the convergence of \emph{PageRank} (web-page importance scores computed from random-surfer walks in Chapter~36).
\end{applicationbox}

\subsection{Fault-tolerant and routing networks}
\label{ch34:robust}

Large cuts make expanders difficult to disconnect and difficult to congest. Edge expansion $h(\Gph)\ge\eps$ means that isolating any $k$-vertex region ($k\le n/2$) requires deleting at least $\eps k$ edges, so random link failures or targeted link removals leave a giant connected component intact. Expanders also support efficient \emph{permutation routing} (simultaneously sending one packet from each vertex $i$ to a distinct destination $\sigma(i)$): paths can be chosen with $\Oh(\log n)$ \emph{dilation} (maximum path length) and low \emph{congestion} (maximum number of paths sharing any single edge), because random-walk paths spread traffic evenly across edges. Data-center fabrics, supercomputer interconnects, and peer-to-peer overlays use expander topologies to extract high bandwidth and resilience from a small fixed number of ports per switch.

\subsection{Derandomization}
\label{ch34:derand}

Suppose a randomized decision algorithm uses an $r$-bit random seed, errs with probability at most $1/3$, and you want error probability at most $2^{-\Omega(k)}$. Running $k$ independent trials and taking a majority vote achieves this, at the cost of $kr$ independent random bits. When random bits are scarce or when we want to enumerate all seeds deterministically, can we spend far fewer than $kr$ bits?

\begin{intuitionbox}
Replace independent draws with an \emph{expander walk} (Figure~\ref{ch34:fig-expander-walk}). View all $N=2^r$ possible $r$-bit seeds as the vertices of a strongly explicit $d$-regular expander ($d=\Oh(1)$). Spend $r$ random bits once to pick a uniformly random start $v_1\in V$, then take $k-1$ random-walk steps $v_2,\dots,v_k$, spending only $\log_2 d = \Oh(1)$ bits per step to pick a neighbor. Total randomness drops from $kr$ bits to $r + (k-1)\log_2 d = r + \Oh(k)$ bits, yet the visited vertices spread across $V$ nearly as well as independent samples.
\end{intuitionbox}

For independent coin flips, the classical \emph{Chernoff bound} states that the probability the empirical average deviates from its expectation by a constant margin decays exponentially in the number of trials $k$. A walk on a two-sided expander satisfies a matching exponential concentration bound.

\begin{theorem}[Ajtai--Koml\'os--Szemer\'edi, 1987; Gillman, 1998: expander Chernoff bound]
\label{ch34:thm-chernoff}
Let $\Gph=(V,E)$ be a $d$-regular graph with $\mu(\Gph)/d = \beta < 1$, and let $B\subseteq V$ be a ``bad'' seed set (inputs where the algorithm errs) with $\abs{B}/\abs{V}\le 1/3$. For a $k$-step random walk $v_1,\dots,v_k$ starting from a uniformly random $v_1\in V$,
\[
  \Pr\!\Bigl[\,\abs{\set{i\in\set{1,\dots,k} : v_i\in B}} \;\ge\; \frac{1}{2}k\,\Bigr] \;\le\; e^{-\Om((1-\beta)k)}.
\]
\end{theorem}

The number of visits to $B$ concentrates around its mean at a rate governed by the normalized spectral gap $1-\beta = (d-\mu)/d$. Taking the majority answer across the $k$ runs fails only when at least $k/2$ steps fall in $B$, giving error $2^{-\Om(k)}$ using $r+\Oh(k)$ random bits instead of $kr$.

\subsection{Error-correcting codes}
\label{ch34:codes}

Expanders also yield error-correcting codes that can be encoded and \emph{decoded} in $\Oh(n)$ time. Sipser and Spielman (1996) built \emph{expander codes} from a bipartite left expander $\Gph=(L\cup R,E)$ (called a \emph{Tanner graph} in coding theory; Figure~\ref{ch34:fig-code}): the $n$ left vertices represent transmitted bits $x_1,\dots,x_n\in\set{0,1}$, and the $m < n$ right vertices represent parity-check constraints, each requiring the bits in its neighborhood to sum to $0 \pmod{2}$ (an even number of $1$s; general Tanner codes allow a small local code at each right vertex). A \emph{codeword} is any $n$-bit assignment satisfying all $m$ constraints; because $m$ linear equations over $\set{0,1}$ leave at least $n-m$ free message bits, the code has \emph{rate} (message bits per transmitted bit) at least $(n-m)/n$.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why lossless expansion enables greedy linear-time decoding.} Suppose $\Gph$ is a $(\gamma n, (1-\eps)d)$ left vertex expander with $\eps < 1/4$, and at most $\gamma n/2$ bits are corrupted during transmission. Let $S\subseteq L$ be the current set of corrupted bits. As long as $0 < \abs{S} \le \gamma n$, at least $(1-\eps)d\abs{S}$ distinct parity checks lie in $N(S)$. Double-counting the $d\abs{S}$ edges leaving $S$ shows that more than $(1-2\eps)d\abs{S} > \frac{1}{2}d\abs{S}$ checks in $N(S)$ receive \emph{only one} edge from $S$: those unique-neighbor checks see a single flipped bit, so their parity is odd and they visibly report ``unsatisfied.'' By averaging over $u\in S$, at least one corrupted bit $u$ has more than $d/2$ unsatisfied neighbor checks (over half its incident checks). Flipping any bit whose unsatisfied checks outnumber its satisfied checks strictly reduces the total count of unsatisfied checks (starting below $d\abs{S}\le \frac{1}{2}\gamma d n$, so $S$ never exceeds size $\gamma n$ during decoding), guaranteeing termination at the true codeword in $\Oh(n)$ flips.
\end{whybox}

\begin{theorem}[Sipser--Spielman, informal]
\label{ch34:thm-expander-codes}
An expander code built from a $d$-left-regular $(\gamma n,\,(1-\eps)d)$ lossless bipartite expander with $\eps<1/4$ and $m < n$ right constraints has constant rate $\ge 1-m/n$, corrects a constant fraction of worst-case bit flips, and decodes via greedy bit-flipping in $\Oh(n)$ time.
\end{theorem}

\section{A fully worked example}
\label{ch34:worked}

We verify every definition and bound on one concrete graph: the \emph{Petersen graph} $P$ (Figure~\ref{ch34:fig-petersen}), which is $3$-regular on $10$ vertices.

\begin{intuitionbox}
$P$ consists of an outer $5$-cycle $0,1,2,3,4$, an inner pentagram $5,6,7,8,9$ (connecting $5$--$7$--$9$--$6$--$8$--$5$), and five spokes joining $i$ to $i+5$. It is \emph{vertex-transitive} (for any two vertices $u$ and $v$, a graph symmetry maps $u$ to $v$, so $P$ looks identical from every vertex) and has \emph{girth} $5$ (its shortest cycle has length $5$; it contains no triangles or $4$-cycles). Its adjacency eigenvalues are $3$ (multiplicity $1$), $1$ (multiplicity $5$), and $-2$ (multiplicity $4$), giving $\mu(P)=\max(\abs{1},\abs{-2})=2$, two-sided gap $d-\mu=1$, and Laplacian spectral gap $\lambda_2=d-\mu_2=3-1=2$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\textbf{Step 1: edge expansion by hand.} Because $P$ is $3$-regular, any set $S$ with $e(S)$ internal edges has $\abs{\partial S} = 3\abs{S} - 2e(S)$. Because $P$ has girth $5$, no subset of size $\abs{S}\le 4$ can contain a cycle; its induced subgraph is a forest with $e(S)\le \abs{S}-1$, forcing
\[
  \frac{\abs{\partial S}}{\abs{S}} \;\ge\; \frac{3\abs{S}-2(\abs{S}-1)}{\abs{S}} \;=\; 1 + \frac{2}{\abs{S}} \;\ge\; \frac{3}{2} \qquad (1\le\abs{S}\le 4).
\]
(For instance, $\abs{S}=1$ gives ratio $3/1=3$; an edge like $\set{0,5}$ gives $e(S)=1$ and ratio $4/2=2$.) For $\abs{S}=5$, girth $5$ allows at most one cycle (hence $e(S)\le 5$), so $\abs{\partial S}\ge 15-10=5$ and $\abs{\partial S}/\abs{S}\ge 1$. The outer pentagon $S=\set{0,1,2,3,4}$ attains $e(S)=5$ and cuts only the $5$ spokes (Figure~\ref{ch34:fig-petersen}, right), proving $h(P)=5/5=1$.

\textbf{Step 2: check Cheeger.} Theorem~\ref{ch34:thm-cheeger} brackets $h(P)$ between
\begin{align*}
  \frac{\lambda_2}{2} &= \frac{2}{2} = 1 \\
  &\le h(P) = 1 \\
  &\le \sqrt{2d\lambda_2} = \sqrt{2\cdot 3\cdot 2} = \sqrt{12} \approx 3.46.
\end{align*}
Both inequalities hold, and the lower bound is met with equality: $h(P)=\lambda_2/2=1$. The upper Cheeger bound has a factor of $\sqrt{12}\approx 3.46$ slack.

\textbf{Step 3: Corollary~\ref{ch34:cor-eml-cut}.} Plugging in the two-sided parameter $\mu=2$ gives $h(P)\ge(d-\mu)/2=(3-2)/2=1/2$. Because $\abs{\mu_n}=\abs{-2}=2$ is larger than $\mu_2=1$, replacing $\mu$ with the one-sided $\mu_2=1$ in $(d-\mu_2)/2=(3-1)/2=1$ recovers the exact value $h(P)=1$.

\textbf{Step 4: mixing.} The lazy-walk eigenvalues are $w_i=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\mu_i}{6}$, mapping $\mu_i=1$ to $w_i=\frac{2}{3}$ and $\mu_i=-2$ to $w_i=\frac{1}{6}$. Thus the largest nontrivial walk eigenvalue is
\[
  w_\star \;=\; \frac{1}{2}+\frac{\mu_2}{2d} \;=\; \frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{6} \;=\; \frac{2}{3},
\]
with walk spectral gap $1-w_\star = 1/3$ (laziness maps the negative $\mu_i=-2$ to the harmless $1/6$ rather than $-2/3$). Theorem~\ref{ch34:thm-mixing} bounds the $\ell_1$ distance by $\sqrt{10}\,(2/3)^t$: solving $\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{10}\,(2/3)^t \le 1/10$ gives $t \ge \frac{\ln(5\sqrt{10})}{\ln(3/2)} \approx 6.8$, so at most $7$ steps suffice to bring total-variation distance below $1/n=0.1$.

\begin{labbox}
Reproduce Step~4 numerically. Start at $p_0=e_0$ (all mass on vertex $0$). After one lazy step, $p_1$ puts $1/2$ on vertex $0$ and $1/6$ on each of its neighbors $1,4,5$. Iterate $p_{t+1}=Wp_t$ twelve times (a $10\times 10$ matrix--vector product) and check that $\frac{1}{2}\norm{p_t-\frac{1}{10}\mathbf{1}}_1$ shrinks by a factor near $2/3$ per step. Then sever expansion by deleting the five spokes, splitting $P$ into two disconnected $5$-cycles: now $\mu_2=d$, $w_\star=1$, and mass stays trapped forever on the outer cycle.
\end{labbox}

\textbf{Step 5: watch the error fall.} By symmetry, $p_t$ assigns identical probability to all vertices at the same shortest-path distance from vertex $0$, collapsing the $10$-vector into three numbers: the probability at vertex $0$ (distance $0$, $1$ vertex), at each neighbor (distance $1$, $3$ vertices), and at each remaining vertex (distance $2$, $6$ vertices). Table~\ref{ch34:tab-mixing} and Figure~\ref{ch34:fig-mixing-curve} track this exact $3$-state recurrence alongside Theorem~\ref{ch34:thm-mixing}'s upper bound and the much slower walk on the $10$-cycle $C_{10}$.

\section{Historical Context and Controversies}
\label{ch34:history-context}

\begin{historybox}
Expander graphs originated in telephone switching. In 1973, Mark Pinsker at the Institute for Information Transmission Problems in Moscow studied non-blocking switching networks: circuits connecting thousands of callers simultaneously without path collisions while using as few internal switches and wires as possible. Pinsker saw that such switches needed sparse bipartite graphs in which every small input set fanned out to a strictly larger output set. Using the probabilistic method (proving an object exists by showing a random draw succeeds with positive probability), Pinsker proved that a randomly chosen sparse bipartite graph is an expander with high probability.

Pinsker's proof was non-constructive: almost all sparse graphs expand, yet the proof gave no deterministic recipe to build even one for a physical exchange. Later in 1973, Gregory Margulis gave the first explicit constant-degree expanders, proving a positive spectral gap through group representation theory via Kazhdan's property~(T) (a rigidity property under which a group has no almost-invariant unit vectors orthogonal to constants). In 1988, Alexander Lubotzky, Ralph Phillips, and Peter Sarnak, and independently Margulis, constructed explicit \emph{Ramanujan graphs} matching the Alon--Boppana threshold $2\sqrt{d-1}$ by invoking Pierre Deligne's proof of the Ramanujan conjecture on automorphic forms (analytic functions on matrix groups with rich arithmetic symmetries).

For nearly three decades, explicit expanders posed a classic ``needle in a haystack'' reversal: almost the entire haystack consists of needles, yet exhibiting a single needle deterministically seemed to demand deep algebraic machinery. Dense graphs like $K_n$ expand trivially at an unaffordable $\Oh(n^2)$ edge cost; achieving $\Oh(1)$ degree with linear boundary expansion combinatorially remained open until 2002, when Omer Reingold, Salil Vadhan, and Avi Wigderson introduced the \emph{zig-zag product}, building constant-degree expanders from elementary graph operations and enabling Reingold's 2005 deterministic logspace connectivity algorithm ($\mathsf{SL} = \mathsf{L}$). Constructing strongly explicit non-bipartite Ramanujan graphs of every degree and vertex count via elementary combinatorial methods remains an active open problem.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{What We Can Now Do and Its Limitations}

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{What expanders provide}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Randomness reduction}: Rather than spending $kr$ independent random bits to run an $r$-bit randomized algorithm $k$ times, a $k$-step walk on a constant-degree expander on $N=2^r$ vertices uses $r + \Oh(k)$ random bits and achieves exponential error decay $2^{-\Om(k)}$.
  \item \textbf{Linear-time error-correcting codes}: Sipser--Spielman codes (1996) on lossless bipartite expanders encode and decode a constant fraction of worst-case bit errors in $\Oh(n)$ time.
  \item \textbf{Logarithmic-round diffusion}: Rumor-spreading protocols and random walks on bounded-degree expanders reach all $n$ vertices and mix in $\Oh(\log n)$ steps with high tolerance to edge deletions.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Limitations}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Restricted algebraic parameters}: Algebraic Ramanujan constructions exist natively only for specific degrees and vertex counts tied to prime powers (such as $d = p+1$ for primes $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$), requiring padding or graph products for general $n$ and $d$.
  \item \textbf{Global versus local shortest-path routing}: Expansion guarantees $\Oh(\log n)$ diameter and low-congestion paths, but on a general unstructured or algebraic expander, finding shortest paths locally using only a neighbor's address (without $\Oh(n)$-entry routing tables) is nontrivial.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Connections ahead}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Chapter~35 (Random Walks and Electrical Networks)} links the spectral gap directly to electrical currents, effective resistances, and commute times.
  \item \textbf{Chapter~36 (PageRank and Markov Chains)} uses the spectral gap of the Google matrix (the web-link walk matrix stabilized by random resets) to bound the convergence rate of power iteration.
  \item \textbf{Chapter~41 (Laplacian Solvers and Electrical Flows)} uses \emph{expander decompositions} (partitioning an arbitrary graph into high-conductance expander clusters with few edges between clusters) inside near-linear-time Laplacian system solvers.
\end{itemize}
\end{focusbox}

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch34:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Warm-up cuts]
For the $4$-dimensional hypercube $Q_4$ ($16$ vertices, $4$-regular), compute $\abs{\partial S}/\abs{S}$ when $S$ is the set of $8$ strings with first bit $0$, when $S$ is a single vertex, and when $S$ is a Hamming ball of radius $1$ (one vertex together with all $4$ neighbors differing from it in a single bit). Conjecture $h(Q_4)$ and argue why the half-cube cut is optimal.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The easy direction on non-regular graphs]
Prove $\lambda_2(\mathcal{L})\le 2\phi(\Gph)$ for any connected (not necessarily regular) graph using the \emph{normalized} Laplacian $\mathcal{L}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\Adjm\,\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}$ and conductance $\phi(\Gph)=\min_{0<\operatorname{vol}(S)\le\operatorname{vol}(V)/2}\abs{\partial S}/\operatorname{vol}(S)$ from Chapter~32. Identify where $d$-regularity was used in the proof of the lower bound of Theorem~\ref{ch34:thm-cheeger} and adapt the test vector accordingly.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Mixing Lemma tightness]
Show that the Petersen graph comes close to saturating Theorem~\ref{ch34:thm-eml}: find subsets $S,T\subseteq V$ where $\abs{e(S,T)-\tfrac{d}{n}\abs{S}\abs{T}}$ is a large fraction of $\mu\sqrt{\abs{S}\abs{T}}$. (Hint: inspect eigenvectors of $\mu_2=1$ or $\mu_n=-2$ to guide your choice of $S$ and $T$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Bipartite obstruction]
\label{ch34:ex-bipartite}
Prove that a connected $d$-regular graph is bipartite if and only if $\mu_n=-d$. Conclude that bipartite graphs are never two-sided spectral expanders, and explain algebraically how the $\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{I}$ term in the lazy walk $W=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{I}+\tfrac{1}{2d}\Adjm$ restores mixing.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Implement Cheeger rounding]
Implement Algorithm~\ref{ch34:alg-cheeger}. Test it on (a) the cycle $C_{1000}$, (b) a $\sqrt{n}\times\sqrt{n}$ grid, and (c) a random $3$-regular graph. Report the returned cut ratio alongside $\lambda_2/2$ and $\sqrt{2d\lambda_2}$, and verify that the quadratic gap between $\lambda_2/2$ and the cut ratio appears on $C_{1000}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Alon--Boppana at small $n$]
For $d=3$, compute $2\sqrt{d-1}=2\sqrt{2}\approx 2.83$ and verify why the Petersen graph's $\mu(P)=2<2.83$ does not contradict the Alon--Boppana bound. Explain why a finite graph of bounded diameter can beat $2\sqrt{d-1}$ while no infinite $d$-regular family can. (Hint: Alon--Boppana has a $-o(1)$ term that shrinks only as diameter and $n$ grow.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Expander Chernoff in code]
Empirically test Theorem~\ref{ch34:thm-chernoff}. On a random $4$-regular graph on $n=600$ vertices, mark a random subset $B$ of $200$ vertices ``bad''; simulate $10^4$ random walks of length $k$ from a uniform start for $k=10,20,40$; plot the empirical failure rate (the fraction of walks visiting $B$ at least $k/2$ times) against the independent-coin Chernoff curve, and compare their exponential decay slopes.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Break a small Tanner code]
Extend the bipartite Tanner graph of Figure~\ref{ch34:fig-code} to $7$ variable bits and $3$ parity checks (assigning the $7$ variables distinct nonzero $3$-bit columns indicating which checks they join). Run the greedy bit-flip decoder on all $1$-bit errors and on $2$-bit errors. Construct a $2$-bit error pattern where greedy flipping fails or stalls, and connect that failure to a subset $S$ violating the lossless condition $\abs{N(S)}\ge(1-\eps)d\abs{S}$ with $\eps<1/4$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Zig-zag bookkeeping]
For the zig-zag product $\Gph\mathbin{\circledcirc} H$ of a $D$-regular graph $\Gph$ on $N$ vertices with a $d$-regular graph $H$ on $D$ vertices, prove directly from the three-step definition that $\Gph\mathbin{\circledcirc} H$ has $ND$ vertices, is symmetric (undirected), and is $d^2$-regular. Explain in one paragraph why squaring the current graph before each zig-zag step is needed to keep the spectral gap bounded away from zero across infinitely many iterations.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Collapse the Petersen walk]
Justify the distance reduction in Table~\ref{ch34:tab-mixing}: because the Petersen graph is \emph{distance-transitive} (for any two vertex pairs $(u,v)$ and $(u',v')$ at the same shortest-path distance, a graph symmetry maps $u\mapsto u'$ and $v\mapsto v'$), the lazy walk from vertex $0$ assigns equal probability to all vertices at the same distance from $0$, reducing the $10$-state chain to an exact $3$-state chain on distances $\set{0,1,2}$. Write down that $3\times 3$ transition matrix, compute its three eigenvalues by hand, and verify that the two nontrivial eigenvalues are $\set{\tfrac{2}{3},\,\tfrac{1}{6}}$, matching $\tfrac{1}{2}+\mu_i/(2d)$ for $\mu_i\in\set{1,-2}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Effective resistance and expansion]
Using the electrical-network view of Chapter~35, argue that in a $d$-regular expander the effective resistance $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(u,v)$ between \emph{every} pair of distinct vertices $u,v$ is $\Th(1/d)$, whereas on the cycle $C_n$ antipodal vertices have $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(u,v)=\Th(n)$. Combine this with the commute-time identity $\operatorname{commute}(u,v)=2\abs{E}\,R_{\mathrm{eff}}(u,v)$ (where $\operatorname{commute}(u,v)$ is the expected number of walk steps to travel from $u$ to $v$ and back to $u$) to contrast worst-case commute times on expanders versus cycles.
\end{exercise}
