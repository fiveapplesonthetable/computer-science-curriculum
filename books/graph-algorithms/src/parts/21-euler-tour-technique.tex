\chapter{The Euler Tour Technique}

The previous chapters split trees at centroids and into heavy-light chains. Many tree tasks need less than that: they only inspect or update an entire subtree at once.

A tree branches in two dimensions, with subtrees nested within subtrees. An array is one-dimensional: a flat sequence of cells that supports slicing and interval queries. The Euler tour technique converts one into the other. It takes a rooted tree, walks around it in a fixed order, and writes down what it sees, producing an array in which \emph{every subtree occupies one contiguous block}. Once a subtree is a contiguous block, ``update everything below $v$'' and ``sum everything below $v$'' become ordinary array range operations that run in $\Oh(\log n)$ time on an $n$-vertex tree.

When the forest itself changes -- edges are added to join two trees or deleted to split one -- a flat array is too rigid, because inserting or removing a subtree can shift up to $n$ array entries. Storing the same tour in a balanced binary search tree (a binary tree kept to height $\Oh(\log n)$) instead of a flat array gives an \emph{Euler tour tree}, in which linking two trees, cutting an edge, and testing whether two vertices lie in the same tree each cost $\Oh(\log n)$ time.

\chapterline

\section{Flattening a tree}
\label{ch21:flatten}

Fix a rooted tree $T=(V,E)$ with $n=\abs{V}$ vertices, root $r$, and for each vertex
a fixed order on its children (any order will do; we commit to one). Run a depth-first search (DFS) from $r$. Two events occur at every vertex as the DFS proceeds: we \emph{enter}
a vertex when we first reach it, and we \emph{leave} it when we have finished all of
its descendants and are about to return to its parent.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Imagine walking around the outside of the tree with your left hand touching an
edge, the way you would trace a maze wall. You enter a vertex from above, explore
its leftmost subtree, return, explore the next subtree, and so on; only when
every subtree is exhausted do you climb back to the parent. If each undirected tree edge is viewed as a pair of opposite directed edges (one downward, one upward), this walk traverses every directed edge once -- an Eulerian circuit in the sense of Chapter~5, which is why it is called the \emph{Euler tour} of the tree. Recording only the first visit to each vertex gives the \emph{DFS preorder} (the order in which DFS discovers vertices). In both views, while you are anywhere inside $v$'s subtree, you
have entered $v$ but not yet left it: entry and exit \emph{bracket} the subtree like
matched parentheses.
\end{intuitionbox}

Two ways of recording this walk serve different tasks.

\subsection{The in/out timestamp encoding}

Maintain a global counter \texttt{timer}, starting at $1$. When DFS first enters a
vertex $v$, assign $\texttt{tin}[v]\leftarrow\texttt{timer}$ and increment the timer.
When DFS finishes $v$ (after all children are done), assign $\texttt{tout}[v]
\leftarrow\texttt{timer}$ and increment again. Every vertex thus receives two distinct
stamps, and the counter runs from $1$ to $2n$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{In/out timestamps via DFS}
\label{ch21:alg-tinout}
\DontPrintSemicolon
\SetKwProg{Fn}{function}{:}{}
\KwIn{rooted tree $T$, root $r$}
\KwOut{arrays $\texttt{tin},\texttt{tout}$}
$\texttt{timer}\leftarrow 1$\;
\Fn{\textsc{DFS}$(v,\,\text{parent }p)$}{
  $\texttt{tin}[v]\leftarrow \texttt{timer}$;\quad $\texttt{timer}\leftarrow \texttt{timer}+1$\;
  \ForEach{child $c$ of $v$ with $c\neq p$}{
    \textsc{DFS}$(c,v)$\;
  }
  $\texttt{tout}[v]\leftarrow \texttt{timer}$;\quad $\texttt{timer}\leftarrow \texttt{timer}+1$\;
}
\textsc{DFS}$(r,\,\textsc{nil})$\;
\end{algorithm}

Run it by hand on the example. The DFS visits $1,2,5,6,3,4,7,8$ on the way down and
unwinds as each subtree finishes. The counter steps once on every enter and once on every
leave, so the sequence of events in time order is
\[
1_{\text{in}},2_{\text{in}},5_{\text{in}},5_{\text{out}},6_{\text{in}},6_{\text{out}},
2_{\text{out}},3_{\text{in}},3_{\text{out}},4_{\text{in}},7_{\text{in}},8_{\text{in}},
8_{\text{out}},7_{\text{out}},4_{\text{out}},1_{\text{out}},
\]
which assigns the stamps $1,2,\dots,16$ in that order:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|cccccccc}
\toprule
vertex $v$ & $1$ & $2$ & $3$ & $4$ & $5$ & $6$ & $7$ & $8$\\
\midrule
$\texttt{tin}[v]$  & $1$  & $2$ & $8$  & $10$ & $3$ & $5$ & $11$ & $12$\\

\bottomrule

Every pair $(\texttt{tin}[v],\texttt{tout}[v])$ is a matched bracket, and the brackets
nest. Vertex $4$ opens at time $10$ and closes at $15$; inside that window sit $7$
($11$--$14$) and $8$ ($12$--$13$), the descendants of $4$. Writing the events as a
balanced-parenthesis string makes the nesting visible:
\[
\underbrace{(_1\,\underbrace{(_2\,(_5\,)_5\,(_6\,)_6\,)_2}_{\text{subtree }2}\,
\underbrace{(_3\,)_3}_{\text{subtree }3}\,
\underbrace{(_4\,\underbrace{(_7\,(_8\,)_8\,)_7}_{\text{subtree }7}\,)_4}_{\text{subtree }4}\,)_1}_{\text{subtree }1}.
\]

\begin{trapbox}
The most common slip is an off-by-one in the unwinding: writing
$\texttt{tout}[7]=14$ after forgetting that $8$ closed at $13$ \emph{before} $7$
closes, or reusing a stamp. Sanity check: the $2n$ stamps
must be a permutation of $\{1,2,\dots,2n\}$ with no repeats, and every vertex $v$ must satisfy
$\texttt{tin}[v]<\texttt{tout}[v]$. Both hold above.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{theorem}[Subtree--ancestor characterization]
\label{ch21:thm-ancestor}
For vertices $u,v$ of $T$, $u$ is an ancestor of $v$ (with $u=v$ allowed) if and only
if
\[
\texttt{tin}[u]\le \texttt{tin}[v]\quad\textnormal{and}\quad \texttt{tout}[v]\le \texttt{tout}[u].
\]
Equivalently, $\texttt{tin}[u]\le\texttt{tin}[v]\le\texttt{tout}[u]$, since exit stamps
exceed entry stamps and the intervals never cross.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The DFS enters $u$, recurses through $u$'s entire subtree, and then exits $u$. The time
interval $[\texttt{tin}[u],\texttt{tout}[u]]$ is the span during which the
DFS call stack contains $u$. A vertex $v$ lies in $u$'s subtree if and only if the DFS reaches $v$ while
$u$ is on the stack, meaning $v$'s enter/leave window lies inside $u$'s
window: $\texttt{tin}[u]\le\texttt{tin}[v]$ and $\texttt{tout}[v]\le\texttt{tout}[u]$.
For two vertices on different branches, one finishes before the other starts, so their
intervals are disjoint and never partially overlap. Thus the single chain of inequalities
$\texttt{tin}[u]\le\texttt{tin}[v]\le\texttt{tout}[u]$ suffices:
$\texttt{tin}[v]$ landing inside $u$'s window forces $\texttt{tout}[v]$
inside it too.
\end{proof}

\begin{whybox}
A DFS visits each subtree as a \emph{single uninterrupted run}: it never half-explores $v$'s subtree, wanders off to a
cousin, and comes back. That makes the enter/leave stamps nest like
parentheses, turning ``descendant of $v$'' into ``inside an
interval.''
\end{whybox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think: why can two subtree intervals never partially overlap?}
In depth-first search, if $u$ is visited before $v$, either $v$ is a descendant of $u$ (in which case $v$'s traversal finishes before $u$ exits, so $[\texttt{tin}[v], \texttt{tout}[v]] \subset [\texttt{tin}[u], \texttt{tout}[u]]$), or $v$ lies in a separate branch (in which case $u$ finishes before $v$ is entered, so $[\texttt{tin}[u], \texttt{tout}[u]]$ and $[\texttt{tin}[v], \texttt{tout}[v]]$ are disjoint). Partial overlap would mean the DFS left $u$'s subtree before finishing it, violating the last-in, first-out order of the DFS call stack.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{The single-array (entry-order) encoding}

The in/out encoding uses $2n$ slots. For \emph{subtree} problems a leaner encoding
suffices: record each vertex once, at its entry, and remember its subtree size. Let
$\texttt{ord}[v]\in\{1,\dots,n\}$ be the rank of $v$ in entry order (its position in
the DFS preorder), let $\texttt{node}[i]$ be the inverse table storing the vertex at position $i$, and let $\texttt{sz}[v]$ be the number of vertices in $v$'s
subtree.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Entry-order flattening with subtree sizes}
\label{ch21:alg-ord}
\DontPrintSemicolon
\SetKwProg{Fn}{function}{:}{}
\KwIn{rooted tree $T$, root $r$}
\KwOut{arrays $\texttt{ord},\texttt{node},\texttt{sz}$}
$\texttt{timer}\leftarrow 1$\;
\Fn{\textsc{DFS}$(v,p)$}{
  $\texttt{ord}[v]\leftarrow \texttt{timer}$;\quad $\texttt{node}[\texttt{timer}]\leftarrow v$;\quad $\texttt{timer}\leftarrow\texttt{timer}+1$\;
  $\texttt{sz}[v]\leftarrow 1$\;
  \ForEach{child $c\neq p$ of $v$}{
    \textsc{DFS}$(c,v)$;\quad $\texttt{sz}[v]\leftarrow \texttt{sz}[v]+\texttt{sz}[c]$\;
  }
}
\textsc{DFS}$(r,\textsc{nil})$\;
\end{algorithm}

On the running example, the DFS preorder is $1,2,5,6,3,4,7,8$, giving the entry ranks and subtree sizes below:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|cccccccc}
\toprule
vertex $v$ & $1$ & $2$ & $3$ & $4$ & $5$ & $6$ & $7$ & $8$\\
\midrule
$\texttt{ord}[v]$ & $1$ & $2$ & $5$ & $6$ & $3$ & $4$ & $7$ & $8$\\
$\texttt{sz}[v]$  & $8$ & $3$ & $1$ & $3$ & $1$ & $1$ & $2$ & $1$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{corollary}[Subtree as a contiguous range]
\label{ch21:cor-range}
In the entry-order array, the subtree of $v$ occupies the index range
\[
\bigl[\,\texttt{ord}[v],\ \texttt{ord}[v]+\texttt{sz}[v]-1\,\bigr].
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{ch21:thm-ancestor} the descendants of $v$ are the vertices
$u$ with $\texttt{tin}[v]\le\texttt{tin}[u]\le\texttt{tout}[v]$, and entry order is
the order of $\texttt{tin}$. Since the DFS visits the subtree in one uninterrupted run, those
descendants receive consecutive entry ranks; there are $\texttt{sz}[v]$ of them, starting with $v$ itself at rank $\texttt{ord}[v]$.
\end{proof}

For vertex $4$, the subtree occupies $[\texttt{ord}[4],\,
\texttt{ord}[4]+\texttt{sz}[4]-1]=[6,8]$, holding $\{4,7,8\}$, as shown in Figure~\ref{ch21:fig-entry-order}.

\begin{focusbox}
The two encodings serve different queries:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Entry-order} ($n$ cells): each vertex appears once. Use it for \emph{subtree}
aggregate queries and subtree updates, because every subtree is one range.
\item \textbf{In/out} ($2n$ cells, often storing $+a_v$ on enter and $-a_v$ on
leave): each vertex appears twice. Use it when \emph{path-to-root} sums must telescope,
or when entering must be distinguished from leaving (as in Euler tour trees).
\end{itemize}
Pick the encoding whose ranges match the queries you need to answer.
\end{focusbox}

\section{Subtree queries with a Fenwick tree}
\label{ch21:subtree-bit}

Suppose each vertex $v$ carries a value $a_v$, and we must support:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textsc{PointUpdate}$(v,\delta)$: add $\delta$ to $a_v$;
\item \textsc{SubtreeSum}$(v)$: return $\sum_{u\in\operatorname{subtree}(v)} a_u$.
\end{itemize}
Flatten once with Algorithm~\ref{ch21:alg-ord}, place value $a_v$ at array position
$\texttt{ord}[v]$, and lay a \emph{Fenwick tree} (also called a \emph{binary indexed tree}) $B[1\dots n]$ over the array (Figure~\ref{ch21:fig-fenwick-subtree}). A Fenwick tree maintains prefix sums $\textsc{prefix}(i) = \sum_{j=1}^{i} A[j]$ (with $\textsc{prefix}(0)=0$) in $\Oh(\log n)$ time: each cell $B[i]$ stores the sum of a block of length $\operatorname{lowbit}(i)$ ending at $i$, where $\operatorname{lowbit}(i) = i \mathbin{\&} (-i)$ is the lowest set bit of $i$ in binary. Calling $\textsc{prefix}(i)$ sums $B[i]$ while repeatedly clearing that lowest set bit ($i \leftarrow i - \operatorname{lowbit}(i)$) down to $0$, and $\textsc{add}(i,\delta)$ adds $\delta$ to every block covering $i$ by stepping $i \leftarrow i + \operatorname{lowbit}(i)$ up to $n$ (Chapter~23 develops range structures in full).

With $B$ in place, a point
update on $v$ updates index $\texttt{ord}[v]$. Writing $[\ell, r] = [\texttt{ord}[v],\,\texttt{ord}[v]+\texttt{sz}[v]-1]$ for the left and right endpoints of $v$'s subtree range, \textsc{SubtreeSum}$(v)$ returns
$\textsc{prefix}(r)-\textsc{prefix}(\ell-1)$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Subtree sum / point update over the Euler flattening}
\label{ch21:alg-subtreesum}
\DontPrintSemicolon
\SetKwProg{Fn}{function}{:}{}
\textbf{Preprocess:} run Algorithm~\ref{ch21:alg-ord}; build empty Fenwick tree $B$ of size $n$\;
\For{each vertex $v$}{ $B.\textsc{add}(\texttt{ord}[v],\,a_v)$ }
\BlankLine
\Fn{\textsc{PointUpdate}$(v,\delta)$}{
  $B.\textsc{add}(\texttt{ord}[v],\,\delta)$\;
}
\Fn{\textsc{SubtreeSum}$(v)$}{
  $\ell\leftarrow\texttt{ord}[v]$;\quad $r\leftarrow\texttt{ord}[v]+\texttt{sz}[v]-1$\;
  \Return $B.\textsc{prefix}(r)-B.\textsc{prefix}(\ell-1)$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{labbox}
Take the example tree with all $a_v=1$, and build the Fenwick tree over the eight entry
positions. Query \textsc{SubtreeSum}$(2)$: the range $[\ell, r] = [\texttt{ord}[2],\texttt{ord}[2]+
\texttt{sz}[2]-1]=[2,4]$ holds nodes $2,5,6$, and $B.\textsc{prefix}(4) - B.\textsc{prefix}(1) = B[4] - B[1] = 4 - 1 = 3$. on range $[5,5]$ reads $(B[5]+B[4]) - B[4] = B[5] = 1$, unaffected.
\end{labbox}

\needspace{6\baselineskip}
\begin{complexitybox}
Preprocess: one DFS in $\Oh(n)$ time, plus $n$ Fenwick inserts in $\Oh(n\log n)$ time (or $\Oh(n)$
with the linear-time Fenwick build). Each \textsc{PointUpdate} and \textsc{SubtreeSum} runs in
$\Oh(\log n)$ time; space is $\Oh(n)$. Swapping the Fenwick tree for a segment tree keeps the
same bounds and also supports range assignment or min/max with lazy propagation.
\end{complexitybox}

\subsection{Subtree updates with a range-update Fenwick or lazy segment tree}

The mirror problem -- \emph{add $\delta$ to every vertex of a subtree}, then query a
single vertex -- reduces to adding $\delta$ across an array interval $[\ell, r]$ and reading one index. A \emph{difference array} handles this with a single Fenwick tree: store consecutive differences $D[i] = A[i] - A[i-1]$ (taking $A[0]=0$), so that each prefix sum $\sum_{j=1}^{i} D[j]$ telescopes back to $A[i]$. Adding $\delta$ to every cell in $[\ell, r]$ changes only two boundary differences -- $D[\ell]$ increases by $+\delta$ and $D[r+1]$ decreases by $-\delta$ (when $r < n$) -- as illustrated in Figure~\ref{ch21:fig-diff-subtree}. With $\ell = \texttt{ord}[v]$ and $r = \texttt{ord}[v]+\texttt{sz}[v]-1$, the dictionary is:

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A frequent bug: indexing the Fenwick tree by vertex number $v$ instead of by
$\texttt{ord}[v]$. Then a subtree is \emph{not} a contiguous range and queries return wrong answers without warning. Always translate through $\texttt{ord}$, and remember that

different order moves every range.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Path-to-root queries with the in/out encoding}
\label{ch21:path}

Entry order groups subtrees; \emph{path-to-root} queries use the in/out
encoding for its telescoping cancellation. Suppose we must support
``add $\delta$ to $a_v$'' updates and answer ``what is the sum of values on the path
from the root down to $v$?'' Place each vertex twice in a length-$2n$ array: at
position $\texttt{tin}[v]$ write $+a_v$, and at position $\texttt{tout}[v]$ write
$-a_v$.

\begin{theorem}[Root-path sum telescopes]
\label{ch21:thm-pathsum}
With $+a_u$ at $\texttt{tin}[u]$ and $-a_u$ at $\texttt{tout}[u]$ for every vertex $u$, any vertex $v$ satisfies
\[
\textsc{prefix}\bigl(\texttt{tin}[v]\bigr)\;=\;\sum_{u\textnormal{ on root-to-}v\textnormal{ path}} a_u .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A vertex $u$ contributes $+a_u$ at $\texttt{tin}[u]$ and $-a_u$ at $\texttt{tout}[u]$.
Sum the array over indices $1, 2, \dots, \texttt{tin}[v]$. For each $u$, its net contribution
to that prefix is:
\begin{itemize}
\item $+a_u$ if $\texttt{tin}[u]\le\texttt{tin}[v]<\texttt{tout}[u]$, meaning $u$'s
window is open at time $\texttt{tin}[v]$ -- which by
Theorem~\ref{ch21:thm-ancestor} holds if and only if $u$ is an ancestor of $v$ (or $v$
itself);
\item $0$ if both stamps of $u$ are $\le\texttt{tin}[v]$ (window already closed, so $+a_u$ and $-a_u$ cancel) or
both are $>\texttt{tin}[v]$ (window not yet opened).
\end{itemize}
The vertices with open windows at time $\texttt{tin}[v]$ are those on the
DFS call stack, which is the root-to-$v$ path. Summing their $+a_u$ terms gives the claim.
\end{proof}

With a single Fenwick tree over the $2n$-length array, ``add $\delta$ to $a_v$''
becomes two point updates ($+\delta$ at $\texttt{tin}[v]$, $-\delta$ at
$\texttt{tout}[v]$), and a root-path query is one prefix sum. Reinterpreting those same two updates gives \emph{subtree-add, point-query} (adding $+\delta$ at $\texttt{tin}[u]$ and $-\delta$ at $\texttt{tout}[u]$ increases $\textsc{prefix}(\texttt{tin}[v])$ for every $v$ in $u$'s subtree; Exercise~\ref{ch21:ex-subtreeadd}), while swapping point updates and subtree range sums gives the transpose \emph{path-to-root-add, point-query} (adding $+\delta$ at $\texttt{tin}[v]$ increases the range sum over $[\texttt{tin}[u],\texttt{tout}[u]]$ whenever $u$ lies on the root-to-$v$ path).

To trace the cancellation on the running example, set $a_v=v$ and query
the path to vertex $8$. Lay $+a_v$ at $\texttt{tin}[v]$ and $-a_v$ at
$\texttt{tout}[v]$ across the $16$ positions, then sum the prefix up to
$\texttt{tin}[8]=12$ (Figure~\ref{ch21:fig-pathsum}). Every vertex whose window has
closed before time $12$ has both $+a_u$ and $-a_u$ inside the prefix and cancels to zero; the four survivors
are $1,4,7,8$, whose windows are still open at time $12$ -- the root-to-$8$
path -- giving prefix sum $1+4+7+8=20$.

\begin{connectionbox}
The path-to-root telescope is a simpler special case of what \textbf{heavy-light decomposition} (HLD, Chapter~19)
and \textbf{link-cut trees} (LCT, Chapter~22) do for arbitrary $u$--$v$ paths. The Euler tour handles

brackets, whereas a general $u$--$v$ path cannot be split into $\Oh(\log n)$ ranges on an Euler tour alone.
For arbitrary paths you split at the lowest common ancestor and use HLD's chain ranges
or LCT's dynamic path chains. Reach for the Euler tour when root paths or subtrees suffice,
and step up to HLD or LCT only when general path updates or dynamic path queries are required.
\end{connectionbox}

\subsection{Lowest common ancestor from the Euler tour}
\label{ch21:lca}

vertex \emph{every time the DFS touches it}, both on initial entry and on each return from a child (Figure~\ref{ch21:fig-lca}). This
walk has length $2n-1$ (each of the $n-1$ edges is crossed twice, plus the starting
vertex). Alongside it, record the depth (distance from the root) of each visited vertex.

Between the first touch of $u$ and the first touch of $v$, the walk must climb out of $u$'s subtree up to $\mathrm{LCA}(u,v)$ before descending toward $v$, and it cannot climb above $\mathrm{LCA}(u,v)$ until the entire subtree of $\mathrm{LCA}(u,v)$ is finished. Therefore $\mathrm{LCA}(u,v)$
is the shallowest vertex appearing between those two positions -- a \emph{range-minimum query} (RMQ) on the depth array. Precomputing a sparse table (introduced in Chapter~19: the minimum of every power-of-two block, so any query range is covered by two overlapping blocks) answers each LCA query in $\Oh(1)$ time after $\Oh(n\log n)$ preprocessing; exploiting the fact that adjacent depths in the walk differ by $+1$ or $-1$ ($\pm 1$ RMQ) reduces preprocessing to $\Oh(n)$. Chapter~23 develops both structures in full.

\begin{historybox}
The name recalls Leonhard Euler's 1736 paper on the bridges of K\"onigsberg, which characterized walks that traverse every edge once (Chapter~5). Robert Tarjan and Uzi Vishkin introduced the Euler tour technique for trees in 1985 to parallelize tree computations on the parallel random-access machine (PRAM, an idealized shared-memory parallel computer): flattening pointer trees into edge-tour arrays reduced subtree and ancestor tasks to parallel prefix sums and list ranking (computing each node's distance to the end of a linked list). In 1995, Monika Henzinger and Valerie King introduced \emph{Euler tour trees} (ETTs), storing tour sequences inside balanced search trees to maintain dynamic graph connectivity under edge insertions and deletions in $\Oh(\log^3 n)$ (later improved to $\Oh(\log^2 n)$) time per update. Designing pointer-free dynamic tour trees that use CPU caches well without tuning to a specific cache size (cache-oblivious layouts) while guaranteeing worst-case logarithmic bounds remains an active research topic.
\end{historybox}

\section{Euler tour trees: dynamic connectivity in a forest}
\label{ch21:ett}

Everything so far assumed a \emph{static} tree: flatten once, query forever. Now we
let the forest change. On a forest $F$ (a collection of trees with no shared vertices) on $n$ vertices, we want to support:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textsc{Link}$(u,v)$: add edge $uv$, merging two trees into one (assuming
$u$ and $v$ lie in different trees, so adding $uv$ creates no cycle);
\item \textsc{Cut}$(u,v)$: remove an existing edge $uv$, splitting one tree into two;
\item \textsc{Connected}$(u,v)$: test whether $u$ and $v$ lie in the same tree;
\item subtree or whole-tree aggregate queries, if values are attached to vertices or edges.
\end{itemize}
A flat array cannot support fast \textsc{Link} or \textsc{Cut}: splicing a subtree tour in or out shifts $\Th(n)$ array entries in the worst case. Instead, we store each tour in a \emph{balanced binary search tree} (BST) keyed
by tour position, turning array splices into $\Oh(\log n)$ tree splits and merges.

\subsection{The representation}

For each tree in the forest, pick an arbitrary root and record its Euler tour as a
sequence of vertex \emph{occurrences}: a vertex $v$ with $c$ children appears $c+1$ times in the tour
(once before its first child, once between consecutive children, and once after its last
child; a leaf has $c=0$ and appears once). A tree on $k$ vertices has $k-1$ child-to-parent returns plus $k$ initial entries, giving $2k-1$ occurrences in total.

Store this occurrence sequence in a height-balanced BST -- such as a randomized \emph{treap} (which maintains BST order alongside random heap priorities), a self-adjusting \emph{splay tree}, or a \emph{red-black tree} -- \emph{keyed implicitly by position}. Rather than sorting nodes by vertex ID, each BST node stores the size of its BST subtree; an \emph{in-order traversal} (visiting the left child's subtree, then the node itself, then the right child's subtree) reads the Euler tour from left to right, and subtree sizes let us split the sequence at any position into two BSTs (\textsc{split}) or concatenate two BSTs (\textsc{merge}) in $\Oh(\log n)$ time. Each vertex $v$ also stores a pointer $\operatorname{occ}(v)$ to one of its occurrence nodes in the BST (say, its first). Figure~\ref{ch21:fig-ett-bst} shows a three-vertex tree, its five-occurrence tour, and a balanced BST storing that tour.

\begin{whybox}
Two vertices lie in the same tree \emph{if and only if their occurrence nodes belong to the same
BST}. To test connectivity, follow parent pointers from $\operatorname{occ}(u)$ and $\operatorname{occ}(v)$ up to their BST roots and check whether the two roots are the same node in $\Oh(\log n)$ time. Connectivity in the forest reduces to checking whether two nodes sit in the same sequence container.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{Link and Cut as sequence surgery}

Structural updates cut and paste the occurrence sequence. Writing $\langle x\rangle$ for a one-element sequence and $A \mathbin{\Vert} B$ for sequence $A$ followed by sequence $B$, we use a
\emph{reroot-then-splice} formulation that keeps the bookkeeping uniform (Figure~\ref{ch21:fig-reroot-link}).

\textbf{Rerooting.} To reroot a tree's tour at vertex $x$: if $x$ is already the root,
nothing changes; otherwise the tour begins and ends with the old root $r$. Split the
sequence immediately before the first occurrence of $x$ into $A\,\Vert\,B$, drop the trailing
closing occurrence of $r$ at the end of $B$ (since $A$ already opens with $r$), and
concatenate $\textsc{merge}(B', A, \langle x\rangle)$ so the new tour begins and ends
at $x$ with the same $2k-1$ length. This uses a constant number of \textsc{split} and \textsc{merge} calls, taking $\Oh(\log n)$ time.

\textbf{Link$(u,v)$.} Suppose $u$'s tree has $k_1$ vertices and $v$'s tree has $k_2$ vertices. Reroot $u$'s tree at $u$ and $v$'s tree at $v$. Now $\operatorname{tour}(u)$
begins and ends at $u$, and $\operatorname{tour}(v)$ begins and ends at $v$ (even when $v$
is a single-vertex tree $\langle v\rangle$). \textsc{Merge} the three pieces $\operatorname{tour}(u)$,
$\operatorname{tour}(v)$, and $\langle u\rangle$ in that order: after $u$'s tour finishes at $u$, the walk steps
down $u\!\to\!v$ into $\operatorname{tour}(v)$, finishes $\operatorname{tour}(v)$ at $v$, and steps back
up $v\!\to\!u$ to the closing occurrence $\langle u\rangle$. The merged tour has length
\[
(2k_1-1) + (2k_2-1) + 1 \;=\; 2(k_1+k_2) - 1,
\]
and the three-way merge takes $\Oh(\log n)$ time.

\textbf{Cut$(u,v)$.} Suppose $u$ is $v$'s parent in the current rooting (if $v$ is $u$'s
parent, swap their roles), and let the two resulting components on $u$'s and $v$'s sides have $k_1$ and $k_2$ vertices. In the occurrence tour, $v$'s subtree occupies the contiguous
block $B_v$ from the first occurrence of $v$ to the last occurrence of $v$, immediately
preceded by an occurrence of $u$ (before descending $u\!\to\!v$) and immediately followed
by a second occurrence of $u$ (upon returning $v\!\to\!u$). Splitting out $B_v$ yields
$S = A\,\Vert\,B_v\,\Vert\,C$, where $B_v$ is already $v$'s $(2k_2-1)$-occurrence
tour, while $A$ ends with $u$ and $C$ begins with $u$. Dropping one of those two adjacent
boundary occurrences of $u$ (the first element of $C$) to obtain $C'$ and calling $\textsc{merge}(A,C')$
restores $u$'s $(2k_1-1)$-occurrence tour in $\Oh(\log n)$ time.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Euler tour tree operations (over an implicit balanced BST)}
\label{ch21:alg-ett}
\DontPrintSemicolon
\SetKwProg{Fn}{function}{:}{}
\Fn{\textsc{Reroot}$(x)$}{
  $S\leftarrow$ sequence (BST) containing $x$'s occurrences\;
  \lIf{$x$ is already the first element of $S$}{\Return $S$}
  split $S$ immediately before the first occurrence of $x$ into $A\,\Vert\,B$\;
  drop the last element of $B$ (the old root's closing visit) to get $B'$\;
  \Return $\textsc{merge}(B',A,\langle x\rangle)$\;
}
\Fn{\textsc{Link}$(u,v)$}{
  \textsc{Reroot}$(u)$;\quad \textsc{Reroot}$(v)$\;
  \Return $\textsc{merge}\bigl(\operatorname{tour}(u),\,\operatorname{tour}(v),\,\langle u\rangle\bigr)$\;
}
\Fn{\textsc{Cut}$(u,v)$}{
  \tcp{assume $u$ is $v$'s parent in the tour (else swap $u\leftrightarrow v$)}
  split $S = A\,\Vert\,B_v\,\Vert\,C$ where $B_v$ spans from $\mathrm{first}(v)$ through $\mathrm{last}(v)$\;
  $\operatorname{tree}(v)\leftarrow B_v$\;
  drop the leading duplicate $u$ from $C$ to get $C'$;\quad $\operatorname{tree}(u)\leftarrow \textsc{merge}(A,C')$\;
}
\Fn{\textsc{Connected}$(u,v)$}{
  \Return $\textsc{root}(\operatorname{occ}(u)) = \textsc{root}(\operatorname{occ}(v))$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{whybox}
Why does \textsc{Cut}$(u,v)$ detach a single contiguous block? By the same
bracketing property that powered static subtree ranges. The descent into $v$ opens
$v$'s window and the return closes it; every visit in between is, by
Theorem~\ref{ch21:thm-ancestor}, a descendant of $v$. Removing edge $uv$ detaches
that subtree, which occupies one contiguous slice of the tour. Static range queries
and dynamic tour splicing rest on the same subtree-contiguity theorem.
\end{whybox}

$\langle u,x,u,v,w,v,u,y,u\rangle$. The two crossings of edge $uv$ -- the descent

\subsection{Correctness and the connectivity invariant}

\begin{theorem}[ETT maintains the forest]
\label{ch21:thm-ett}
Maintain, for each tree of the forest, one balanced BST holding its Euler tour
occurrences in tour order. After any sequence of \textsc{Link} and \textsc{Cut}
operations performed as in Algorithm~\ref{ch21:alg-ett}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the BSTs are in bijection with the trees of the current forest, and
\item the in-order sequence of each BST is a valid Euler tour of its tree.
\end{enumerate}
Consequently \textsc{Connected}$(u,v)$ is correct.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Both claims hold initially (single-vertex trees, each with a one-element tour). We check that each operation
preserves the invariant.

\emph{Link.} Rerooting cyclically rotates the closed walk around the tree and restores the new root's closing visit, yielding another valid Euler tour of the same tree.
After rerooting $u$'s tree at $u$ and $v$'s tree at $v$, merging $\operatorname{tour}(u)\mathbin{\Vert}\operatorname{tour}(v)\mathbin{\Vert}\langle u\rangle$ appends the descent $u\!\to\!v$, $v$'s full tour, and the return $v\!\to\!u$ at the
end of $u$'s tour. That is the Euler tour of the tree formed by attaching
$v$ as the last child of $u$ via edge $uv$: DFS from $u$ visits $u$'s existing children as before, descends into $v$, traverses $v$'s subtree, and returns to $u$. The merged BST's in-order sequence is therefore a valid tour of
the merged tree, with one BST representing that single merged tree.

\emph{Cut.} Because $uv$ is a tree edge, it is crossed once downward ($u\!\to\!v$) and once upward ($v\!\to\!u$). Those two crossings bracket $B_v$, which is $v$'s complete subtree tour. Splitting $B_v$ out gives a valid Euler tour for $v$'s component, and dropping one of the two surrounding occurrences of $u$ before merging $A$ and $C'$ removes the detour into $v$ and leaves a valid Euler tour for $u$'s component. This leaves two BSTs, one for each resulting tree.

Both invariants hold after every step. Since each tree owns one BST,
$u$ and $v$ lie in the same tree if and only if their occurrence nodes share a BST root, which is what
\textsc{Connected} tests.
\end{proof}

\begin{complexitybox}
With a balanced BST (treap, splay tree, or red-black tree) supporting \textsc{split} and
\textsc{merge} in $\Oh(\log n)$ time, every ETT operation -- \textsc{Link}, \textsc{Cut},
\textsc{Connected}, \textsc{Reroot} -- runs in $\Oh(\log n)$ time (worst-case for red-black trees, expected for treaps, and \emph{amortized} for splay trees, in the sense of Chapter~1).
Each tree on $k$ vertices stores $2k-1$ occurrences, so total space across the forest is $\Oh(n)$. Parent pointers on BST nodes let \textsc{Connected} climb from $\operatorname{occ}(v)$ to the BST root in $\Oh(\log n)$ steps.
\end{complexitybox}

\subsection{Aggregates over a dynamic subtree}

Each BST node can also store an aggregate (such as a sum, maximum, or count of marked vertices) over its BST subtree, recomputed in $\Oh(1)$ time from its two children during \textsc{split} and \textsc{merge}. Because a vertex $w$ with $c$ children appears $c+1$ times in the occurrence tour, vertex values are stored at one designated occurrence of $w$ (with $0$ or the identity element at its other occurrences) so each vertex is counted once. Reading the root of $v$'s BST then returns the aggregate over $v$'s entire connected tree in $\Oh(1)$ time after finding the root. To aggregate only the subtree on $v$'s side of a tree edge $uv$, split out $v$'s bracketed block $[\mathrm{first}(v),\mathrm{last}(v)]$ when rooted on $u$'s side (or temporarily \textsc{Cut}$(u,v)$, read the root aggregate of $v$'s BST, and \textsc{Link}$(u,v)$ back) in $\Oh(\log n)$ time. Henzinger--King and Holm--de Lichtenberg--Thorup use this augmentation to count \emph{incident non-tree edges} (graph edges outside the spanning forest with an endpoint in the subtree), a core subroutine in dynamic connectivity for general graphs.

\begin{connectionbox}
Euler tour trees solve dynamic connectivity for \emph{forests}. General-graph dynamic
connectivity (Chapter~39) builds directly on top: keep a
spanning forest in an ETT, and when a tree edge is cut, search a logarithmic hierarchy
of non-tree edges for a replacement edge reconnecting the two sides. The subtree-aggregate augmentation above makes finding non-tree edges incident to the smaller side an $\Oh(\log n)$-per-edge step, yielding the $\Oh(\log^2 n)$ amortized update of Holm--de Lichtenberg--Thorup.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{trapbox}
ETT maintains \emph{connectivity and component/subtree aggregates}, not \emph{depths or arbitrary paths}. Because \textsc{Link} reroots trees freely, parent--child orientation changes whenever edges are added, so ETT cannot directly answer ``what is the sum on the path from $u$ to $v$?'' or ``what is $\mathrm{LCA}(u,v)$?'' under updates. Use ETT for connectivity and subtree aggregates; use link-cut trees (Chapter~22) when dynamic path aggregates or rooted depths are required.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Putting it together: a design checklist}
\label{ch21:checklist}

When a problem is phrased on a rooted tree, ask three questions in order:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Static or dynamic shape?} If edges never change, flatten once into an array
(Section~\ref{ch21:flatten}). If edges are inserted and deleted by link and cut, use an Euler tour
tree (Section~\ref{ch21:ett}) or a link-cut tree (Chapter~22).
\item \textbf{Subtree or path queries?} For subtree aggregates, use entry-order
flattening with a range structure (Section~\ref{ch21:subtree-bit}). For root-path sums with point updates,
use the in/out $\pm$ encoding (Section~\ref{ch21:path}). For general $u$--$v$ path queries or non-invertible operations like $\max$,
split at the LCA and use heavy-light decomposition or link-cut trees.
\item \textbf{Which array structure?} Point updates and prefix/range sums fit a Fenwick tree. Range assignments, $\min/\max$ updates, or lazy range updates call for a segment tree. When the array is \emph{immutable} (never modified after build) and the operation is \emph{idempotent} (combining a value with itself leaves it unchanged, $x \oplus x = x$, as in $\min$, $\max$, or $\gcd$), a sparse table answers queries in $\Oh(1)$ time.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{applicationbox}
Static Euler tour flattening drives subtree-sum and subtree-add queries, marking modified subtrees for recomputation in incremental compilers and 3D scene trees, summing metrics across organizational or directory hierarchies with a single prefix-sum array, and static $\Oh(1)$ LCA queries through depth-RMQ. Its dynamic counterpart,
the Euler tour tree, maintains the spanning-forest layer of dynamic graph
connectivity in network-reliability analyses and graph databases.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{complexitybox}
\textbf{Static flattening.} Build $\Oh(n)$; subtree point-update/sum $\Oh(\log n)$ with a
Fenwick tree; root-path update/query $\Oh(\log n)$ with the $\pm$ encoding; space $\Oh(n)$.
\textbf{Euler tour trees.} \textsc{Link}, \textsc{Cut}, \textsc{Connected}, and dynamic subtree
aggregates each $\Oh(\log n)$; space $\Oh(n)$.
\end{complexitybox}

\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{Where We Go Next (Link-Cut Trees):} Euler tour trees turn dynamic trees into dynamic tour sequences, handling \textsc{Link}, \textsc{Cut}, connectivity, and subtree sums in $\Oh(\log n)$ time. When a changing forest must instead answer \emph{path} queries or apply updates along the simple path between two arbitrary vertices $u$ and $v$, tour order no longer groups the target vertices into one slice. Chapter~22 introduces Sleator and Tarjan's \emph{link-cut trees}, which decompose dynamic trees into path chains stored in splay trees to support both edge updates and arbitrary path operations in $\Oh(\log n)$ amortized time.
\end{connectionbox}

Using the $\pm a$ in/out encoding (Theorem~\ref{ch21:thm-pathsum}), implement
\emph{subtree-add, single-vertex-read}: adding $\delta$ to all of $\operatorname{subtree}(v)$, then
reading $a_w$ for a single $w$. Which positions get $\pm\delta$, and why does the point
read become a prefix sum up to $\texttt{tin}[w]$? Prove correctness.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Show that the Euler tour technique \emph{cannot} reduce a general $u$--$v$ path sum to
$\Oh(1)$ ranges in entry order, by exhibiting a tree and a path whose vertices land at
$\Th(n)$ disjoint, non-adjacent entry positions. (This is the gap that heavy-light
decomposition and link-cut trees close.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Augment the static flattening to answer \textsc{SubtreeMax}$(v)$ and
\textsc{PointAssign}$(v,x)$ in $\Oh(\log n)$. Explain why a plain Fenwick tree does not
suffice for max under assignment and what structure you use instead.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Implement \textsc{Reroot}$(x)$ for an Euler tour tree over an implicit treap, and prove
that the resulting in-order sequence is a valid Euler tour rooted at $x$. Count the
exact number of \textsc{split} and \textsc{merge} calls.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
In an Euler tour tree, augment each occurrence node with a subtree sum of vertex
values (each vertex's value attributed to one designated occurrence of that vertex). Given a designated root $r$ of $v$'s component, describe how
to answer ``sum over the subtree of $v$ relative to $r$'' in $\Oh(\log n)$ time under \textsc{Link} and
\textsc{Cut}, and prove that rerooting does not corrupt the per-vertex single-attribution
bookkeeping.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Give a sequence of \textsc{Link}, \textsc{Cut}, and \textsc{Connected} operations on five
vertices that produces a forest with two trees, one of which is a path of length $3$ ($3$ edges on $4$ vertices).
Trace the occurrence-sequence BST after each operation, and at the end verify
\textsc{Connected} answers for all $\binom{5}{2}$ vertex pairs.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
(Counting.) Prove that the Euler tour of a rooted tree on $k$ vertices used by ETT has
$2k-1$ occurrences when each vertex with $c$ children contributes $c+1$ occurrences (equivalently, a non-root vertex of degree $d$ contributes $d$ occurrences and the root contributes $d+1$). Then
show that the directed-edge formulation (recording each of the $k-1$ edges in both directions)
has length $2(k-1)$, and reconcile the two counts.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
(Break it.) A student implements Algorithm~\ref{ch21:alg-subtreesum} with the same range formula $[\texttt{ord}[v],\,\texttt{ord}[v]+\texttt{sz}[v]-1]$, but sets $\texttt{ord}[v]$ to the DFS
\emph{post}order rank (finish order, numbering $v$ after all of its children) instead of the \emph{pre}order rank. For which trees does
\textsc{SubtreeSum} still return the right answer on every vertex, and for which does it fail? Give a
concrete failing tree with the wrong numerical answer, and state how the range endpoints would need to change to work with postorder.
\end{exercise}
