\chapter{Dynamic Graph Algorithms}
\label{ch:dynamic}

In the preceding chapters, every algorithm operated on a fixed static graph: whether finding
shortest paths, maximum flow, or approximation guarantees, the graph held still while computation ran.
Real graphs rarely hold still. A social network gains and loses friendships; a road map closes a
bridge for repair; a circuit simulator toggles wires; a game search adds and removes pieces on
a board encoded as a graph. Recomputing from scratch after each change works for a single static query,
but fails on a long stream of changes: on a graph with $n$ vertices and $m$ edges, an $\Oh(m)$ rebuild
after each of $q$ updates costs $\Oh(qm)$ time, which is quadratic when $q\approx m$.

\emph{Dynamic graph algorithms} maintain a graph property (connectivity, a spanning forest, the
minimum spanning tree (MST) weight, component counts, reachability, or shortest-path distances) as
edges are inserted, deleted, or reweighted. The goal is to answer each \textbf{update} and each
\textbf{query} in \emph{polylogarithmic} time---$\Oh(\log^c n)$ for a small constant $c$ (often
shortened to \emph{polylog})---by storing auxiliary structure so that a local edit touches only a
small part of it.

On undirected graphs, keeping a spanning forest and organizing the remaining edges carefully lets us
handle both insertions and deletions in polylogarithmic time. On directed graphs, by contrast, even
testing whether one vertex can reach another resists fast updates, and a simple matrix--vector
multiplication reduction explains why many dynamic problems hit a wall near $\sqrt{n}$ or $n$ time per
operation.

\section{A taxonomy of ``dynamic''}
\label{ch39:taxonomy}

\begin{focusbox}
Three update models, in increasing generality.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Incremental}: edges are only \emph{inserted} (the graph grows).
  \item \textbf{Decremental}: edges are only \emph{deleted} (the graph shrinks).
  \item \textbf{Fully dynamic}: both insertions and deletions, interleaved
        arbitrarily.
\end{itemize}
Two cost models, with different promises.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Amortized}: the bound holds \emph{on average} over a sequence of
        operations; an individual operation may be slow as long as the running
        total is controlled.
  \item \textbf{Worst-case}: the bound holds for \emph{every single} operation,
        no exceptions.
\end{itemize}
\end{focusbox}

Incremental connectivity is fast: a union-find (disjoint-set) forest answers
``are $u$ and $v$ connected?'' under edge insertions in $\Oh(\alpha(n))$ amortized
time per operation, where $\alpha(n)$ is the inverse Ackermann function (which grows
so slowly that $\alpha(n)\le 4$ for every practical $n$; Chapter~6). Deletions are
harder because union-find has no un-union: once two components merge, splitting them
apart on an edge deletion is an operation the structure cannot perform. Fully dynamic
connectivity is hardest of all, and most of the work in this chapter goes into handling
deletions.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Why are deletions so much harder than insertions? An insertion can only
\emph{merge} information---two components become one, or the MST weight drops---so
components only grow and earlier merges never split apart. A deletion can
\emph{shatter} information: cutting one tree edge may or may not disconnect the
graph, depending on whether some \emph{other} edge, currently sitting unused,
reconnects the two halves. Answering that requires searching for a replacement
edge without scanning the whole graph.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{marginnotebox}
Dynamic connectivity bounds progressed from $\Oh(\sqrt{m})$ via hierarchical tree clustering (Frederickson, 1985) and $\Oh(\sqrt{n})$ via edge sparsification (1992), to amortized polylogarithmic bounds via Henzinger and King (1995) and Holm, de~Lichtenberg, and Thorup (1998), and randomized worst-case polylogarithmic bounds via Kapron, King, and Mountjoy (2013).
\end{marginnotebox}

\section{Warm-up: incremental connectivity and the limits of union-find}
\label{ch39:incremental}

To maintain connected components under insertions only, union-find represents each
component as a rooted tree. Every vertex $x$ stores a parent pointer $\mathrm{par}[x]$
(initially $x$ itself) and an integer $\mathrm{rank}[x]$ (an upper bound on the height
of $x$'s subtree); the tree root identifies the component.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Incremental connectivity via union-find}
\label{ch39:alg-unionfind}
\SetKwFunction{Find}{Find}\SetKwFunction{Union}{Union}\SetKwFunction{Conn}{Connected}
\SetKwProg{Fn}{Function}{:}{}
\Fn{\Union{$u,v$}}{
  $r_u \gets \Find{u}$; \quad $r_v \gets \Find{v}$\;
  \lIf{$r_u = r_v$}{\Return}
  attach the smaller-rank root under the larger (union by rank)\;
}
\Fn{\Conn{$u,v$}}{
  \Return $\Find{u} = \Find{v}$\;
}
\Fn{\Find{$x$}}{
  \If{$\mathrm{par}[x]\neq x$}{
    $\mathrm{par}[x]\gets\Find{$\mathrm{par}[x]$}$ \tcp*[r]{path compression}
  }
  \Return $\mathrm{par}[x]$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

With union by rank and path compression, any sequence of $m$ operations on $n$ vertices
runs in $\Oh(m\,\alpha(n))$ total time, or $\Oh(\alpha(n))$ amortized time per operation.

\begin{trapbox}
A common temptation is to ``support deletion'' by storing each merge on a stack and
undoing the last union. That works only for a stack-like \emph{rollback} pattern:
disable path compression, keep union by rank so tree height stays $\Oh(\log n)$, and
undo merges strictly in last-in, first-out (LIFO) order in $\Oh(\log n)$ time per
operation. It does \emph{not} support deleting an \emph{arbitrary} earlier edge: the
component that edge helped form may now be held together by later edges, and severing
an old parent pointer corrupts the tree. Rollback union-find solves the \emph{offline}
problem (where the entire sequence of updates and queries is known before answering
anything), not the \emph{online} problem (where each query must be answered before the
next update arrives).
\end{trapbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Rollback union-find plus a segment tree over the timeline solves \emph{offline}
fully dynamic connectivity (Figure~\ref{fig:ch39-offline-segtree}). Suppose there are
$q$ time steps and each edge $e$ is present during a known time interval
$[t_{\text{in}}, t_{\text{out}})$. Build a segment tree over $[0,q)$ and place each
edge on the $\Oh(\log q)$ \emph{canonical nodes} of its interval---the $\Oh(\log q)$
disjoint tree segments whose union equals $[t_{\text{in}}, t_{\text{out}})$
(Chapter~23). Traverse the segment tree depth-first, calling \textsc{Union} for a
node's edges on entry and rolling those unions back on exit; at each leaf $t$, the
union-find holds every edge alive at time $t$ and answers that step's query. The total
time is $\Oh((m+q)\log q\,\log n)$. The rest of this chapter solves the online problem.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Euler-tour trees: connectivity in a forest}
\label{ch39:ett}

Before handling general graphs, we need dynamic connectivity on a \emph{forest}:
support \textsc{Link} (add an edge between two trees), \textsc{Cut} (delete a tree
edge), and connectivity queries where no replacement edge ever exists because a forest
has no cycles. Link-cut trees (which decompose a tree into vertex-disjoint paths stored
in splay trees; Chapter~22) already support \textsc{Link} and \textsc{Cut}, but for
connectivity we want a structure that also supports \emph{subtree} aggregates and
``pick any non-tree edge incident to this tree'': the \emph{Euler-tour tree} (ETT) of
Henzinger and King, and Tarjan.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Recall the Euler tour of a tree (Chapter~21): walk around the tree depth-first,
traversing each of its $k-1$ edges once in each direction. Forget the tree shape and
keep only this linear \emph{sequence}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item ``Are $u$ and $v$ in the same tree?'' $\Leftrightarrow$ ``do their
        tour entries lie in the same sequence?''
  \item \textsc{Link} and \textsc{Cut} become \emph{splits and concatenations} of
        sequences.
\end{itemize}
Store each sequence in a balanced binary search tree (BST) keyed implicitly by
position: rather than sorting by vertex name, each BST node stores the size of its
subtree so an in-order traversal reads out the tour from left to right. Using subtree
sizes, splitting a sequence at any node or concatenating two sequences takes
$\Oh(\log n)$ time (worst-case in a randomized treap or red-black tree, amortized in a
self-adjusting splay tree; Chapters~21--22).
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection*{The Euler tour as a sequence}

Split each undirected tree edge $\{u,v\}$ into its two opposite directed arcs (often
called \emph{half-edges}), $\langle u,v\rangle$ and $\langle v,u\rangle$, and give
every vertex $v$ a single self-loop entry $\langle v,v\rangle$ recorded the first time
the tour visits $v$ (an isolated vertex has the 1-item tour $\langle v,v\rangle$). As
Figure~\ref{ch39:fig-ett} shows, a $k$-vertex tree has $k$ self-loops and $2(k-1)$
directed edge entries, or $3k-2$ BST nodes in total. Unlike Chapter~21's $(2k-1)$-entry
vertex-visit list (where an internal vertex appears after every child), giving each
vertex $v$ a unique self-loop $\langle v,v\rangle$ provides a single anchor node in the
BST, and we store pointers from each vertex $v$ to $\langle v,v\rangle$ and from each
tree edge $\{u,v\}$ to both $\langle u,v\rangle$ and $\langle v,u\rangle$.

\subsection*{Link and Cut as sequence surgery}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch39-ett-surgery} illustrates how rerooting, linking, and cutting
reduce to splitting and concatenating these sequences.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Reroot.} Making any vertex $u$ the start of its tour is a cyclic shift. The
tour is a closed walk, and $u$ has a unique self-loop entry $\langle u,u\rangle$. If
the sequence reads $S = X\,\langle u,u\rangle\,Y$, then the tour rooted at $u$ is
$\langle u,u\rangle\,Y\,X$: split the BST right before $\langle u,u\rangle$ and swap
the two halves, using one split and one concatenation.

\textbf{Link$(u,v)$} (join two separate trees by edge $\{u,v\}$). Reroot $u$'s tour at
$u$ and $v$'s tour at $v$, so $T_u$ begins with $\langle u,u\rangle$ and $T_v$ begins
with $\langle v,v\rangle$. The combined tour walks $T_u$, crosses $\langle u,v\rangle$
into $T_v$, and returns via $\langle v,u\rangle$:
\[
  \underbrace{\langle u,u\rangle\,\cdots}_{T_u}\; \langle u,v\rangle \;
  \underbrace{\langle v,v\rangle\,\cdots}_{T_v}\; \langle v,u\rangle.
\]
This uses two rerootings and three concatenations.

\textbf{Cut$(\{u,v\})$.} The edge contributes $\langle u,v\rangle$ and
$\langle v,u\rangle$ (ordered so $\langle u,v\rangle$ appears first in the current
tour), and the entries strictly between them form the complete Euler tour $M$ of the
subtree entered via $\langle u,v\rangle$. Writing the sequence as
$L\,\langle u,v\rangle\,M\,\langle v,u\rangle\,R$, splitting around
$\langle u,v\rangle$ and $\langle v,u\rangle$ isolates $M$ as one tree's sequence, and
concatenating $L$ with $R$ forms the other tree's sequence while discarding
$\langle u,v\rangle$ and $\langle v,u\rangle$.
\end{whybox}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Euler-tour tree operations (implicit-key balanced BST)}
\label{ch39:alg-ett}
\SetKwFunction{Reroot}{Reroot}\SetKwFunction{Link}{Link}\SetKwFunction{Cut}{Cut}
\SetKwFunction{Conn}{Connected}\SetKwFunction{Root}{TourRoot}
\SetKwFunction{Split}{Split}\SetKwFunction{Cat}{Concat}
\SetKwProg{Fn}{Function}{:}{}
\Fn{\Reroot{$u$}}{
  let $p$ be the BST node of the self-loop $\langle u,u\rangle$\;
  $(A,B)\gets \Split$ the sequence right before $p$ \tcp*[r]{$A=X,\;B=\langle u,u\rangle\,Y$}
  \Return $\Cat(B,\,A)$ \tcp*[r]{cyclic shift}
}
\Fn{\Link{$u,v$}}{
  $T_u\gets\Reroot{u}$; \quad $T_v\gets\Reroot{v}$\;
  \Return $\Cat(T_u,\,\langle u,v\rangle,\,T_v,\,\langle v,u\rangle)$\;
}
\Fn{\Cut{$u,v$}}{
  locate $\langle u,v\rangle$ and $\langle v,u\rangle$ (swap $u,v$ if needed so $\langle u,v\rangle$ appears first)\;
  split the sequence into $L,\,\langle u,v\rangle,\,M,\,\langle v,u\rangle,\,R$\;
  keep $M$ as one tree and $\Cat(L,R)$ as the other; discard $\langle u,v\rangle$ and $\langle v,u\rangle$\;
}
\Fn{\Conn{$u,v$}}{
  \Return $\Root{u} = \Root{v}$ \tcp*[r]{walk parent pointers from $\langle u,u\rangle$ to its BST root}
}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{complexitybox}
Each ETT operation performs $\Oh(1)$ splits and concatenations on balanced BST sequences
of length $\Oh(n)$, taking $\Oh(\log n)$ time (worst-case with a treap or red-black
tree; amortized with a splay tree). Space is $\Oh(n)$: a $k$-vertex tree stores $3k-2$
BST nodes, summing to at most $3n$ across the forest. \textsc{Connected} walks from
$\langle u,u\rangle$ and $\langle v,v\rangle$ up to their BST roots in $\Oh(\log n)$ time.
\end{complexitybox}

\begin{labbox}
Trace \textsc{Link}$(b,d)$ by hand when $\{b\}$ is an isolated vertex and $\{c,d\}$ is
a separate $2$-vertex tree rooted at $c$. Initially $T_b = \langle b,b\rangle$ and
$T_c = \langle c,c\rangle\,\langle c,d\rangle\,\langle d,d\rangle\,\langle d,c\rangle$.
Rerooting $T_b$ at $b$ leaves $\langle b,b\rangle$. Rerooting $T_c$ at $d$ splits
before $\langle d,d\rangle$ and swaps the halves to give
$T_d = \langle d,d\rangle\,\langle d,c\rangle\,\langle c,c\rangle\,\langle c,d\rangle$.
Splicing them with \textsc{Link}$(b,d)$ produces the $7$-entry tour
\[
  \langle b,b\rangle\;\langle b,d\rangle\;\langle d,d\rangle\;\langle d,c\rangle\;
  \langle c,c\rangle\;\langle c,d\rangle\;\langle d,b\rangle,
\]
so $\langle b,b\rangle$ and $\langle c,c\rangle$ now share a BST and
$\textsc{Connected}(b,c)$ returns true; applying \textsc{Cut}$(b,d)$ slices out the
middle block $T_d$ and restores $\langle b,b\rangle$.
\end{labbox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
ETT also maintains \emph{subtree} aggregates directly, because when a tree is rooted,
every subtree beneath an edge $\{u,v\}$ forms a contiguous slice $M$ between
$\langle u,v\rangle$ and $\langle v,u\rangle$. Augmenting each BST node with the sum of
vertex values on the self-loops $\langle x,x\rangle$ in its BST subtree gives dynamic
subtree sums in $\Oh(\log n)$ time. What ETT does \emph{not} provide, and link-cut trees
do, are \emph{root-to-node path} aggregates: a tree path is scattered across non-adjacent
slices of the Euler tour. Use ETT for connectivity and subtree aggregates, and link-cut
trees for path queries.
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{Fully dynamic connectivity: the HdLT logarithmic levels}
\label{ch39:hdlt}

We now maintain fully dynamic connectivity on a \emph{general} undirected graph using
the logarithmic-level structure of Holm, de~Lichtenberg, and Thorup (HdLT). Keep a
spanning forest $F$ of the current graph $G$. Inserting an edge is immediate; the
hard case is deleting a \emph{tree} edge of $F$, where we must decide whether some
non-tree edge can reconnect the two split pieces without scanning the whole graph.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Keep a spanning forest $F$ inside the graph $G$. Non-tree edges are
\emph{spares}: they connect two vertices already connected through $F$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Insert $\{u,v\}$}: if $u$ and $v$ are already connected in $F$, store
        $\{u,v\}$ as a spare; else \textsc{Link} it into $F$ as a tree edge.
  \item \textbf{Query}: test connectivity in $F$ (an ETT query).
  \item \textbf{Delete a spare}: remove it from the spare list.
  \item \textbf{Delete a tree edge $\{u,v\}$}: \textsc{Cut} it, splitting $F$'s
        tree into parts $T_u$ (containing $u$) and $T_v$ (containing $v$). Search for
        a spare edge with one endpoint in $T_u$ and the other in $T_v$. If found,
        install it as a tree edge (\textsc{Link}); if none exists, the deletion
        disconnected that component.
\end{itemize}
The bottleneck is finding a replacement spare. Scanning all spares incident to the
smaller side can cost $\Th(m)$ on a single deletion. HdLT assigns levels to edges so
that every unsuccessful scan permanently raises an edge's level, bounding the total
number of scans over the edge's lifetime.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection*{Levels and the smaller-half rule}

\begin{focusbox}
Assign every edge a \textbf{level} $\ell(e)\in\{0,1,\dots,L\}$ with
$L=\floor{\log_2 n}$. Maintain, for each level $i$, a spanning forest $F_i$ of the
subgraph $G_i$ formed by all edges of level at least $i$. Two invariants hold:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(I1)] $F_0\supseteq F_1\supseteq\cdots\supseteq F_L$; each $F_i$ is a
        spanning forest of $G_i$, and $F = F_0$ is a spanning forest of all of $G$.
  \item[(I2)] Every connected component of $G_i$ has at most $\floor{n/2^i}$ vertices.
\end{enumerate}
A tree edge of $F$ at level $i$ belongs to $F_i,F_{i-1},\dots,F_0$.
We keep an ETT for each forest $F_i$, augmented so each BST subtree records whether any
vertex in its range has an incident \emph{non-tree} edge of level $i$.
\end{focusbox}

Invariant (I2) drives the amortized bound (Figure~\ref{ch39:fig-levels}). Because every
component of $G_i$ has at most $\floor{n/2^i}$ vertices, no edge can exceed level
$L = \floor{\log_2 n}$ (where $\floor{n/2^L} = 1$, which cannot even hold a $2$-vertex
edge). Thus an edge can be \emph{promoted} (its level increased by $1$) at most $L$
times, and each unsuccessful scan of an edge pays for itself by promoting that edge.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{HdLT fully dynamic connectivity}
\label{ch39:alg-hdlt}
\SetKwFunction{Ins}{Insert}\SetKwFunction{Del}{Delete}\SetKwFunction{Conn}{Connected}
\SetKwFunction{Replace}{Replace}\SetKwFunction{Link}{Link}\SetKwFunction{Cut}{Cut}
\SetKwProg{Fn}{Function}{:}{}
\Fn{\Conn{$u,v$}}{\Return $u,v$ in same tree of $F_0$ (ETT query at level $0$)\;}
\Fn{\Ins{$u,v$}}{
  $\ell(\{u,v\})\gets 0$\;
  \lIf{\Conn{$u,v$}}{store $\{u,v\}$ as a non-tree edge at level $0$}
  \lElse{add $\{u,v\}$ to $F_0$ as a tree edge (ETT \Link\ at level $0$)}
}
\Fn{\Del{$u,v$}}{
  $i\gets \ell(\{u,v\})$\;
  \uIf{$\{u,v\}$ is a non-tree edge}{remove it from the level-$i$ adjacency list\;}
  \Else{
    remove $\{u,v\}$ from $F_i,F_{i-1},\dots,F_0$ (ETT \Cut\ at each level)\;
    \Replace{$u,v,i$}\;
  }
}
\Fn{\Replace{$u,v,i$}}{
  \For{$j\gets i$ \textbf{\emph{downto}} $0$}{
    let $T_u,T_v$ be the two pieces of the level-$j$ forest split by the cut\;
    let $T$ be the \emph{smaller} of $T_u,T_v$ (fewer vertices, via ETT subtree size)\;
    \ForEach{tree edge $e$ inside $T$ with $\ell(e)=j$}{
      $\ell(e)\gets j+1$; add $e$ to $F_{j+1}$ \tcp*[r]{promote tree edges}
    }
    \ForEach{non-tree edge $e=\{x,y\}$ with $\ell(e)=j$ and $x\in T$}{
      \uIf{$y \notin T$}{
        add $e$ to $F_j,F_{j-1},\dots,F_0$ as the replacement tree edge\;
        \Return \tcp*[r]{reconnected}
      }
      \Else{$\ell(e)\gets j+1$ \tcp*[r]{both endpoints in $T$: promote}}
    }
  }
  \tcp{no replacement at any level: $u$ and $v$ are now in separate components}
}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection*{Why \textsc{Replace} is correct}

\begin{whybox}
When we cut a level-$i$ tree edge, we search for a replacement starting at the
\emph{highest} level $i$ and step down toward $0$. At level $j$, the cut has split the
level-$j$ tree into $T_u$ and $T_v$, and we scan from the smaller side $T$. Two cases
arise for each level-$j$ edge touching $T$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Non-tree edge crossing between $T_u$ and $T_v$}: it reconnects the two
        pieces. Add it to $F_j, F_{j-1}, \dots, F_0$ and stop. Installing a replacement
        at the highest possible level $j$ preserves (I1), and because $T_u \cup T_v$
        was already a single level-$j$ component before the cut, its size already
        satisfied (I2).
  \item \textbf{Edge with both endpoints inside $T$} (either a level-$j$ tree edge of
        $T$ or a level-$j$ spare inside $T$): promote it to level $j+1$. Because $T$ is
        the smaller of the two pieces and $T_u \cup T_v$ obeyed (I2) at level $j$,
        \begin{align*}
          \abs{T}
            &\le \bigl\lfloor \tfrac{1}{2}\abs{T_u \cup T_v} \bigr\rfloor
              &&\text{($T$ is the smaller half)} \\
            &\le \bigl\lfloor \tfrac{1}{2}\floor{n / 2^j} \bigr\rfloor
              &&\text{(invariant (I2) at level $j$)} \\
            &= \floor{n / 2^{j+1}}
              &&\text{(since $\floor{\floor{x}/2} = \floor{x/2}$ for all $x \in \R$)}.
        \end{align*}
        Thus promoting edges inside $T$ never violates (I2) at level $j+1$.
\end{itemize}
If level $j$ produces no crossing spare, every level-$j$ spare touching $T$ had both
endpoints in $T$ and has been promoted, so no edge of level at least $j$ crosses between
$T_u$ and $T_v$; we descend to $j-1$ and repeat on the larger forest $F_{j-1}$. If level
$0$ also has no crossing spare, no edge of $G$ connects the two sides, so the deletion
disconnected the component.
\end{whybox}

\begin{formalbox}
\textbf{Theorem (HdLT).} Fully dynamic connectivity on an $n$-vertex graph can be
maintained in $\Oh(\log^2 n)$ amortized time per update and $\Oh(\log n / \log\log n)$
time per query, using $\Oh(m+n\log n)$ space.

\textbf{Proof sketch.} Each edge enters at level $0$ and is promoted at most
$L = \floor{\log_2 n}$ times by (I2). During \textsc{Replace}, every examined edge is
promoted by one level except at most one crossing replacement edge, whose $\Oh(\log^2 n)$
re-linking cost across $F_j,\dots,F_0$ we charge to the \textsc{Delete} call itself.
Moving a promoted edge up one level costs $\Oh(\log n)$ ETT work, so $m$ insertions can
pay for at most $mL = \Oh(m\log n)$ promotions, totaling $\Oh(m\log^2 n)$ work or
$\Oh(\log^2 n)$ amortized per insertion. Cutting a deleted tree edge across at most
$L+1$ forests also costs $\Oh(\log^2 n)$. With binary-BST Euler-tour trees, queries cost
$\Oh(\log n)$; implementing the level-$0$ ETT as a balanced tree with branching factor
$\Th(\log n)$ shrinks its height and reduces query time to $\Oh(\log n / \log\log n)$.
\end{formalbox}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Finding a vertex of $T$ that still has a level-$j$ non-tree edge.}
We do not loop over every vertex of $T$ to look for spares. Instead, each vertex $x$
keeps a doubly linked list of its incident level-$j$ spares, and each level-$j$ ETT node
stores a $1$-bit flag indicating whether its BST subtree contains a self-loop
$\langle x,x\rangle$ whose level-$j$ spare list is nonempty (and similarly a flag for
level-$j$ tree edges). Finding the next level-$j$ spare incident to $T$ is then a single
$\Oh(\log n)$ root-to-leaf walk in $T$'s BST, so we pay $\Oh(\log n)$ only when an edge
is found and either promoted or used as the replacement.
\end{whybox}

$T_b=\{b,c,e,f\}$ ($4$ vertices). Scanning level-$0$ edges touching the smaller side
$T_a$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item tree edge $\{a,d\}$ lies inside $T_a$, so raise $\ell(\{a,d\})$ to $1$ and add
        $\{a,d\}$ to $F_1$;
  \item spare $\{d,e\}$ has $e\in T_b$, so it crosses between $T_a$ and $T_b$ and
        becomes the replacement tree edge in $F_0$ at level $0$.
\end{itemize}
The spanning forest $F_0$ becomes $\{\{a,d\},\{d,e\},\{b,e\},\{b,c\},\{c,f\}\}$, a
single connected tree found after examining two edges. Next, delete the spare $\{e,f\}$
and then the tree edge $\{d,e\}$: at level $0$, $F_0$ splits into $T_a=\{a,d\}$ and
$T_b=\{b,c,e,f\}$ with no remaining level-$0$ edges touching $T_a$ (since $\{a,d\}$ is
already at level $1$), so \textsc{Replace} finishes and $\{a,d\}$ is now disconnected
from $\{b,c,e,f\}$.
\end{labbox}

\begin{trapbox}
Three places people get HdLT wrong. (1) Scanning the \emph{larger} half: the
size-halving budget (I2) only holds for the smaller half; scan the wrong side and
promotions can violate the level cap, destroying the amortized bound. (2)
Forgetting to promote the level-$j$ \emph{tree} edges of $T$ alongside the
spares -- if tree edges are left behind, $F_{j+1}$ stops being a spanning forest
of $G_{j+1}$ and (I1) breaks. (3) Installing a replacement at the wrong level: the
replacement edge must enter $F_j,\dots,F_0$ (all levels at and below where it was
found), or the lower forests lose connectivity.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{marginnotebox}
The smaller-half promotion charging mirrors union-by-size and small-to-large merging. HdLT combined this charging scheme with Euler-tour trees so that finding a replacement edge becomes a logarithmic-time BST descent, reducing the update bound from $\Oh(\sqrt n)$ to $\Oh(\log^2 n)$.
\end{marginnotebox}

\section{Dynamic minimum spanning tree}
\label{ch39:dynamic-mst}

When a weighted graph may be disconnected, we maintain a \emph{minimum spanning forest}
(MSF)---a minimum spanning tree inside each connected component (equal to the MST when
the graph is connected). Updates follow the same spanning-forest-plus-spares structure,
except that replacement edges must be chosen by \emph{weight}.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Two properties from Chapter~6 govern every update:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Cycle rule} (cycle property). Inserting edge $e$ closes a cycle in the
        current MSF $T$ if and only if its endpoints already lie in the same tree of
        $T$. If so, $e$ replaces the \emph{maximum-weight} edge $e^\star$ on the tree
        path between its endpoints whenever $e$ is lighter than $e^\star$; otherwise
        $e$ becomes a spare.
  \item \textbf{Cut rule} (cut property). Deleting a tree edge $e$ splits its tree into
        two sides $T_u$ and $T_v$; the replacement edge is the \emph{minimum-weight}
        spare crossing between $T_u$ and $T_v$.
\end{itemize}
\end{intuitionbox}

Figure~\ref{ch39:fig-dmst-rules} contrasts the two update primitives: path-maximum
replacement on an insertion versus lightest-crossing replacement on a tree-edge
deletion.

\begin{figure}[htbp]

\subsection*{Insertion: the cycle rule with path-max}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Dynamic MSF -- edge insertion}
\label{ch39:alg-dmst-ins}
\SetKwFunction{InsMST}{InsertMST}\SetKwFunction{Link}{Link}\SetKwFunction{Cut}{Cut}
\SetKwProg{Fn}{Function}{:}{}
\Fn{\InsMST{$u,v,w$}}{
  \uIf{$u,v$ in different trees of $T$}{\Link{$u,v$} with weight $w$ \tcp*[r]{forest grows}}
  \Else{
    $e^\star\gets$ maximum-weight edge on the $u$--$v$ tree path \tcp*[r]{$\Oh(\log n)$ via LCT path-max}
    \uIf{$w < \mathrm{weight}(e^\star)$}{
      \Cut{$e^\star$}; move $e^\star$ out of $T$ as a spare; \Link{$u,v$} with weight $w$\;
    }
    \lElse{store $\{u,v\}$ as a spare of weight $w$}
  }
}
\end{algorithm}

The path-maximum query and \textsc{Cut}/\textsc{Link} operations are the link-cut tree
primitives of Chapter~22: a link-cut tree stores the current MSF $T$ and returns the
heaviest edge on the $u$--$v$ path in $\Oh(\log n)$ amortized time, solving
\emph{incremental} MSF in $\Oh(\log n)$ amortized time per insertion.

\subsection*{Deletion: weighted replacement via leveled HdLT}

Deleting a tree edge requires the \emph{minimum-weight} crossing spare, whereas plain
HdLT returns the first crossing spare it encounters. Holm, de~Lichtenberg, and Thorup
adapt the leveled-forest hierarchy so that candidate spares are examined in
nondecreasing order of weight, guaranteeing that the first crossing spare found at the
appropriate level is the lightest one. Maintaining this weight ordering across levels
costs two additional logarithmic factors, giving $\Oh(\log^4 n)$ amortized time per
update.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Dynamic MSF bound.} The minimum spanning forest of a fully dynamic weighted
graph can be maintained in $\Oh(\log^4 n)$ amortized time per insertion and per
deletion (later improved to $\Oh(\log^4 n/\log\log n)$ by Holm, Rotenberg, and
Wulff-Nilsen). Keeping a running sum of tree-edge weights across every \textsc{Link}
and \textsc{Cut} then reports the total MSF weight in $\Oh(1)$ time.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why weight-ordered replacement is correct.} After cutting tree edge $e$, the
split tree forms two vertex sets $T_u$ and $T_v$. Every other tree edge of $T$ lies
entirely inside $T_u$ or entirely inside $T_v$, so only spare edges cross between $T_u$
and $T_v$. By the cut property (Chapter~6), replacing $e$ with the minimum-weight spare
between $T_u$ and $T_v$ (or adding nothing if no spare crosses) yields a minimum
spanning forest of $G \setminus \{e\}$.
\end{whybox}

\begin{labbox}
Assign weights to Figure~\ref{ch39:fig-hdlt}(1): tree edges
$\{a,b\}:1,\; \{b,c\}:2,\; \{a,d\}:3,\; \{b,e\}:4,\; \{c,f\}:5$, and spares
$\{d,e\}:6,\; \{e,f\}:7$. First delete tree edge $\{b,e\}$ (weight $4$), which isolates
$\{e\}$ from $\{a,b,c,d,f\}$. Both spares $\{d,e\}$ ($6$) and $\{e,f\}$ ($7$) cross the
cut; the cut rule installs the lighter spare $\{d,e\}$ ($6$), giving MSF weight
$1+2+3+5+6=17$. Starting over from the original graph, delete $\{a,b\}$ (weight $1$)
instead: across the cut between $\{a,d\}$ and $\{b,c,e,f\}$, only $\{d,e\}$ ($6$)
crosses, so the new MSF weight is $2+3+4+5+6=20$. Recomputing Kruskal's algorithm from
scratch confirms both totals.
\end{labbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Dynamic MST is where Chapter~22 (link-cut trees, for the cheap incremental side
and path-max) and this chapter's leveled ETT (for the hard decremental side) meet.
The same path-max primitive powers Sleator--Tarjan dynamic-tree speedups of
blocking-flow max-flow (Chapter~12--13). And the offline ``segment tree over the
timeline'' from \S\ref{ch39:incremental}, combined with rollback union-find that
tracks the maximum edge on each merge, gives a simple offline dynamic-MST that
is often the practical choice when all updates are known in advance.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Sparsification: trading \texorpdfstring{$m$}{m} for \texorpdfstring{$n$}{n}}
\label{ch39:sparsification}

Many dynamic bounds depend on $n$ rather than $m$, even when $m=\Th(n^2)$. The reason
dense graphs do not slow updates down to $m$ is \emph{sparsification}, introduced by
Eppstein, Galil, Italiano, and Nissenzweig (1992).

\begin{intuitionbox}
A \emph{sparse certificate} for a graph property on a graph $H$ is a subgraph
$C \subseteq H$ with $\Oh(n)$ edges that gives the exact same answer as $H$ even after
more edges are added to both: for connectivity (or MSF), any spanning forest (or MSF)
of $H$ has at most $n-1$ edges and preserves all component relationships; similar
$\Oh(n)$-edge certificates exist for bipartiteness and $k$-edge-connectivity (whether
removing fewer than $k$ edges can disconnect the graph; Chapter~10).

As Figure~\ref{fig:ch39-sparsification} illustrates, partition $G$'s edge set into
leaves of $\Oh(n)$ edges each and build a balanced binary tree above them. Each internal
node stores a sparse certificate $C$ computed from the union of its two children's
certificates---an input of $\Oh(n)$ edges reduced back down to $\Oh(n)$ edges. An edge
insertion or deletion modifies one leaf and recomputes only the $\Oh(\log(m/n)) \subseteq \Oh(\log n)$
certificates along the path to the root. Running a dynamic algorithm on the root
certificate replaces $m$ by $\Oh(n)$ everywhere inside the base algorithm.
\end{intuitionbox}

Sparsification multiplies the per-update work by $\Oh(\log n)$ while replacing $m$ with

\end{complexitybox}

\section{Worst-case versus amortized, and a glance at harder problems}
\label{ch39:worstcase}

HdLT's $\Oh(\log^2 n)$ guarantee is \emph{amortized}: a single deletion can trigger a
cascade of promotions and take $\Th(n)$ time, even though the average over all updates
stays $\Oh(\log^2 n)$. When an individual operation must never stall (as in real-time
systems), worst-case bounds per operation are required.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Complexity bounds for fully dynamic connectivity.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Amortized: $\Oh(\log^2 n)$ deterministic (HdLT), refined to
        $\Oh(\log^2 n/\log\log n)$ (Wulff-Nilsen).
  \item Worst-case: $\Oh(\sqrt n)$ deterministic (Frederickson plus sparsification);
        $\Oh(\log^c n)$ worst-case \emph{randomized} via cut-set XOR sketching
        (Kapron, King, and Mountjoy, 2013, and successors).
  \item Lower bound: $\Om(\log n)$ per operation in the cell-probe model
        (P\u{a}tra\c{s}cu and Demaine, 2006), ruling out constant-time updates and
        queries.
\end{itemize}
\end{focusbox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
\textbf{The cut-set sketch.} Bitwise exclusive-or ($\oplus$, written XOR) combines two
bitstrings bit by bit so equal bits give $0$ and different bits give $1$; thus every
value cancels itself ($x \oplus x = 0$) and $0$ is the identity ($x \oplus 0 = x$).
Assign each edge $e=\{u,v\}$ a random $\Oh(\log n)$-bit identifier $\mathrm{id}(e)$,
and store at each vertex $x$ the XOR of the identifiers of all edges incident to $x$,
denoted $\mathrm{sketch}(x)$. For any subset of vertices $S \subseteq V$ with complement
$\bar S = V \setminus S$ (all vertices outside $S$), XORing $\mathrm{sketch}(x)$ over
$x \in S$ cancels every edge with both endpoints inside $S$ because its identifier
appears twice (Figure~\ref{fig:ch39-cutsketch}):
\[
  \bigoplus_{x \in S} \mathrm{sketch}(x)
  \;=\; \bigoplus_{e \in E(S,\bar S)} \mathrm{id}(e),
\]
where $E(S,\bar S)$ is the set of edges with one endpoint in $S$ and one in $\bar S$.
When a single edge $e^\star$ crosses $(S,\bar S)$, that sum equals $\mathrm{id}(e^\star)$
directly, recovering a replacement edge with no search. Maintaining $\Oh(\log n)$
independent subsampled layers isolates a single crossing edge \emph{with high
probability} (probability at least $1 - 1/n^c$ for a chosen constant $c \ge 1$). This is
an instance of \emph{linear sketching}---compressing a graph into small additive or
XOR summaries---which returns in Chapter~40.
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{Conditional lower bounds: the OMv wall}
\label{ch39:omv}

Why do so many dynamic problems stop at $\Oh(\sqrt n)$ or $\Oh(n)$ per operation despite
decades of work? A unified explanation comes from the \emph{Online Matrix--Vector
multiplication} (OMv) conjecture of Henzinger, Krinninger, Nanongkai, and Saranurak
(2015).

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{OMv problem.} Preprocess a fixed $n\times n$ \emph{Boolean matrix} $M$ (whose
entries are $0$ or $1$). Then receive $n$ column vectors $v_1,\dots,v_n\in\{0,1\}^n$
\emph{online}: for each $t$, output the Boolean matrix--vector product $Mv_t$, defined
using logical OR ($\vee$) in place of addition and logical AND ($\wedge$) in place of
multiplication,
\[
  (Mv_t)_i = \bigvee_{j=1}^{n} \bigl(M_{ij} \wedge (v_t)_j\bigr),
\]
before $v_{t+1}$ is revealed.

\textbf{OMv conjecture.} No algorithm solves OMv in \emph{truly subcubic} total time
$\Oh(n^{3-\eps})$ for any constant $\eps>0$ (saving a polynomial factor $n^\eps$ over
$n^3$), even after arbitrary polynomial-time preprocessing of $M$.
\end{focusbox}

Computing each $Mv_t$ directly takes $\Oh(n^2)$ time, or $\Oh(n^3)$ across all $n$
vectors. Offline---when $v_1,\dots,v_n$ are all known up front---stacking them into an
$n\times n$ matrix beats $\Oh(n^3)$ via fast matrix multiplication, but no truly
subcubic algorithm is known when each $Mv_t$ must be produced before seeing $v_{t+1}$.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{From OMv to a dynamic lower bound: the reduction pattern}
(Figure~\ref{ch39:fig-omv}). Encode $M$ as a directed bipartite graph $H$ on vertex
parts $A=\{a_1,\dots,a_n\}$ and $B=\{b_1,\dots,b_n\}$, placing a directed edge
$b_j \to a_i$ whenever $M_{ij}=1$, plus an isolated source vertex $s$. To process an
online vector $v_t$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Update phase}: for each $j$ with $(v_t)_j=1$, insert the directed edge
        $s \to b_j$ (activating column $j$). This performs at most $n$ insertions.
  \item \emph{Query phase}: for each $i\in\{1,\dots,n\}$, ask ``is $a_i$ reachable from
        $s$?'' A $2$-step path $s \to b_j \to a_i$ exists if and only if $(v_t)_j=1$
        and $M_{ij}=1$, so the answer equals $\bigvee_{j=1}^n (M_{ij}\wedge (v_t)_j) = (Mv_t)_i$.
        This performs $n$ queries.
  \item \emph{Cleanup}: delete the inserted edges out of $s$ to reset for $v_{t+1}$
        (at most $n$ deletions).
\end{enumerate}
If a fully dynamic reachability structure ran in $u(n)$ time per update and $q(n)$ time
per query, answering all $n$ vectors would take total time
\[
  n \cdot \Oh\bigl(n \cdot (u(n) + q(n))\bigr)
  \;=\; \Oh\bigl(n^2 (u(n) + q(n))\bigr).
\]
If both $u(n)$ and $q(n)$ were $\Oh(n^{1-\eps})$ for some constant $\eps>0$, the total
time would be $\Oh(n^{3-\eps})$, refuting the OMv conjecture. Thus, \emph{assuming OMv,
fully dynamic directed $s$--$t$ reachability cannot achieve $\Oh(n^{1-\eps})$ time for
both updates and queries.}
\end{whybox}

\needspace{12\baselineskip}
\begin{formalbox}
\textbf{Consequences (conditional on OMv).} No fully dynamic algorithm achieves
$\Oh(n^{1-\eps})$ worst-case or amortized time per operation (for any constant $\eps>0$)
for:
\begin{itemize}
  \item single-source or $s$--$t$ \textbf{reachability} in directed graphs;
  \item \textbf{strong connectivity} (testing whether the whole digraph is one strongly
        connected component, SCC; Chapter~5);
  \item maintaining whether a graph has a \textbf{perfect matching} (a set of
        vertex-disjoint edges covering every vertex; Chapter~15);
  \item $\Oh(\log^c n)$-approximate shortest paths and several exact distance problems;
  \item triangle detection and subgraph counting under edge updates.
\end{itemize}
These bounds rule out polynomial speedups below $\Oh(n)$ (or $\Oh(\sqrt m)$ on sparse
graphs) for the problems captured by the reduction.
\end{formalbox}

\begin{intuitionbox}
\emph{Undirected} connectivity escapes the OMv reduction because undirected edges
propagate paths in both directions: connecting $s$ to $b_1$ and $b_3$ in
Figure~\ref{ch39:fig-omv} would let walks bounce backward $s$--$b_1$--$a_2$--$b_3$ and
blur columns together, whereas spanning forests maintain undirected components in
polylogarithmic time. \emph{Directed} reachability, exact shortest paths, and maximum
matching preserve the one-way $s \to B \to A$ barrier and inherit OMv hardness.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
The OMv conjecture belongs to \emph{fine-grained complexity}: whereas NP-hardness
(Chapter~37) separates polynomial from exponential time, fine-grained reductions prove
conditional lower bounds inside polynomial time (such as $\Om(n^2)$ or $\Om(n^3)$).
Alongside OMv, three standard hypotheses anchor the area: the Strong Exponential Time
Hypothesis (\textbf{SETH}, that $k$-SAT requires $(2-o(1))^n$ time as $k$ grows),
\textbf{3SUM} (that testing whether $n$ integers contain three summing to $0$ requires
$n^{2-o(1)}$ time), and \textbf{APSP} (that all-pairs shortest paths on general weighted
graphs requires $n^{3-o(1)}$ time; Chapter~9). This division explains why
\S\ref{ch39:hdlt}--\S\ref{ch39:sparsification} focus on undirected, spanning-forest
properties: those are the problems that avoid the barrier. Chapter~40 studies the same
graph properties when memory, rather than update time, is the bottleneck.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Putting it together}
\label{ch39:summary}

\begin{complexitybox}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Problem & Model & Per-operation cost \\
\midrule
Connectivity & incremental & $\Oh(\alpha(n))$ amortized (union-find) \\
Connectivity (forest) & fully dynamic & $\Oh(\log n)$ (Euler-tour tree) \\
Connectivity & fully dynamic & $\Oh(\log^2 n)$ amortized (HdLT) \\
Connectivity & fully dynamic, w.c. & $\Oh(\sqrt n)$ det.; polylog rand. (sketch) \\
MST weight & incremental & $\Oh(\log n)$ (link-cut, cycle rule) \\
Min spanning forest & fully dynamic & $\Oh(\log^4 n)$ amortized (leveled ETT) \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{complexitybox}

In summary: maintain a \emph{spanning forest}, store everything else as
spares, and handle deletions by a smaller-half charging argument that bounds how
often any one edge is touched. Euler-tour trees make ``connectivity = same
sequence'' and ``find an incident spare'' both logarithmic; the leveled-forest
wrapper turns the expensive replacement search into a bounded number of
promotions. Where the spanning-forest idea does not apply -- directed
reachability, exact distances, matching -- the OMv conjecture tells us, with high
confidence, that no polylog method exists, and $\sqrt n$ or $n$ is the best we can
hope for.

\section{Historical Context and Controversies}

\begin{historybox}
For the first decades of graph algorithms, dynamic updates meant rerunning Dijkstra's
algorithm or depth-first search from scratch in linear time. Dynamic graph data
structures began systematically in 1985, when Greg Frederickson introduced
\emph{topology trees} (hierarchical balanced tree clusterings of vertices) to maintain
minimum spanning trees in $\Oh(\sqrt{m})$ time per update. In 1992, David Eppstein, Zvi
Galil, Giuseppe Italiano, and Amnon Nissenzweig developed sparsification, maintaining a
balanced binary tree of $\Oh(n)$-edge sparse certificates over the edge set to improve
the bound to $\Oh(\sqrt{n})$ on dense undirected graphs.

The jump from $\Oh(\sqrt{n})$ to polylogarithmic time came in 1995, when Monika
Henzinger and Valerie King combined Euler-tour trees with randomized edge sampling to
achieve $\Oh(\log^3 n)$ expected amortized time per update. In 1998, Jacob Holm,
Kristian de~Lichtenberg, and Mikkel Thorup replaced random sampling with the
deterministic smaller-half promotion hierarchy presented in \S\ref{ch39:hdlt}, reaching
$\Oh(\log^2 n)$ amortized time for connectivity and $\Oh(\log^4 n)$ for minimum spanning
forests. On worst-case bounds, Valerie King, Bruce Kapron, and Ben Mountjoy (2013)
achieved worst-case polylogarithmic updates via randomized XOR cut-set sketches, though
their guarantee assumes an oblivious update sequence. Whether a \emph{deterministic}
algorithm (or a randomized one facing an \emph{adaptive adversary}---an input generator
that chooses each next edge update after seeing the algorithm's previous answers and
internal state) can achieve worst-case polylogarithmic time per operation remains a
major open problem, with subpolynomial $n^{o(1)}$ deterministic worst-case bounds established
by Chuzhoy, Gao, Li, Nanongkai, Peng, and Saranurak. Meanwhile, the 2015 Online
Matrix--Vector ($\mathrm{OMv}$) conjecture of Henzinger, Krinninger, Nanongkai, and
Saranurak explained why thirty years of work had failed to achieve polylogarithmic
updates for directed reachability and exact shortest paths.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{What We Can Now Do and Its Limitations}
\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{What we can now maintain}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Undirected connectivity under online edge insertions and deletions in
        $\Oh(\log^2 n)$ amortized time per update and $\Oh(\log n / \log\log n)$ query
        time, and minimum spanning forests in $\Oh(\log^4 n)$ amortized time.
  \item Dynamic forest operations (\textsc{Link}, \textsc{Cut}, connectivity, and
        subtree aggregates) in $\Oh(\log n)$ time via Euler-tour trees.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Limitations}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Amortized vs.\ worst-case latency}: HdLT's $\Oh(\log^2 n)$ bound is
        amortized; a single deletion can promote many edges across levels and take
        $\Om(n)$ worst-case time, which is unsuitable for hard real-time budgets.
  \item \emph{The directed barrier}: Under the $\mathrm{OMv}$ conjecture, directed
        reachability, strong connectivity, and exact shortest paths require polynomial
        time per update or query.
  \item \emph{Memory overhead}: Storing $\Oh(\log n)$ levels of augmented Euler-tour
        BSTs requires $\Oh(m + n\log n)$ words and substantial pointer overhead compared
        with a static adjacency list.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Where we go next}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Chapter~40 (Streaming and Massive Graphs) considers graphs whose edges arrive as
        a stream and do not fit in main memory, compressing connectivity, cuts, and
        approximate distances into $\Oh(n\,\log^c n)$ space via sparse
        distance-preserving subgraphs and randomized linear sketches.
  \item Chapter~41 (Laplacian Solvers and Electrical Flows) connects dynamic maintenance
        to numerical optimization, where dynamically updating effective resistances
        (Chapter~35) speeds up electrical-flow algorithms for maximum flow.
\end{itemize}
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch39:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Offline beats online]
Implement offline fully dynamic connectivity using a segment tree over the
timeline plus rollback union-find (union by rank, no path compression, LIFO undo).
Each edge is alive on an interval of operations; place it on the canonical segment
nodes, traverse depth-first unioning on entry and rolling back on exit, and answer each
query at its leaf. Prove the total time is $\Oh((m+q)\log q\,\log n)$ and explain why
this technique fails when queries are \emph{online}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Euler-tour subtree sums]
Augment an Euler-tour tree so that each vertex carries a value and you support
\textsc{SubtreeSum}$(v)$ = sum of values in $v$'s subtree, under \textsc{Link},
\textsc{Cut}, and point updates, all in $\Oh(\log n)$. Argue why a subtree is a
contiguous range of the tour, and explain why the same trick does \emph{not} give
you path sums (so you would need a link-cut tree for those).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The smaller-half budget]
In HdLT \textsc{Replace}, prove invariant (I2) is preserved: when you promote every
level-$j$ edge inside the smaller side $T$ to level $j{+}1$, every level-$(j{+}1)$
component still has at most $\floor{n/2^{j+1}}$ vertices. Then show that if
you mistakenly scanned and promoted the \emph{larger} half instead, (I2) can fail,
and exhibit a sequence of operations whose amortized cost then degrades to
$\Om(\sqrt n)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Counting components]
Extend HdLT so that \textsc{NumComponents}() returns the current number of
connected components in $\Oh(1)$. (Hint: a forest on $n$ vertices with $k$ trees
has exactly $n-k$ tree edges; maintain the count of tree edges across all
\textsc{Link}/\textsc{Cut}.) State exactly which lines of
Algorithm~\ref{ch39:alg-hdlt} must update the counter.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Incremental MST, the easy side]
Using a link-cut tree with path-max, implement \textsc{InsertMST} (cycle rule) so
that inserting an edge updates the MST in $\Oh(\log n)$ amortized. Prove
correctness from the cycle rule, and explain in one sentence why the \emph{deletion}
side cannot be handled by the link-cut tree alone (what does the cut rule need that
a single tree cannot provide?).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Break the weight order]
Construct a small fully dynamic graph and a deletion where the HdLT replacement
search, if it returns the \emph{first} crossing spare it finds rather than the
\emph{minimum-weight} one, produces a spanning forest that is not minimum. Then fix
the search to restore the cut rule and re-verify on your example.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The cut-set sketch]
Give each edge a uniformly random $2\ceil{\log n}$-bit id and store at each
vertex the XOR of incident edge ids. Prove that for any vertex set $S$ with exactly
one edge crossing $(S,\bar S)$, the XOR of stored values over $S$ equals that
edge's id. Then design an $\Oh(\log n)$-layer scheme that, with high probability,
recovers a crossing edge even when the number of crossers is unknown. Where does
the failure probability come from?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[OMv reduction, in full]
Write out the reduction of \S\ref{ch39:omv} for fully dynamic $s$--$t$
reachability completely: specify the gadget graph, the update sequence per query
vector $v_t$, how the $n$ reachability queries reconstruct $Mv_t$, and the cleanup.
Conclude that an $\Oh(n^{1-\eps})$-per-operation structure would refute OMv. Then
identify exactly which step of your reduction breaks if the graph is
\emph{undirected} -- and connect this to why undirected connectivity escapes the
barrier.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Decremental only]
Suppose edges are only ever deleted (decremental connectivity), starting from a
known graph. Show how to preprocess so that the \emph{total} time over \emph{all}
deletions plus connectivity queries is $\Oh(m\log^2 n)$, and argue why the
decremental setting can sometimes beat the fully dynamic bound (hint: levels never
need to go back down, so part of the bookkeeping disappears).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Worst-case via lazy rebuilding]
Sketch how to convert an amortized $\Oh(\log^2 n)$ structure into one with
$\Oh(\sqrt n)$ worst-case per operation by combining sparsification with periodic
rebuilding of overloaded levels. Identify the trade-off: what do you give up in the
amortized total to buy the worst-case guarantee?
\end{exercise}
