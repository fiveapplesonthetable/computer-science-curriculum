\chapter{Laplacian Solvers and Max-Flow via Electrical Flows}
\label{ch:laplacian-solvers}

Part~VI used the graph Laplacian $\Lap$ of an $n$-vertex, $m$-edge graph to analyze smoothness, cut structure, and random-walk commute times. Here we turn $\Lap$ into an algorithmic primitive. Given a demand vector $b \in \R^n$ whose entries sum to zero ($\mathbf{1}^\top b = 0$, where $\mathbf{1}$ is the all-ones vector, meaning total current injected equals total current extracted), we want to find a vector of vertex potentials $x \in \R^n$ solving
\[
  \Lap x = b .
\]
Spielman and Teng showed that this linear system can be solved to relative error $\eps > 0$ in time \emph{nearly linear} in the number of edges: $\Oh(m \log^c n \log(1/\eps))$ for a fixed constant $c$, often abbreviated $\widetilde{\Oh}(m \log(1/\eps))$ where $\widetilde{\Oh}(\cdot)$ hides $\poly(\log n)$ factors. Once $\Lap x = b$ is that cheap, several graph problems reduce to calling a Laplacian solver:
\begin{itemize}
  \item One solve computes the electrical current routing $b$ across a resistor network.
  \item $\Oh(\log n / \eps^2)$ solves along random directions estimate effective resistances on all $m$ edges at once, letting us compress a dense graph into a sparse one that preserves every cut within $1 \pm \eps$.
  \item A sequence of electrical-flow solves approximates \emph{maximum $s$--$t$ flow} (Part~III) on undirected capacitated graphs, replacing discrete augmenting paths with continuous linear algebra.
\end{itemize}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Keep the spring-and-resistor picture from Chapters~31 and~35 in front of you. Inject $+1$ unit of current into vertex $s$ and extract $1$ unit from vertex $t$; the network settles into the unique current obeying Ohm's and Kirchhoff's laws while minimizing dissipated heat energy. Write $e_v \in \R^n$ for the standard basis vector with $1$ in vertex $v$'s coordinate and $0$ elsewhere. The vertex potentials $x$ driving this unit $s$--$t$ current solve $\Lap x = b$ for $b = e_s - e_t$. Solving a Laplacian system and finding an electrical flow are two sides of Ohm's law: $x$ holds the vertex potentials and $\Lap x$ records the net current injected at each vertex.

For maximum flow, an electrical flow already routes the desired current from $s$ to $t$, though it ignores edge capacities and may overload narrow bottlenecks. If we raise the resistance of overloaded edges and re-solve, the next electrical flow routes current around them. Repeating this update and averaging the resulting electrical flows produces a capacity-respecting near-maximum flow.
\end{intuitionbox}

The edges are $e_1=\{s,a\}$, $e_2=\{s,b\}$, $e_3=\{a,c\}$, $e_4=\{b,c\}$, $e_5=\{a,b\}$, and $e_6=\{c,t\}$. Orient each edge from its earlier endpoint to its later one in the order $s<a<b<c<t$; these arrows are only sign conventions, and a negative flow value means current runs opposite the arrow. Recall from Chapter~31 that $\Lap = \mathbf{D} - \Adjm$, where $\mathbf{D}$ is the diagonal matrix of weighted vertex degrees and $\Adjm$ is the weighted adjacency matrix. With unit conductances and vertex order $(s,a,b,c,t)$,
\[
  \Lap \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    2 & -1 & -1 & 0 & 0\\
    -1 & 3 & -1 & -1 & 0\\
    -1 & -1 & 3 & -1 & 0\\
    0 & -1 & -1 & 3 & -1\\
    0 & 0 & 0 & -1 & 1
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Every row sums to zero, so $\Lap \mathbf{1} = 0$. Because $\Gph$ is connected, the null space $\ker \Lap = \{x \in \R^n : \Lap x = 0\}$ is the one-dimensional line $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{1}\}$ of constant vectors, and $\rank \Lap = n - 1 = 4$. Injecting unit current at $s$ and extracting it at $t$ corresponds to solving $\Lap x = b$ for $b = e_s - e_t = (1,0,0,0,-1)^\top$.

\begin{focusbox}
The edge--vertex \emph{incidence matrix} $\mathbf{B}\in\R^{m\times n}$ has one row per oriented edge: for $e=(u\to v)$, row $e$ equals $e_u^\top - e_v^\top$ ($+1$ in column $u$, $-1$ in column $v$, and $0$ elsewhere), so $(\mathbf{B}x)_e = x_u - x_v$ records the potential drop across edge $e$. Writing $\mathbf{W}=\operatorname{diag}(w_e)$ for the $m\times m$ diagonal matrix of conductances gives
\[
  \Lap \;=\; \mathbf{B}^\top \mathbf{W}\, \mathbf{B},
  \qquad
  x^\top \Lap x \;=\; \sum_{e=\{u,v\}} w_e\,(x_u - x_v)^2 .
\]
Because every term $w_e(x_u - x_v)^2 \ge 0$, $\Lap$ is positive semidefinite ($\Lap \succeq 0$), with $x^\top \Lap x = 0$ if and only if $x$ is constant on each connected component ($\mathbf{B}x = 0$). Since $w_e(x_u - x_v)^2 = (x_u - x_v)^2 / r_e$, the quadratic form $x^\top \Lap x$ equals the total electrical power dissipated under vertex potentials $x$.
\end{focusbox}

\section{Electrical flows as energy minimization}\label{ch41:elec}

A \emph{flow} is a vector $f\in\R^m$ assigning one real number $f_e$ to each oriented edge $e=(u\to v)$, with $f_e < 0$ meaning $\abs{f_e}$ units move from $v$ to $u$. For any vertex $v$, column $v$ of $\mathbf{B}$ has $+1$ on edges leaving $v$ and $-1$ on edges entering $v$, so $(\mathbf{B}^\top f)_v$ is the net flow leaving $v$. A flow \emph{routes demand} $b \in \R^n$ when it obeys flow conservation $\mathbf{B}^\top f = b$ at every vertex. Summing all $n$ rows gives $\mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{B}^\top f = (\mathbf{B}\mathbf{1})^\top f = 0$, confirming that a routing flow exists only when $\mathbf{1}^\top b = 0$. Among all flows routing $b$, Thomson's principle (Chapter~35) characterizes the physical electrical current as the unique flow of minimum dissipated energy.

\begin{definition}[Electrical flow]\label{ch41:def-elec}
Let $\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{W}^{-1}=\operatorname{diag}(r_e)$ be the $m\times m$ diagonal matrix of edge resistances. The \emph{energy} of a flow $f \in \R^m$ is
\[
  \mathcal{E}(f) \;=\; \sum_{e \in E} r_e f_e^2 \;=\; f^\top \mathbf{R}\, f .
\]
The \emph{electrical flow} routing demand $b$ (with $\mathbf{1}^\top b = 0$) is the minimum-energy routing
\[
  f^\star \;=\; \arg\min_{f:\ \mathbf{B}^\top f = b}\ \sum_{e \in E} r_e f_e^2 .
\]
\end{definition}

Minimizing the strictly upward-curving quadratic $f^\top \mathbf{R} f$ subject to linear equality constraints $\mathbf{B}^\top f = b$ has a unique solution. To find it, the method of \emph{Lagrange multipliers} attaches one scalar multiplier $x_v \in \R$ to each vertex constraint $(\mathbf{B}^\top f)_v = b_v$ and sets the partial derivatives of the combined objective (the \emph{Lagrangian}) to zero; those multipliers turn out to be the vertex potentials, and the resulting optimality conditions are Ohm's and Kirchhoff's laws.

\begin{theorem}[Ohm + Kirchhoff $=$ a Laplacian solve]\label{ch41:thm-ohm}
Let $x \in \R^n$ be any solution to $\Lap x = b$ (all solutions differ by adding a scalar multiple of $\mathbf{1}$). Then
\[
  f^\star \;=\; \mathbf{W}\mathbf{B}\, x,
  \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad
  f^\star_e \;=\; w_e\,(x_u - x_v)\ \text{ for each } e=(u\to v),
\]
is the unique electrical flow routing $b$. Conversely, $f^\star$ determines the potentials $x$ on a connected graph up to adding a constant to all vertices.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
Form the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}(f, x) = f^\top \mathbf{R} f - 2x^\top(\mathbf{B}^\top f - b)$ with multiplier vector $x\in\R^n$ (the factor $-2$ cancels the $2$ from differentiating $f_e^2$). Setting derivatives with respect to $f$ and $x$ to zero gives the two circuit laws:
\begin{align*}
  \nabla_f \mathcal{L} = 2\mathbf{R}f - 2\mathbf{B}x &= 0
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f = \mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{B}x = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{B}x
    &&\text{(Ohm's law)}, \\
  \mathbf{B}^\top f &= b
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{B}\,x = \Lap x = b
    &&\text{(Kirchhoff's current law)}.
\end{align*}
Ohm's law sets each edge's current to conductance times potential drop; Kirchhoff's current law equates net current leaving each vertex to its external injection $b_v$. Together they reduce to the single linear system $\Lap x = b$.
\end{whybox}

\begin{proof}
Because $\mathbf{1}^\top b = 0$ and $\Gph$ is connected, $b$ is orthogonal to $\ker\Lap = \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{1}\}$, so $\Lap x = b$ has a solution $x$, unique up to adding $\tau\mathbf{1}$ ($\tau\in\R$). Since $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{1} = 0$, the candidate flow $f^\star = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{B}x$ does not depend on $\tau$, and it satisfies conservation $\mathbf{B}^\top f^\star = \mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{B}x = \Lap x = b$.

Now let $f$ be any flow routing $b$, and write $c = f - f^\star$. Because $\mathbf{B}^\top c = b - b = 0$ ($c$ is a \emph{circulation}, carrying zero net flow out of every vertex), the cross term in $\mathcal{E}(f^\star + c)$ vanishes:
\[
  (f^\star)^\top \mathbf{R}\, c \;=\; x^\top \mathbf{B}^\top \mathbf{W}\mathbf{R}\, c \;=\; x^\top \mathbf{B}^\top c \;=\; 0 .
\]
Expanding $\mathcal{E}(f) = (f^\star + c)^\top \mathbf{R} (f^\star + c)$ therefore gives
\[
  \mathcal{E}(f) \;=\; \mathcal{E}(f^\star) + c^\top \mathbf{R}\, c \;=\; \mathcal{E}(f^\star) + \sum_{e \in E} r_e c_e^2 .
\]
Since every resistance $r_e > 0$ ($\mathbf{R}$ is strictly positive definite, written $\mathbf{R} \succ 0$), we have $\sum_e r_e c_e^2 > 0$ whenever $c \ne 0$. Hence $f^\star$ is the unique global minimizer of $\mathcal{E}(f)$. Conversely, given $f^\star$, the differences $x_u - x_v = r_e f^\star_e$ fix $x$ along every path from a chosen reference vertex, determining $x$ up to an additive constant.
\end{proof}

\begin{labbox}
Solve $\Lap x = e_s - e_t$ on the running network by hand. Because adding a constant to every coordinate of $x$ leaves all differences $x_u - x_v$ unchanged, pin $x_t = 0$ (grounding $t$) and drop the last row and column, leaving a $4\times 4$ system in $(x_s,x_a,x_b,x_c)$:
\[
  \begin{pmatrix} 2&-1&-1&0\\ -1&3&-1&-1\\ -1&-1&3&-1\\ 0&-1&-1&3\end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix}x_s\\x_a\\x_b\\x_c\end{pmatrix}
  =\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Swapping $a$ and $b$ leaves the network and demand unchanged, so $x_a = x_b$; write $p = x_a = x_b$. Eliminating step by step gives
\begin{align*}
  -x_s + 3p - p - x_c = 0 &\;\Longrightarrow\; x_s + x_c = 2p &&\text{(row 2)}, \\
  -2p + 3x_c = 0 &\;\Longrightarrow\; x_c = \tfrac{2}{3}p,\quad x_s = 2p - x_c = \tfrac{4}{3}p &&\text{(row 4)}, \\
  2x_s - 2p = 1 &\;\Longrightarrow\; \tfrac{8}{3}p - 2p = \tfrac{2}{3}p = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; p = \tfrac{3}{2} &&\text{(row 1)}.
\end{align*}
Thus $x_s = 2$, $x_a = x_b = \tfrac{3}{2}$, $x_c = 1$, and $x_t = 0$. Ohm's law $f_e = w_e(x_u - x_v) = x_u - x_v$ reads off the six edge currents:
\[
  f_{sa}=2-\tfrac{3}{2}=\tfrac{1}{2},\quad f_{sb}=\tfrac{1}{2},\quad f_{ab}=0,\quad
  f_{ac}=\tfrac{3}{2}-1=\tfrac{1}{2},\quad f_{bc}=\tfrac{1}{2},\quad f_{ct}=1-0=1.
\]
Conservation holds at $s$ ($\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}=1=b_s$) and at $c$ ($\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}$ in, $1$ out). Edge $\{a,b\}$ sees zero potential drop and carries zero current. The dissipated energy equals the total voltage drop from $s$ to $t$:
\[
  \mathcal{E}(f^\star) \;=\; \sum_{e \in E} r_e (f^\star_e)^2 \;=\; 4\cdot\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)^2 + 0 + 1^2 \;=\; 2 \;=\; b^\top x \;=\; x_s - x_t .
\]
\end{labbox}

\subsection{Effective resistance}\label{ch41:reff}

\begin{definition}[Effective resistance]\label{ch41:def-reff}
Let $\Lap^{+}$ be the Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse of $\Lap$ (Chapters~31 and~35), which inverts $\Lap$ on $\mathbf{1}^\perp = \{y \in \R^n : \mathbf{1}^\top y = 0\}$ and sends $\mathbf{1}$ to $0$. For vertices $s\ne t$, the \emph{effective resistance} between $s$ and $t$ is
\[
  R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t) \;=\; (e_s-e_t)^\top \Lap^{+}(e_s-e_t) .
\]
It equals both the potential drop $x_s - x_t$ and the dissipated energy $\mathcal{E}(f^\star)$ when one unit of current is routed from $s$ to $t$.
\end{definition}

On the running network, $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)=x_s-x_t=2$: four unit resistors on $\{s,a,b,c\}$ form two parallel paths of resistance $2$ (net resistance $1$), in series with $\{c,t\}$ of resistance $1$. Effective resistance is a \emph{metric} on $V$ (symmetric, zero only when $s=t$, and obeying the triangle inequality $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t) \le R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,u) + R_{\mathrm{eff}}(u,t)$). For an edge $e=\{u,v\}$ of conductance $w_e$, the dimensionless product
\[
  \tau_e \;=\; w_e\, R_{\mathrm{eff}}(u,v) \;\in\; (0, 1]
\]
is called the \emph{leverage score} of edge $e$. By Ohm's law, when $1$ unit of current is injected at $u$ and extracted at $v$, the current crossing edge $e$ directly is $w_e(x_u - x_v) = w_e R_{\mathrm{eff}}(u,v) = \tau_e$, while the remaining $1 - \tau_e$ detours through other paths. A bridge (cut edge) has no alternate path, so $\tau_e = 1$; an edge buried inside a dense cluster has many parallel detours, so $\tau_e \ll 1$.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think}: Why does the effective resistance between two endpoints $u$ and $v$ never exceed the resistance $r_e$ of a direct edge between them? Routing all $1$ unit of current across $e$ alone is one valid $u$--$v$ flow of energy $r_e \cdot 1^2 = r_e$. Because the electrical flow minimizes energy over all valid routings, alternative paths through the rest of the graph can only lower the minimum energy $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(u,v) \le r_e$ (equivalently, $\tau_e = R_{\mathrm{eff}}(u,v)/r_e \le 1$).
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Effective resistance connects three topics from Part~VI:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Euclidean geometry}: writing $\Lap^{+/2}$ for the symmetric square root of $\Lap^{+}$, $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t) = \norm{\Lap^{+/2}(e_s - e_t)}_2^2$ embeds vertices into $\R^n$ so that squared Euclidean distance equals effective resistance.
  \item \emph{Random walks}: in an unweighted graph, the expected round-trip walk time (commute time, Chapter~35) between $s$ and $t$ equals $2m\,R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)$.
  \item \emph{Spanning trees}: by Kirchhoff's matrix-tree theorem (Chapter~31), an edge's leverage score $w_e R_{\mathrm{eff}}(e)$ equals the exact probability that $e$ belongs to a random spanning tree chosen with probability proportional to $\prod_{f \in T} w_f$.
\end{itemize}
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Solving \texorpdfstring{$\Lap x = b$}{Lx=b} in nearly linear time}\label{ch41:solve}

Dense Gaussian elimination takes $\Oh(n^3)$ time and destroys graph sparsity: eliminating a vertex connects all of its remaining neighbors into a clique, introducing new nonzero entries called \emph{fill-in}. To solve $\Lap x = b$ in $\widetilde{\Oh}(m \log(1/\eps))$ time instead, we combine two ingredients: an \emph{iterative} linear solver whose step count depends on the condition number of the matrix, and an easy-to-invert \emph{preconditioner} built from a spanning tree plus a small sample of off-tree edges.

\subsection{Preconditioned conjugate gradient}\label{ch41:pcg}

On the zero-mean subspace $\mathbf{1}^\perp$ of a connected graph, $\Lap$ is symmetric positive definite (SPD), with condition number $\kappa(\Lap)=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ over its $n-1$ positive eigenvalues. Measure approximation error in the \emph{$\Lap$-norm} (or \emph{energy norm}), defined by
\[
  \norm{y}_{\Lap} \;=\; \sqrt{y^\top \Lap y} \;=\; \sqrt{\sum_{e=\{u,v\}} w_e\,(y_u - y_v)^2} .
\]
Because $\norm{\tilde{x} - x^\star}_{\Lap}^2 = \mathcal{E}(\tilde{f} - f^\star)$, small relative error in the $\Lap$-norm guarantees small error in electrical flow energy. Unpreconditioned \emph{conjugate gradient} (CG, Chapter~31) solves $\Lap x=b$ to relative $\Lap$-norm error $\eps$ in $\Oh\bigl(\sqrt{\kappa(\Lap)}\,\log(1/\eps)\bigr)$ sparse matrix--vector products, each costing $\Oh(m)$ time. On ill-conditioned graphs $\kappa(\Lap)$ can be $\poly(n)$ --- a path on $n$ vertices has $\kappa(\Lap)=\Th(n^2)$, costing $\Th(n)$ iterations --- so plain CG is not near-linear.

Preconditioning transforms $\Lap x = b$ on $\mathbf{1}^\perp$ into the equivalent system $\mathbf{P}^{+}\Lap x = \mathbf{P}^{+}b$ using another symmetric matrix $\mathbf{P} \succeq 0$ (sharing the null space $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{1}\}$) for which solving $\mathbf{P} z = r$ is fast and $\mathbf{P}$ approximates $\Lap$ on every vector's quadratic form. Write $\mathbf{A} \preceq \mathbf{B}$ (the \emph{Loewner order}) when $x^\top \mathbf{A} x \le x^\top \mathbf{B} x$ for all $x \in \R^n$.

\begin{definition}[Spectral approximation]\label{ch41:def-spectral}
Symmetric positive semidefinite matrices $\mathbf{P},\Lap\succeq 0$ satisfy $\mathbf{P}\approx_\kappa \Lap$ for a factor $\kappa \ge 1$ when $\tfrac{1}{\kappa}\mathbf{P} \preceq \Lap \preceq \kappa\mathbf{P}$, that is,
\[
  \tfrac{1}{\kappa}\,x^\top \mathbf{P}\,x \;\le\; x^\top \Lap\,x \;\le\; \kappa\,x^\top \mathbf{P}\,x
  \qquad\text{for all } x \in \R^n .
\]
\end{definition}

When $\mathbf{P}\approx_\kappa\Lap$, every nonzero eigenvalue of $\mathbf{P}^{+}\Lap$ on $\mathbf{1}^\perp$ lies in $[1/\kappa,\kappa]$, bounding its condition number $c = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ by $\kappa^2$. When we have a one-sided bound $\mathbf{P}\preceq\Lap\preceq\kappa\mathbf{P}$ (or $\Lap\preceq\mathbf{P}\preceq\kappa\Lap$), as holds for the tree and ultrasparsifier preconditioners below, the eigenvalues lie in an interval of ratio $\kappa$, so $c \le \kappa$. Preconditioned conjugate gradient (PCG, Algorithm~\ref{ch41:alg-pcg}) maintains the current residual $r = b - \Lap x$ (unmet demand), the preconditioned residual $z = \mathbf{P}^{+}r$, and a search direction $p$, reaching relative $\Lap$-norm error $\eps$ in $\Oh(\sqrt{c}\,\log(1/\eps))$ iterations. Each iteration performs one multiplication by $\Lap$ in $\Oh(m)$ time plus one linear solve $\mathbf{P} z = r_{\mathrm{new}}$. Our goal is therefore to build $\mathbf{P}$ with small relative condition number $c \le \kappa$ and fast solves.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\DontPrintSemicolon
\caption{\textsc{PreconditionedSolve}$(\Lap,b,\mathbf{P},\eps)$}\label{ch41:alg-pcg}
\KwIn{Laplacian $\Lap$, demand $b\perp\mathbf{1}$, preconditioner $\mathbf{P}$ (with $\ker\mathbf{P}=\ker\Lap$), condition-number bound $c$ on $\mathbf{P}^{+}\Lap$ ($c\le\kappa$ if $\mathbf{P}\preceq\Lap\preceq\kappa\mathbf{P}$, or $c\le\kappa^2$ if $\mathbf{P}\approx_\kappa\Lap$), accuracy $\eps$.}
\KwOut{$x$ with $\norm{x-\Lap^{+}b}_{\Lap}\le\eps\norm{\Lap^{+}b}_{\Lap}$.}
$x\gets 0$;\quad $r\gets b$;\quad $z\gets \textsc{Solve}_{\mathbf{P}}(r)$;\quad $p\gets z$\;
\For(\tcp*[h]{CG bound: error shrinks by $2e^{-2k/\sqrt c}$ in $\Lap$-norm}){$k=1$ \KwTo $\lceil\tfrac12\sqrt{c}\,\ln(2/\eps)\rceil$}{
  $q\gets \Lap p$\;
  $\alpha\gets (r^\top z)/(p^\top q)$\;
  $x\gets x+\alpha p$;\quad $r_{\mathrm{new}}\gets r-\alpha q$\;
  $z_{\mathrm{new}}\gets \textsc{Solve}_{\mathbf{P}}(r_{\mathrm{new}})$\;
  $\beta\gets (r_{\mathrm{new}}^\top z_{\mathrm{new}})/(r^\top z)$\;
  $p\gets z_{\mathrm{new}}+\beta p$;\quad $r\gets r_{\mathrm{new}}$;\quad $z\gets z_{\mathrm{new}}$\;
}
\Return $x$\;
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Low-stretch spanning trees: the first preconditioner}\label{ch41:lsst}

The simplest preconditioner is the Laplacian $\Lap_T$ of a spanning tree $T \subseteq \Gph$. Solving $\Lap_T z = r$ takes $\Oh(n)$ time without ever forming a matrix inverse. Root $T$ at an arbitrary vertex (pinning $z_{\mathrm{root}} = 0$) and orient every tree edge toward the root: when vertices are ordered from leaves up to the root, the grounded tree incidence matrix $\mathbf{B}_T$ is triangular, so $\Lap_T z = \mathbf{B}_T^\top \mathbf{W}_T \mathbf{B}_T z = r$ splits into two linear-time passes (Figure~\ref{ch41:fig-tree-solve}):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Upward pass (leaves to root):} solve $\mathbf{B}_T^\top f = r$. Each leaf $u$ has only one incident tree edge $e=(u\to \mathrm{parent}(u))$, forcing $f_e = r_u$; peel off $u$, add $f_e$ into $\mathrm{parent}(u)$'s demand, and repeat up to the root.
  \item \textbf{Downward pass (root to leaves):} solve $\mathbf{W}_T \mathbf{B}_T z = f$. Starting from $z_{\mathrm{root}} = 0$, walk down each tree edge $e=(u\to \mathrm{parent}(u))$ setting $z_u = z_{\mathrm{parent}(u)} + r_e f_e$.
\end{enumerate}

Now we ask how accurately $\Lap_T$ approximates $\Lap$. Since $T\subseteq\Gph$, dropping non-tree edges only removes nonnegative terms from $x^\top \Lap x$, giving $\Lap_T\preceq\Lap$. Bounding $x^\top \Lap x$ from above by a multiple of $x^\top \Lap_T x$ depends on how far each missing edge detours through $T$, measured by \emph{stretch}.

\begin{definition}[Stretch]\label{ch41:def-stretch}
For an edge $e=\{u,v\} \in E$ of resistance $r_e$, let $P_T(u,v)$ be the unique path between $u$ and $v$ in $T$. The \emph{stretch} of $e$ with respect to $T$ is the ratio of its tree-path resistance to its own resistance:
\[
  \operatorname{st}_T(e) \;=\; \frac{\sum_{f\in P_T(u,v)} r_f}{r_e} .
\]
Each tree edge $e \in T$ has $P_T(u,v) = \{e\}$ and $\operatorname{st}_T(e) = 1$. The \emph{total stretch} of $T$ is $\operatorname{st}_T(\Gph) = \sum_{e\in E}\operatorname{st}_T(e)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Tree preconditioning bound]\label{ch41:lem-stretch}
For any spanning tree $T$ of $\Gph$, $\Lap_T\preceq\Lap\preceq \operatorname{st}_T(\Gph)\,\Lap_T$, so the preconditioned operator $\Lap_T^{+}\Lap$ on $\mathbf{1}^\perp$ has condition number at most $\operatorname{st}_T(\Gph)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{whybox}
Fix an edge $e=\{u,v\}$ with tree path $P=P_T(u,v)$, and write $\Delta_f x$ for the potential difference across a tree edge $f \in P$. Telescoping along $P$ gives $x_u - x_v = \sum_{f \in P} \pm \Delta_f x$. Applying the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality $\bigl(\sum_i a_i b_i\bigr)^2 \le \bigl(\sum_i a_i^2\bigr)\bigl(\sum_i b_i^2\bigr)$ with $a_f = \sqrt{r_f}$ and $b_f = \pm \Delta_f x / \sqrt{r_f}$ yields
\begin{align*}
  (x_u-x_v)^2 \;=\; \biggl(\sum_{f\in P}\sqrt{r_f}\cdot\frac{\pm\Delta_f x}{\sqrt{r_f}}\biggr)^2
  &\;\le\; \biggl(\sum_{f\in P}r_f\biggr)\sum_{f\in P}\frac{(\Delta_f x)^2}{r_f} \\
  &\;\le\; \biggl(\sum_{f\in P}r_f\biggr)\,x^\top\Lap_T x .
\end{align*}
Dividing both sides by $r_e$ gives $\frac{(x_u-x_v)^2}{r_e}\le\operatorname{st}_T(e)\,x^\top\Lap_T x$. Summing over all $e \in E$ produces $x^\top\Lap x\le\operatorname{st}_T(\Gph)\,x^\top\Lap_T x$. Physically, a low-resistance shortcut whose tree detour has large total resistance inflates the bound; sampling off-tree edges proportional to $\operatorname{st}_T(e)$ in Section~\ref{ch41:ultra} repairs exactly those high-stretch detours.
\end{whybox}

\begin{labbox}
Compute stretch on the running network (Figure~\ref{ch41:fig-tree-stretch}, left). Take the spanning tree $T=\{e_1,e_2,e_4,e_6\}=\{\{s,a\},\{s,b\},\{b,c\},\{c,t\}\}$. Every edge has $r_e = 1$, and the two non-tree edges are $e_3=\{a,c\}$ and $e_5=\{a,b\}$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $e_5=\{a,b\}$: tree path $a\to s\to b$ uses two unit resistors, so $\operatorname{st}_T(e_5)=2/1=2$.
  \item $e_3=\{a,c\}$: tree path $a\to s\to b\to c$ uses three unit resistors, so $\operatorname{st}_T(e_3)=3/1=3$.
\end{itemize}
Adding $1$ for each of the four tree edges gives $\operatorname{st}_T(\Gph)=4+2+3=9$, so Lemma~\ref{ch41:lem-stretch} bounds the preconditioned condition number by $9$. Trying $T'=\{e_1,e_3,e_4,e_6\}$ leaves non-tree edges $e_2=\{s,b\}$ (path $s\to a\to c\to b$, stretch $3$) and $e_5=\{a,b\}$ (path $a\to c\to b$, stretch $2$), giving $\operatorname{st}_{T'}(\Gph)=4+3+2=9$ again. On this five-vertex graph symmetry ties the two trees, whereas on a long ladder or grid (Figure~\ref{ch41:fig-tree-stretch}, right) a poorly chosen tree stretches cross-edges across the entire graph.
\end{labbox}

An arbitrary breadth-first or shortest-path tree can have total stretch $\Th(mn)$ (for instance $\Th(n^2)$ on grids or ladders). Every weighted graph, however, has a spanning tree whose average stretch per edge is polylogarithmic.

\begin{theorem}[Low-stretch spanning trees]\label{ch41:thm-lsst}
Every weighted graph $\Gph$ has a spanning tree $T$ with $\operatorname{st}_T(\Gph)=\Oh(m\log n\log\log n)$, constructible in $\Oh(m\log n\log\log n)$ time. (Alon, Karp, Peleg, and West first achieved $m^{1+o(1)}$; Abraham, Bartal, and Neiman and Abraham and Neiman obtained the near-linear bound.)
\end{theorem}

Such trees are built by recursively partitioning the graph into clusters of small shortest-path diameter joined radially to a central cluster (a \emph{star decomposition}), ensuring that few edges cross cluster boundaries with long detours. Plugging this tree into Lemma~\ref{ch41:lem-stretch} gives condition number $c \le \Oh(m\log n\log\log n)$, so PCG takes $\Oh(\sqrt{m}\,\poly(\log n)\log(1/\eps))$ iterations --- $\widetilde{\Oh}(m^{3/2})$ total time, still a factor of $\sqrt{m}$ away from near-linear. To remove the $\sqrt{m}$, we add a small sample of high-stretch off-tree edges back to $T$ and recurse.

\subsection{From a tree to an ultrasparsifier}\label{ch41:ultra}

Spielman and Teng (streamlined by Koutis, Miller, and Peng) bridge the $\sqrt{m}$ gap by building an \emph{ultrasparsifier}: start with a low-stretch spanning tree $T$ (scaled up slightly so $H$ dominates $\Gph$), then randomly \emph{sample} $t$ non-tree edges with probability $p_e$ proportional to their stretch $\operatorname{st}_T(e)$, dividing each sampled edge's conductance by its sampling probability so its expected conductance remains $w_e$ (unbiased). Adding $t$ sampled edges yields a sparse graph $H$ with $n - 1 + t$ edges whose relative condition number against $\Lap$ shrinks in proportion to $1/t$.

\begin{theorem}[Ultrasparsifier]\label{ch41:thm-ultra}
For any $\kappa > 1$, from a spanning tree $T$ one can construct in near-linear time a graph $H$ with
\[
  n - 1 + \Oh\!\left(\frac{\operatorname{st}_T(\Gph)}{\kappa}\log n\right) \text{ edges}
  \qquad\text{satisfying}\qquad
  \Lap \;\preceq\; \Lap_H \;\preceq\; \kappa\,\Lap .
\]
\end{theorem}

Because $H$ is a tree plus $t = \Oh\bigl((\operatorname{st}_T(\Gph)/\kappa)\log n\bigr)$ extra edges, solving $\Lap_H z = r$ does not require solving on all $n$ vertices. Instead, we repeatedly eliminate vertices of degree $1$ and $2$ (a partial Cholesky factorization --- symmetric Gaussian elimination --- that introduces zero fill-in, illustrated in Figure~\ref{ch41:fig-ultrasparsifier-elim}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item A \emph{degree-$1$ leaf} $u$ attached to neighbor $v$ must send all of its demand $r_u$ across edge $\{u,v\}$, fixing its current immediately and folding $r_u$ into $r_v$.
  \item A \emph{degree-$2$ vertex} $v$ between neighbors $u$ and $w$ (after folding $v$'s demand proportionally onto $u$ and $w$) acts as two resistors in series, collapsing $\{u,v\}$ and $\{v,w\}$ into one edge $\{u,w\}$ of resistance $r_{uv} + r_{vw}$ without adding any new edges elsewhere.
\end{itemize}
Pruning all leaves and contracting all degree-$2$ paths reduces $H$ to a core multigraph $\Gph'$ whose vertices all have degree at least $3$ in the contracted off-tree skeleton, bounding $\Gph'$ to $\Oh(t)$ vertices and edges.

Now balance the recursion cost. With $\Lap \preceq \Lap_H \preceq \kappa\Lap$, PCG calls the recursive solver on $\Gph'$ only $\Oh(\sqrt{\kappa})$ times (for fixed constant inner accuracy, e.g.\ $\eps_0 = 1/2$). Since $\operatorname{st}_T(\Gph) = \widetilde{\Oh}(m)$, the core $\Gph'$ has $m' = \widetilde{\Oh}(m/\kappa)$ edges. Choosing $\kappa = \log^{c_1} n$ for a large enough constant $c_1$ ensures that the work multiplier per level satisfies
\[
  \Oh(\sqrt{\kappa}) \cdot \frac{m'}{m} \;=\; \frac{\widetilde{\Oh}(1)}{\sqrt{\kappa}} \;\le\; \frac{1}{2} .
\]
Total work halves at each of the $\Oh(\log n)$ recursion levels, summing geometrically to near-linear time.

\begin{complexitybox}
Choosing $\kappa$ so that PCG takes $\Oh(\sqrt\kappa)$ inner steps while the core shrinks by more than $2\sqrt{\kappa}$ at each level solves $\Lap x = b$ to relative $\Lap$-norm error $\eps$ in time
\[
  \Oh\!\bigl(m\,\log^{c} n\,\log(1/\eps)\bigr) \;=\; \widetilde{\Oh}\bigl(m\log(1/\eps)\bigr)
\]
for a small constant $c$. Spielman and Teng's 2004 algorithm had a large exponent $c$; Koutis, Miller, and Peng reduced $c$ to roughly $1$--$2$, and subsequent work (Kyng and Sachdeva's sparsified Cholesky, among others) obtained $c < 1$ using purely random sampling during Gaussian elimination without low-stretch trees. From here on, treat $\textsc{LaplacianSolve}$ as a subroutine returning $\tilde{x}$ with $\norm{\tilde{x}-\Lap^{+}b}_{\Lap}\le\eps\norm{\Lap^{+}b}_{\Lap}$ in $\widetilde{\Oh}(m\log(1/\eps))$ time.
\end{complexitybox}

\begin{trapbox}
Two subtleties arise when calling an approximate Laplacian solver in code:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Null-space drift}: $\Lap x = b$ has a solution only when $\mathbf{1}^\top b = 0$, and $x$ is determined only up to adding a constant multiple of $\mathbf{1}$. Floating-point rounding can introduce a small nonzero mean $\bar{b} = \frac{1}{n}(\mathbf{1}^\top b)\mathbf{1}$; always subtract $\bar{b}$ from $b$ before solving, or CG will fail to converge.
  \item \textbf{Relative $\Lap$-norm error}: the guarantee $\norm{\tilde{x}-\Lap^{+}b}_{\Lap}\le\eps\norm{\Lap^{+}b}_{\Lap}$ bounds total energy error, not worst-case coordinate error, and $\tilde{f} = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{B}\tilde{x}$ only approximately conserves flow ($\mathbf{B}^\top\tilde{f} \approx b$). When chaining many solves (as in maximum flow), set $\eps = 1/\poly(n)$ --- since $\log(1/\eps) = \Oh(\log n)$, high precision adds only a logarithmic factor --- and route any tiny residual demand $b - \mathbf{B}^\top\tilde{f}$ along a spanning tree if exact conservation is needed.
\end{enumerate}
\end{trapbox}

\section{Spectral sparsification}\label{ch41:sparsify}

Whereas an ultrasparsifier keeps a fixed spanning tree intact, a \emph{spectral sparsifier} replaces $\Gph$ (with $m$ edges) by a reweighted subgraph $\widetilde\Gph$ with only $\Oh(n\log n/\eps^2)$ --- or even $\Oh(n/\eps^2)$ --- edges satisfying $\Lap_{\widetilde\Gph}\approx_{1+\eps}\Lap$. In particular, testing $x = \mathbf{1}_S$ (the $0$--$1$ indicator vector of any vertex set $S \subseteq V$) shows that $\widetilde\Gph$ preserves the weight of every cut in $\Gph$ within $1 \pm \eps$. Sparsifying $\Gph$ once lets downstream cut and flow algorithms run on $\widetilde{\Oh}(n/\eps^2)$ edges instead of $m$.

\begin{theorem}[Spielman--Srivastava: sample by effective resistance]\label{ch41:thm-ss}
Draw $q=\Oh(n\log n/\eps^2)$ edges independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) with replacement from $E$, choosing edge $e$ on each draw with probability
\[
  p_e \;=\; \frac{w_e R_{\mathrm{eff}}(e)}{n-1}
\]
proportional to its leverage score $\tau_e = w_e R_{\mathrm{eff}}(e)$, and adding weight $w_e/(q\,p_e)$ to $e$ in $\widetilde\Gph$ each time $e$ is drawn. With high probability ($1 - n^{-\Om(1)}$), the resulting sparse graph $\widetilde\Gph$ satisfies $\Lap_{\widetilde\Gph}\approx_{1+\eps}\Lap$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
For each oriented edge $e=(u\to v)$, let $b_e = e_u - e_v \in \R^n$ be row $e$ of $\mathbf{B}$ written as a column vector, so $\Lap=\mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{B}=\sum_{e \in E} w_e\,b_e b_e^\top$ is a sum of rank-one matrices. Let $\Lap^{+/2}$ be the symmetric square root of $\Lap^{+}$ (which keeps $\Lap$'s eigenvectors, replaces each positive eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ by $1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}$, and sends $\mathbf{1}$ to $0$). Multiplying on both sides by $\Lap^{+/2}$ gives
\begin{align*}
  \Pi \;=\; \Lap^{+/2}\Lap\Lap^{+/2} &\;=\; \sum_{e \in E} v_e v_e^\top,
    \qquad v_e = \sqrt{w_e}\,\Lap^{+/2}b_e, \\
  \norm{v_e}_2^2 \;=\; w_e\,b_e^\top\Lap^{+}b_e &\;=\; w_e R_{\mathrm{eff}}(e) \;=\; \tau_e .
\end{align*}
Because $\Pi$ acts as the identity on the $(n-1)$-dimensional subspace $\mathbf{1}^\perp$ and sends $\mathbf{1}$ to $0$, it is an orthogonal projection of rank $n-1$. Its trace (sum of diagonal entries, equal to the sum of its eigenvalues) therefore satisfies
\[
  \sum_{e \in E} w_e R_{\mathrm{eff}}(e) \;=\; \sum_{e \in E} \norm{v_e}_2^2 \;=\; \operatorname{tr}(\Pi) \;=\; n - 1 ,
\]
confirming that $p_e = w_e R_{\mathrm{eff}}(e)/(n-1)$ is a valid probability distribution on $E$. Sparsifying $\Lap$ reduces to approximating $\Pi = \sum_e v_e v_e^\top$ within $(1\pm\eps)\Pi$ by averaging $q$ i.i.d.\ rank-one terms $\frac{1}{p_e} v_e v_e^\top$, each bounded in norm by $\frac{1}{p_e}\norm{v_e}_2^2 = n-1$. A \emph{matrix Chernoff bound} (which extends the scalar Chernoff bound of Chapter~34 to sums of independent random positive semidefinite matrices) proves concentration around $\Pi$ using $q = \Oh(n\log n/\eps^2)$ draws.
\end{whybox}

Computing all $m$ effective resistances one by one would take $m$ Laplacian solves --- $\widetilde{\Oh}(m^2)$ time. Instead, we use a \emph{Johnson--Lindenstrauss} (JL) random projection (Chapter~35). Using $\Lap^{+}\Lap\Lap^{+} = \Lap^{+}$ and $\Lap = \mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{B}$, rewrite each edge's effective resistance as the squared Euclidean length of an $m$-dimensional vector:
\[
  R_{\mathrm{eff}}(e) \;=\; b_e^\top \Lap^{+} b_e \;=\; b_e^\top \Lap^{+}\mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{B}\Lap^{+} b_e \;=\; \norm{\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\mathbf{B}\,\Lap^{+} b_e}_2^2 .
\]
Let $\mathbf{Q} \in \R^{k \times m}$ be a random matrix with $k = \Oh(\log n / \eps^2)$ rows and independent $\pm 1/\sqrt{k}$ entries. By the Johnson--Lindenstrauss lemma, multiplying $\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\mathbf{B}\Lap^{+}$ on the left by $\mathbf{Q}$ preserves the squared Euclidean norm of every column $b_e = e_u - e_v$ within $1 \pm \eps$ with high probability. Computing the $k \times n$ matrix $\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\mathbf{B}\Lap^{+}$ requires only $k = \Oh(\log n / \eps^2)$ Laplacian solves (one solve $\Lap z_j = (\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\mathbf{B})_j^\top$ per row of $\mathbf{Q}$), after which reading off $\norm{\mathbf{Z}(\cdot, u) - \mathbf{Z}(\cdot, v)}_2^2$ gives $(1\pm\eps)$-accurate leverage scores for every edge $e=\{u,v\}$ in $\Oh(k)$ time per edge, or $\widetilde{\Oh}(m/\eps^2)$ total time.

\begin{connectionbox}
Solvers and sparsifiers reinforce each other: even a slow solver gives enough leverage-score accuracy to sample a spectral sparsifier, and sparsifying intermediate graphs speeds up the solver itself. Batson, Spielman, and Srivastava (2009) later removed the $\log n$ factor from Theorem~\ref{ch41:thm-ss}, proving that every graph has a $(1\pm\eps)$-spectral sparsifier with $\Oh(n/\eps^2)$ edges by deterministically adding edges one at a time while keeping the extreme eigenvalues of the sum bounded by smooth potential functions. Later work extended these sampling and solver techniques from quadratic ($\ell_2$) flows to general $\ell_p$-norm objectives.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Maximum flow via a sequence of electrical flows}\label{ch41:maxflow}

We now approximate maximum $s$--$t$ flow in an \emph{undirected, capacitated} graph $\Gph = (V,E)$ using only electrical-flow computations, each reduced to one Laplacian solve. Every edge $e$ has a capacity $u_e>0$, and we seek the largest value $F$ for which $F$ units of flow can be routed from $s$ to $t$ while obeying $\abs{f_e}\le u_e$ on every edge. We present the 2011 algorithm of Christiano, Kelner, Madry, Spielman, and Teng (CKMST), which computes a $(1-\eps)$-approximate maximum flow in $\Oh\bigl(m^{4/3}\,\poly(\eps^{-1},\log n)\bigr)$ time --- the first undirected max-flow algorithm to break the $\Oh(m^{3/2})$ barrier on sparse graphs, and the prototype for later almost-linear-time flow solvers.

\subsection{Why electrical flow is the right relaxation}\label{ch41:why-elec}

A capacity-respecting flow must bound its worst edge load: $\max_e \abs{f_e}/u_e \le 1$, an \emph{$\ell_\infty$ constraint}. An electrical flow instead minimizes a weighted sum of squared edge loads $\sum_e r_e f_e^2$, an \emph{$\ell_2$ objective} solvable by a single linear system. To bridge $\ell_2$ and $\ell_\infty$, we use the \emph{multiplicative weights update} (MWU) method: maintain a positive penalty weight $w_e$ on each edge, solve the $\ell_2$ electrical problem under resistances proportional to $w_e$, and multiply each weight $w_e$ by a factor that grows with edge $e$'s overload $\rho_e = \abs{f_e}/u_e$. Edges that are repeatedly overloaded grow exponentially more resistive, forcing later electrical flows onto underused paths; Figure~\ref{ch41:fig-mwu-reroute} traces this shift on a two-path network.

\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think}: Why can't a single electrical flow solve max-flow directly? An electrical flow minimizes total $\ell_2$ energy $\sum r_e f_e^2$, treating an edge that carries twice its capacity as paying four times the energy rather than hitting a hard wall. Because it has no hard ceiling enforcing $\abs{f_e} \le u_e$, we must iterate, penalizing overloaded edges until the averaged flow respects every capacity.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{The multiplicative-weights outer loop}\label{ch41:mwu}

Using binary search over $F$, it suffices to solve the decision problem: either find a flow routing $F$ units with congestion $\rho_e = \abs{f_e}/u_e \le 1 + \Oh(\eps)$ on every edge (then dividing the flow by $1 + \Oh(\eps)$ makes it strictly feasible with value $(1 - \Oh(\eps))F$), or certify that no feasible flow of value $F$ exists.

Maintain edge weights $w_e \ge 1$ (initialized to $1$) with total weight $W = \sum_{e \in E} w_e$, and set each edge's resistance to
\[
  r_e \;=\; \frac{w_e + \frac{\eps}{m}W}{u_e^2} .
\]
Dividing by $u_e^2$ expresses energy in units of squared congestion: $r_e f_e^2 = (w_e + \frac{\eps}{m}W)\rho_e^2$, so an edge at full capacity ($\rho_e = 1$) contributes $w_e + \frac{\eps}{m}W$ to total energy. Adding the small floor $\frac{\eps}{m}W$ prevents any edge's resistance from becoming negligible compared to $W$, which caps the worst single-round congestion $\rho_e$ by $\mu = \sqrt{(1+\eps)m/\eps}$ whenever the round's energy test passes. Each round computes the electrical flow $f^{(i)}$ routing $F$ units under resistances $r$, checks whether $\mathcal{E}(f^{(i)}) > (1+\eps)W$, and otherwise updates $w_e \gets w_e(1 + \frac{\eps}{\mu}\rho_e)$.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\DontPrintSemicolon
\caption{\textsc{ElectricalMaxFlow}$(\Gph,u,s,t,F,\eps)$}\label{ch41:alg-maxflow}
\KwIn{Undirected graph $\Gph$, capacities $u$, source/sink $s,t$, target value $F$, accuracy $\eps \in (0, 1/2)$.}
\KwOut{A flow of value $F$ with congestion $\le 1+\Oh(\eps)$, or a certificate that $F$ exceeds the max-flow.}
$w_e\gets 1$ for all $e \in E$;\quad $\bar f\gets 0$;\quad $\mu \gets \sqrt{(1+\eps)m/\eps}$;\quad $N\gets \lceil 2\mu\,\eps^{-2}\ln m \rceil$\;
\For{$i=1$ \KwTo $N$}{
  $W\gets \sum_{e \in E} w_e$;\quad $r_e\gets (w_e+\tfrac{\eps}{m}W)/u_e^2$ for all $e \in E$\;
  $x\gets \textsc{LaplacianSolve}(\Lap_r,\, F(e_s-e_t),\, \eps_{\mathrm{sol}})$ \tcp*{$\Lap_r=\mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{B}$, $\eps_{\mathrm{sol}} = 1/\poly(n)$}
  $f^{(i)}_e\gets (x_u-x_v)/r_e$ for each $e=(u\to v)$ \tcp*{electrical flow routing $F$}
  \If{$\mathcal{E}(f^{(i)})=\sum_{e \in E} r_e (f^{(i)}_e)^2 > (1+\eps)\,W$}{
     \Return ``$F$ is infeasible'' \tcp*{energy exceeds every feasible flow's ceiling}
  }
  $\rho_e\gets \abs{f^{(i)}_e}/u_e$ for all $e \in E$ \tcp*{energy test guarantees $\rho_e \le \mu$}
  $w_e\gets w_e\,\bigl(1+\tfrac{\eps}{\mu}\,\rho_e\bigr)$ for all $e \in E$\;
  $\bar f\gets \bar f + \tfrac1N f^{(i)}$\;
}
\Return $\bar f$ \tcp*{average flow: value $F$, congestion $\le 1+\Oh(\eps)$}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{whybox}
Two inequalities make Algorithm~\ref{ch41:alg-maxflow} work:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Electrical energy lower-bounds feasibility.} Because $f^{(i)}$ minimizes $\mathcal{E}$ among all flows routing $F$, any capacity-respecting flow $g$ of value $F$ ($\abs{g_e} \le u_e$, so $g_e^2/u_e^2 \le 1$) must satisfy
  \[
    \mathcal{E}(f^{(i)}) \;\le\; \mathcal{E}(g) \;=\; \sum_{e \in E} \Bigl(w_e + \frac{\eps}{m}W\Bigr)\frac{g_e^2}{u_e^2} \;\le\; \sum_{e \in E} \Bigl(w_e + \frac{\eps}{m}W\Bigr) \;=\; (1+\eps)W .
  \]
  If $\mathcal{E}(f^{(i)}) > (1+\eps)W$, no feasible flow of value $F$ can exist.
  \item \textbf{Multiplicative weights regret bound.} Whenever the energy check passes, Cauchy--Schwarz gives $\sum_e w_e \rho_e^{(i)} \le \sqrt{W \sum_e w_e (\rho_e^{(i)})^2} \le \sqrt{W \cdot \mathcal{E}(f^{(i)})} \le (1+\eps)W$, so the $w$-weighted average congestion per round is at most $1+\eps$. Using $1 + z \le e^z$ for the upper bound on total weight $W$ and $1 + z \ge \exp(z - z^2)$ (valid for $0 \le z = \frac{\eps}{\mu}\rho_e^{(i)} \le \eps < \frac{1}{2}$) for each edge weight $w_e$ gives
  \begin{align*}
    \exp\biggl((1-\eps)\frac{\eps}{\mu}\sum_{i=1}^N \rho_e^{(i)}\biggr)
    \;\le\; w_e^{(N+1)} \;\le\; W^{(N+1)} \;\le\; m \exp\biggl(N\,\frac{\eps(1+\eps)}{\mu}\biggr) .
  \end{align*}
  Taking logarithms and dividing by $N\frac{\eps}{\mu}$ bounds every edge's average congestion $\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \rho_e^{(i)}$ --- and hence $\abs{\bar{f}_e}/u_e$, by the triangle inequality --- by $1 + \Oh(\eps)$ once $N \ge 2\mu\,\eps^{-2}\ln m$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{whybox}

\subsection{The width problem and the \texorpdfstring{$m^{1/3}$}{m^1/3} fix}\label{ch41:width}

Algorithm~\ref{ch41:alg-maxflow} as written needs $N = \Th(\mu\,\eps^{-2}\log m)$ rounds, where the \emph{width} $\mu$ bounds the largest single-round congestion $\max_e \rho_e$. Without further modification, $\max_e \rho_e$ can reach $\Th(\sqrt{m})$ even when $F$ is feasible (Exercise~\ref{ch41:ex-width} constructs an explicit example: one short edge in parallel with $\sqrt{m}$ paths of length $\sqrt{m}$), forcing $\Th(\sqrt{m}\,\eps^{-2}\log m)$ solves and $\widetilde{\Oh}(m^{3/2})$ total time.

CKMST reduce the width from $\sqrt{m}$ to a chosen threshold $\rho^\star \ll \sqrt{m}$ by \emph{deleting} any edge whose electrical congestion exceeds $\rho^\star$ and recomputing the electrical flow:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Normal MWU rounds}: when every edge satisfies $\rho_e \le \rho^\star$, we can use $\mu = \rho^\star$, shrinking the number of MWU rounds to $\widetilde{\Oh}(\rho^\star \eps^{-2})$ and their total solver cost to $\widetilde{\Oh}(m \rho^\star \eps^{-2})$.
  \item \textbf{Edge-deletion steps}: in any round with $\mathcal{E}(f) \le (1+\eps)W$, an edge with $\rho_e > \rho^\star$ alone dissipates at least $\frac{\eps}{m}W (\rho^\star)^2$ energy. Across the entire run (charging against edge resistances), at most $\widetilde{\Oh}\bigl(m\,\eps^{-\Oh(1)} / (\rho^\star)^2\bigr)$ edges can ever exceed $\rho^\star$, and deleting them reduces the maximum flow capacity by at most an $\Oh(\eps)$ fraction because high-energy edges lie across near-minimum cuts. Each deletion triggers one fresh $\widetilde{\Oh}(m)$ solve, costing $\widetilde{\Oh}\bigl(m^2 / (\rho^\star)^2\bigr)$ total time.
\end{itemize}
Balancing normal-round work against deletion work (Figure~\ref{ch41:fig-width-tradeoff}) sets
\[
  m\,\rho^\star \;=\; \frac{m^2}{(\rho^\star)^2}
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  (\rho^\star)^3 \;=\; m
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \rho^\star \;=\; m^{1/3},
\]
yielding $\widetilde{\Oh}(m^{1/3}\eps^{-\Oh(1)})$ Laplacian solves and $\widetilde{\Oh}(m^{4/3}\eps^{-\Oh(1)})$ total running time.

\begin{theorem}[CKMST]\label{ch41:thm-ckmst}
Algorithm~\ref{ch41:alg-maxflow}, augmented with width reduction (deleting edges whose electrical congestion exceeds $\rho^\star = m^{1/3}$ and re-solving), computes a $(1-\eps)$-approximate maximum $s$--$t$ flow in an undirected capacitated graph in time
\[
  \Oh\bigl(m^{4/3}\,\poly(\eps^{-1},\log m)\bigr),
\]
using $\Oh\bigl(m^{1/3}\,\poly(\eps^{-1},\log m)\bigr)$ Laplacian solves.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By the two invariants in the box above, whenever no edge exceeds $\rho^\star$, $\Th(\rho^\star \eps^{-2}\log m)$ MWU rounds either certify infeasibility or produce an averaged flow of congestion at most $1 + \Oh(\eps)$. In any round passing the energy check $\mathcal{E}(f) = \sum_e r_e f_e^2 \le (1+\eps)W$, an edge of congestion $\rho_e$ contributes
\[
  r_e f_e^2 \;=\; \Bigl(w_e + \frac{\eps}{m}W\Bigr)\rho_e^2 \;\ge\; \frac{\eps}{m}W\,\rho_e^2 ,
\]
which first implies $\rho_e \le \sqrt{(1+\eps)m/\eps}$ unconditionally, and second shows that each deleted edge ($\rho_e > \rho^\star$) carries at least an $\frac{\eps (\rho^\star)^2}{(1+\eps)m}$ fraction of the entire network's electrical energy $\mathcal{E}(f)$. Tracking how edge deletions raise the effective resistance $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)$ bounds the total number of deletions before $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)$ exceeds the feasibility threshold by $\widetilde{\Oh}\bigl(m\,\eps^{-\Oh(1)} / (\rho^\star)^2\bigr)$, while losing at most an $\Oh(\eps)$ fraction of $s$--$t$ cut capacity across all deletions. Setting $\rho^\star = m^{1/3}$ equates $\rho^\star$ and $m/(\rho^\star)^2$ at $m^{1/3}$ solves, each costing $\widetilde{\Oh}(m)$ time.
\end{proof}

Trace two rounds on the running network (Figure~\ref{ch41:fig-maxflow-running}) with unit capacities $u_e=1$ and target $F=1$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Round 1}: $w_e=1$ for all six edges, so $W=6$ and $r_e = 1 + \frac{\eps}{6}\cdot 6 = 1+\eps$. Scaling resistances uniformly leaves the unit electrical flow of Section~\ref{ch41:elec} unchanged: congestions are $\rho_{sa}=\rho_{sb}=\rho_{ac}=\rho_{bc}=\tfrac{1}{2}$, $\rho_{ab}=0$, and $\rho_{ct}=1$. Energy is $\mathcal{E}=2(1+\eps) \le 6(1+\eps) = (1+\eps)W$, so $F=1$ passes the feasibility test. Edge $\{c,t\}$ has the largest congestion ($\rho_{ct}=1$) and receives the largest multiplicative weight update.
  \item \textbf{Round 2}: $r_{ct}$ is now larger than the upstream resistances, stiffening $\{c,t\}$. Because $\{c,t\}$ is the unique edge entering $t$, conservation still forces $f_{ct}=1$, while $a\leftrightarrow b$ symmetry keeps the upstream flow split $\tfrac{1}{2}/\tfrac{1}{2}$.
\end{itemize}
Averaging across rounds leaves $\bar{f}$ equal to $\tfrac{1}{2}$ on $\{s,a\},\{s,b\},\{a,c\},\{b,c\}$, $0$ on $\{a,b\}$, and $1$ on $\{c,t\}$, matching the exact maximum flow of value $1$ across minimum cut $\{c,t\}$.
\end{labbox}

\section{Applications of near-linear Laplacian solvers}\label{ch41:hammer}

Once $\Lap x=b$ runs in $\widetilde{\Oh}(m\log(1/\eps))$ time, many problems reduce to expressing a graph objective in terms of $\ell_2$ Laplacian energy and calling the solver one or more times:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Electrical flow and single-pair effective resistance.} One solve (Theorem~\ref{ch41:thm-ohm}).
  \item \textbf{Spectral sparsification.} $\Oh(\log n/\eps^2)$ solves via JL random projection to estimate all $m$ leverage scores (Section~\ref{ch41:sparsify}), compressing $\Gph$ down to $\Oh(n\log n/\eps^2)$ edges.
  \item \textbf{Undirected approximate max-flow and min-cut.} $\widetilde{\Oh}(m^{1/3}\poly(1/\eps))$ solves via CKMST (Theorem~\ref{ch41:thm-ckmst}).
  \item \textbf{Random spanning tree sampling.} Leverage scores $w_e R_{\mathrm{eff}}(e)$ give exact edge marginals via Kirchhoff's matrix-tree identity (Section~\ref{ch41:reff}), speeding up random-tree generators.
  \item \textbf{Graph semi-supervised learning and heat diffusion.} Given known labels on a subset of seed vertices, solving $(\Lap+\tau\mathbf{I})x=b$ for $\tau>0$ spreads smooth label scores across unlabeled neighbors (\emph{label propagation}) in one solve, where $\mathbf{I}$ is the $n\times n$ identity matrix.
  \item \textbf{Spectral clustering.} \emph{Inverse power iteration} repeatedly applies $\Lap^{+}$ via linear solves (projecting onto $\mathbf{1}^\perp$ each step) to amplify $1/\lambda_2$ and approximate the Fiedler vector (Chapter~32) in $\widetilde{\Oh}(m)$ time.
  \item \textbf{Interior-point methods for exact flows and matchings.} \emph{Interior-point methods} keep flows strictly inside capacity bounds $\abs{f_e} < u_e$ using a smooth barrier penalty that diverges at the boundary; each local quadratic update (a \emph{Newton step}) reduces to one reweighted Laplacian solve.
\end{itemize}

\begin{aestheticbox}
Classical graph algorithms are \emph{combinatorial}: they advance by discrete local moves --- augmenting along a path, relaxing an edge, or contracting a blossom --- and bound progress with discrete invariants. The Laplacian approach is \emph{continuous}: relax the discrete graph problem into a smooth convex optimization over $\R^n$, solve quadratic subproblems by linear algebra, and round or average back to a discrete solution. Quadratic ($\ell_2$) energy plays the central role because minimizing a quadratic subject to linear conservation constraints reduces directly to solving $\Lap x = b$.
\end{aestheticbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
Resistance-based sampling powers graph-sparsification routines used to accelerate graph neural network (GNN) training and large-scale spectral clustering. \emph{Algebraic multigrid} (AMG) and combinatorial-multigrid solvers --- practical relatives of recursive tree preconditioning that coarsen a graph into a hierarchy of smaller summaries and smooth errors across scales --- solve \emph{symmetric diagonally dominant} (SDD) systems (symmetric matrices $\mathbf{M}$ with $M_{ii} \ge \sum_{j\ne i}\abs{M_{ij}}$, equivalent to Laplacians plus nonnegative diagonal weights) in image segmentation, 3D mesh processing, and integrated-circuit simulation. Interior-point algorithms built around dynamic Laplacian solvers give the fastest known asymptotic running times for minimum-cost flow, bipartite matching, and discrete optimal transport (Chapter~16).
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Historical Context and Controversies}

\begin{historybox}
For over fifty years, maximum flow was treated purely through discrete graph searches. Starting with Ford and Fulkerson (1956), Dinitz (1970), Edmonds and Karp (1972), and Goldberg and Tarjan (1988, push-relabel), researchers designed augmenting-path, blocking-flow, and preflow methods, yet running times on sparse general-capacity graphs stalled near $\Oh(m\sqrt{n})$ and $\Oh(m^{3/2})$ (achieved by Goldberg and Rao in 1998). In 2004, Daniel Spielman and Shang-Hua Teng gave the first nearly linear-time solver for SDD linear systems $\Lap x = b$, running in $\widetilde{\Oh}(m\log(1/\eps))$ time by combining low-stretch spanning trees with recursive graph sparsification.

Between 2008 and 2011, Daniel Spielman and Nikhil Srivastava introduced spectral sparsification by effective-resistance sampling, proving that $\Oh(n\log n/\eps^2)$ edges sampled proportional to $w_e R_{\mathrm{eff}}(e)$ preserve all cuts and quadratic forms within $1\pm\eps$. In 2011, Christiano, Kelner, Madry, Spielman, and Teng (CKMST) coupled electrical flows with multiplicative weights and width reduction to compute $(1-\eps)$-approximate undirected max-flow in $\widetilde{\Oh}(m^{4/3}\eps^{-3})$ time, breaking the $\Oh(m^{3/2})$ barrier on sparse graphs. In 2022, Chen, Kyng, Liu, Peng, Probst Gutenberg, and Sachdeva combined interior-point methods with dynamic data structures to solve exact directed minimum-cost flow in $m^{1+o(1)}$ time.

When continuous flow relaxations first appeared, many combinatorialists doubted that $\ell_2$ electrical currents --- which minimize $\sum r_e f_e^2$ with no hard capacity ceiling --- could compete on $\ell_\infty$ capacity-constrained problems, precisely because low-resistance shortcuts concentrate up to $\Th(\sqrt{m})$ congestion on bottleneck edges. CKMST's energy-charging deletion step overcame that width barrier in theory. Bridging theory and practice remains active: asymptotic $m^{1+o(1)}$ flow algorithms carry large polylogarithmic overheads, so production solvers still rely on push-relabel variants for discrete max-flow and algebraic multigrid for linear systems.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{What We Can Now Do and Its Limitations}
\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{What this gives us}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Linear systems $\Lap x = b$ in graph Laplacians can be solved to relative $\Lap$-norm error $\eps$ in $\widetilde{\Oh}(m \log(1/\eps))$ time, making electrical flows and effective resistances nearly as cheap as reading the graph.
  \item Any $m$-edge weighted graph can be compressed in $\widetilde{\Oh}(m/\eps^2)$ time into a $(1 \pm \eps)$-spectral sparsifier on $\Oh(n \log n / \eps^2)$ edges (and $\Oh(n/\eps^2)$ edges deterministically).
  \item Undirected maximum flow and minimum cut can be approximated within $1 \pm \eps$ in $\widetilde{\Oh}(m^{4/3}\poly(1/\eps))$ time via CKMST (and $\widetilde{\Oh}(m\,\poly(1/\eps))$ with $\ell_\infty$ congestion approximators).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Limitations}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Approximation versus exactness}: Multiplicative-weights electrical-flow loops output $(1 \pm \eps)$-approximate fractional flows; obtaining exact integral flows requires interior-point iterations and cycle-canceling rounding.
  \item \emph{Constant factors and implementation overhead}: Full recursive low-stretch-tree solvers involve intricate graph decompositions and polylogarithmic constants, so practical software typically uses algebraic multigrid (AMG) or Cholesky-sampling heuristics.
  \item \emph{Undirected / SDD scope}: Laplacian solvers require symmetric diagonally dominant matrices. Directed graphs yield asymmetric Laplacians whose linear systems do not self-adjointly minimize a quadratic energy without specialized reductions.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Where We Go Next}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Chapter~42 (Appendix: Math and Data Structures) collects the linear-algebraic and algorithmic background referenced across the book: Courant--Fischer min-max characterization, Rayleigh quotients, scalar and matrix concentration bounds, Euler-tour trees, segment trees, and disjoint-set union.
\end{itemize}
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{exercise}[Hand-solve a second demand]
On the running network of Section~\ref{ch41:running} with unit conductances, solve $\Lap x = e_a - e_b$ (inject $+1$ at $a$, extract $1$ at $b$). Use symmetry to determine $x_s,x_c,x_t$ first, then verify $\Lap x = e_a - e_b$. Compute the electrical flow and $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(a,b)$, and explain why $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(a,b)<1$ even though edge $\{a,b\}$ itself has resistance $1$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Energy equals resistance]
Prove that for the unit electrical flow $f^\star$ from $s$ to $t$, the minimum energy $\mathcal{E}(f^\star)$ equals $R_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,t)=(e_s-e_t)^\top\Lap^{+}(e_s-e_t)$, and that both equal the potential drop $x_s-x_t$. (Hint: substitute $f^\star=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{B}x$ into $\mathcal{E}(f^\star) = (f^\star)^\top \mathbf{R} f^\star$ and use $\Lap x = e_s-e_t$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Stretch of a cycle]
For the unweighted $n$-vertex cycle $C_n$, let $T$ be $C_n$ minus one edge $e$. Compute $\operatorname{st}_T(C_n)$ exactly, together with the condition-number bound from Lemma~\ref{ch41:lem-stretch}. Compare your bound against the exact condition number $\kappa(\Lap_{C_n})=\Th(n^2)$ on $\mathbf{1}^\perp$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Leverage scores sum to $n-1$]
Prove that $\sum_{e \in E} w_e R_{\mathrm{eff}}(e) = n-1$ for every connected weighted graph on $n$ vertices. (Hint: use cyclic trace $\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}) = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A})$ to write $w_e R_{\mathrm{eff}}(e)=\operatorname{tr}(\Lap^{+}\,w_e b_e b_e^\top)$, sum over $e \in E$, and evaluate the trace of $\Lap^{+}\Lap$ on $\mathbf{1}^\perp$.) Explain why this sum keeps $q = \Oh(n\log n/\eps^2)$ samples sufficient in Theorem~\ref{ch41:thm-ss} regardless of $m$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[PCG iteration count]
Suppose $\mathbf{P}\preceq\Lap\preceq\kappa\mathbf{P}$ on $\mathbf{1}^\perp$ (as for a spanning-tree preconditioner; for two-sided $\mathbf{P}\approx_\kappa\Lap$, replace $\kappa$ by $\kappa^2$). Show that all eigenvalues of $\mathbf{P}^{+}\Lap$ on $\mathbf{1}^\perp$ lie in $[1,\kappa]$, so its condition number is at most $\kappa$ and PCG (Algorithm~\ref{ch41:alg-pcg}) reaches relative $\Lap$-norm error $\eps$ in $\Oh(\sqrt\kappa\log(1/\eps))$ iterations. Where does the $\sqrt\kappa$ improvement over gradient descent's $\Oh(\kappa\log(1/\eps))$ come from?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Width is the bottleneck]\label{ch41:ex-width}
Construct an undirected unit-capacity graph ($u_e = 1$) with $m$ edges and terminals $s,t$ for which a feasible target flow value $F$ causes the round-$1$ electrical flow (with unit resistances) to put congestion $\Th(\sqrt{m})$ on one edge. (Hint: place one direct edge $(s,t)$ in parallel with $k = \Th(\sqrt{m})$ edge-disjoint paths of length $k$, and route the true max-flow value $F = k+1$.) Explain why width $\mu = \Th(\sqrt{m})$ forces Algorithm~\ref{ch41:alg-maxflow} without edge deletion to take $\Th(\sqrt{m}\,\eps^{-2}\log m)$ solves ($\widetilde{\Oh}(m^{3/2})$ time), and how deleting high-congestion edges reduces the solve count to $\widetilde{\Oh}(m^{1/3})$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[From electrical flow to a cut]

\end{exercise}
