\chapter{Perfect Graphs and Structure}

Graph coloring, maximum clique, and maximum independent set are all $\mathrm{NP}$-hard on general graphs. Even the size of the largest clique, $\omega(\Gph)$, gives only a loose lower bound on the chromatic number $\chi(\Gph)$: as Chapter~28 showed, triangle-free graphs ($\omega(\Gph)=2$) can still require arbitrarily many colors. This chapter studies the \emph{perfect graphs}, the class of graphs where that gap vanishes on every \emph{induced subgraph} $H$ --- every graph formed by picking a subset of vertices and keeping all edges of $\Gph$ between them. When $\chi(H) = \omega(H)$ holds on every induced subgraph, four hard problems become solvable in polynomial time: minimum coloring, maximum clique, maximum independent set, and \emph{minimum clique cover} (partitioning the vertices into the fewest cliques). When a graph also has no chordless cycles of length four or more, a single linear-time vertex ordering solves all four problems in one pass.

\begin{historybox}
The concept of perfect graphs grew out of information theory. In 1956, Claude Shannon
studied the \emph{zero-error capacity} of a noisy
channel: transmitting symbols over a communications channel where receiver noise can
confuse certain pairs of signals. Modeling the channel as a confusability graph (one vertex
per symbol, with an edge between any two symbols that can be mistaken for each other),
Shannon noted that the maximum number of mutually distinguishable single-letter messages is
the independence number $\alpha(\Gph)$, while multi-letter block codes behave much more subtly.

In 1961, French mathematician Claude Berge observed that for several structured graph
families --- bipartite graphs (K\H{o}nig 1931), overlap graphs of intervals on a line
(Hajnal--Sur\'anyi 1958), and graphs of order relations on partially ordered sets
(Dilworth 1950) --- the chromatic number $\chi(H)$
equaled the clique number $\omega(H)$ on every induced subgraph $H$.
Berge called such graphs \emph{perfect} and posed two conjectures: the Weak
Perfect Graph Conjecture (a graph $\Gph$ is perfect if and only if its complement
$\overline{\Gph}$ --- the graph on the same vertices whose edges are the non-edges of $\Gph$ ---
is perfect) and the Strong Perfect Graph Conjecture ($\Gph$ is perfect if and only if neither
$\Gph$ nor $\overline{\Gph}$ contains an induced odd cycle of length at least $5$).

In 1972, 24-year-old Hungarian mathematician L{\'a}szl{\'o} Lov{\'a}sz proved the Weak
Conjecture using his Replication Lemma. Lov\'asz later introduced the $\vartheta$ (``theta'')
function, a continuous bound defined by a \emph{semidefinite program} (an optimization problem
over symmetric matrices with nonnegative eigenvalues). Martin Gr{\"o}tschel, Lov{\'a}sz, and
Alexander Schrijver (1981) optimized $\vartheta$ via the \emph{ellipsoid method} --- an iterative
geometric algorithm that shrinks a bounding ellipsoid around the optimal point --- giving
the first polynomial-time algorithms for coloring, clique, and independent set on any perfect
graph. Maria Chudnovsky, Neil Robertson, Paul Seymour, and Robin Thomas proved the Strong
Conjecture in 2002 (published 2006) in a 178-page decomposition paper, and Chudnovsky,
G{\'e}rard Cornu{\'e}jols, Xinming Liu, Seymour, and Kristina Vu{\v{s}}kovi{\'c} (2005) gave a
polynomial-time algorithm to test whether a given graph is perfect. Finding a purely
\emph{combinatorial} polynomial-time coloring algorithm for general perfect graphs --- one that
operates directly on vertices and edges without continuous matrix optimization --- remains open.
\end{historybox}

\section{What perfection means}

Start with two numbers attached to every undirected graph $\Gph=(V,E)$ with vertex set $V$ and edge set $E$.

\begin{definition}[Clique and chromatic numbers]\label{ch30:def-omega-chi}
The \emph{clique number} $\omega(\Gph)$ is the size of the largest set of pairwise-adjacent vertices. The \emph{chromatic number} $\chi(\Gph)$ is the fewest colors needed in a proper coloring (an assignment of colors to vertices in which adjacent vertices receive different colors).
\end{definition}

There is always a one-sided inequality between them.

\begin{proposition}[The trivial bound]\label{ch30:prop-trivial-bound}
For every graph $\Gph$, $\chi(\Gph)\ge\omega(\Gph)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A clique of size $\omega=\omega(\Gph)$ is a set of $\omega$ mutually adjacent vertices. In any proper coloring no two of them may share a color, so at least $\omega$ distinct colors appear on that clique alone. Every proper coloring therefore uses at least $\omega$ colors, giving $\chi(\Gph)\ge\omega(\Gph)$.
\end{proof}

So $\omega(\Gph)$ is a \emph{lower bound} on $\chi(\Gph)$: you cannot color with fewer colors than your largest clique demands. Is a large clique the \emph{only} obstruction to coloring with few colors? It is not.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Think of $\omega(\Gph)$ as the local reason a graph needs many colors: $k$ mutually adjacent vertices cannot fit into fewer than $k$ color classes (the independent sets of same-colored vertices). The gap $\chi(\Gph)-\omega(\Gph)$ measures coloring difficulty with \emph{no} local clique witness: difficulty caused by global structure, such as a long odd cycle that looks like a path around every vertex yet refuses two colors overall. A \emph{perfect} graph has no such hidden difficulty anywhere; the clique bound is always tight.
\end{intuitionbox}

In $C_5$ the largest clique is a single edge, so $\omega(C_5)=2$. Yet walking around the odd cycle alternating two colors returns to the start needing a third color, so $\chi(C_5)=3>2=\omega(C_5)$. Every single vertex's neighborhood is two non-adjacent vertices --- locally as harmless as a path --- so the obstruction is global.

Perfection requires $\chi=\omega$ not only on the whole graph, but on every induced subgraph. Recall the distinction from Definition~\ref{ch01:def-subgraph}: the induced subgraph $\Gph[S]$ keeps \emph{every} edge of $\Gph$ with both endpoints in $S$ (unlike an arbitrary subgraph, you may not delete an edge while keeping both of its endpoints), and $\Gph-S=\Gph[V\setminus S]$.

\begin{definition}[Perfect graph]\label{ch30:def-perfect}
A graph $\Gph$ is \emph{perfect} if $\chi(H)=\omega(H)$ for every induced subgraph $H=\Gph[S]$ with $S\subseteq V$ (including $H=\Gph$ itself).
\end{definition}

Checking every induced subgraph is what gives the definition teeth. Requiring $\chi(\Gph)=\omega(\Gph)$ on the whole graph alone is satisfied by accident whenever you attach a large clique to a messy graph, as Figure~\ref{ch30:fig-induced-trap} illustrates.

\begin{trapbox}
``$\chi(\Gph)=\omega(\Gph)$ for the whole graph'' does \emph{not} make $\Gph$ perfect. Take $C_5$ together with a disjoint triangle $K_3$ (Figure~\ref{ch30:fig-induced-trap}, right): the combined graph has $\omega(\Gph)=3$ and can be $3$-colored, so $\chi(\Gph)=\omega(\Gph)=3$ globally, yet the induced subgraph $H=C_5$ still has $\chi(H)=3>2=\omega(H)$. Perfection is a \emph{hereditary} property --- one that automatically holds on every induced subgraph whenever it holds on $\Gph$ --- and only that stronger guarantee yields polynomial-time algorithms.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{focusbox}
On a perfect graph you can find four separately hard objects in polynomial time:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a maximum clique, of size $\omega(\Gph)$;
  \item a maximum independent set (pairwise non-adjacent vertices), of size $\alpha(\Gph)$;
  \item an optimal proper coloring, using $\chi(\Gph)=\omega(\Gph)$ colors;
  \item a minimum clique cover, partitioning $V$ into $\bar\chi(\Gph)=\alpha(\Gph)$ cliques.
\end{itemize}
On a \emph{general} graph all four are $\mathrm{NP}$-hard. Perfection is the structural guarantee that makes all four tractable together.
\end{focusbox}

\section{The complement, and the Perfect Graph Theorem}

Definition~\ref{ch30:def-perfect} hides a symmetry between a graph and its complement. Recall from Chapter~29 that the \emph{complement} $\overline{\Gph}$ has the same vertex set $V$, with $uv$ an edge of $\overline{\Gph}$ if and only if $uv$ is \emph{not} an edge of $\Gph$ (for $u\neq v$).

Cliques and independent sets swap under complementation. A set of pairwise-adjacent vertices in $\Gph$ is a set of pairwise-non-adjacent vertices in $\overline{\Gph}$, so
\[
  \omega(\Gph)=\alpha(\overline{\Gph}),\qquad \alpha(\Gph)=\omega(\overline{\Gph}).
\]
Coloring translates too. A proper coloring of $\overline{\Gph}$ partitions $V$ into independent sets of $\overline{\Gph}$, and each independent set of $\overline{\Gph}$ is a clique of $\Gph$. Thus $\chi(\overline{\Gph})$ is the minimum number of cliques of $\Gph$ needed to partition all vertices --- the \emph{clique-cover number} $\bar\chi(\Gph)$:
\[
  \bar\chi(\Gph)=\chi(\overline{\Gph}).
\]
Putting these identities together, the equation $\chi(\overline{\Gph})=\omega(\overline{\Gph})$ reads, in the language of $\Gph$, as $\bar\chi(\Gph)=\alpha(\Gph)$: the minimum clique cover has the same size as the maximum independent set.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Perfection of $\Gph$ compares colorings against cliques ($\chi=\omega$). Perfection of $\overline{\Gph}$ compares clique covers against independent sets ($\bar\chi=\alpha$). On the surface those look like unrelated conditions. Lov\'asz proved that they are equivalent: $\Gph$ is perfect if and only if $\overline{\Gph}$ is.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{theorem}[Perfect Graph Theorem; Lov\'asz 1972]\label{ch30:thm-pgt}
A graph $\Gph$ is perfect if and only if its complement $\overline{\Gph}$ is perfect.
\end{theorem}

Theorem~\ref{ch30:thm-pgt} has a self-contained proof built on one lemma: duplicating a vertex inside a perfect graph keeps the graph perfect.

\begin{definition}[Vertex replication]\label{ch30:def-replication}
To \emph{replicate} a vertex $v$ of $\Gph$, add a new vertex $v'$ adjacent to $v$ and to every neighbor of $v$ (making $v$ and $v'$ adjacent ``twins''). Replicating $v$ into $k$ copies repeats this $k-1$ times, replacing $v$ by a $k$-clique $\{v_1,\dots,v_k\}$ whose vertices all share $v$'s outside neighborhood. Figure~\ref{ch30:fig-replication} illustrates a single replication.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Replication Lemma]\label{ch30:lem-replication}
If $\Gph$ is perfect and $\Gph'$ is obtained from $\Gph$ by replicating a vertex, then $\Gph'$ is perfect.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Every proper induced subgraph of $\Gph'$ is either an induced subgraph of $\Gph$ (if it omits $v$ or $v'$) or a single replication of one (if it keeps both $v$ and $v'$). By induction on $\abs{V(\Gph)}$ it therefore suffices to show $\chi(\Gph')=\omega(\Gph')$ for one new twin $v'$ of $v$. Write $\omega=\omega(\Gph)$ and fix a proper $\omega$-coloring of $\Gph$.

\emph{Case 1: $v$ lies in some maximum clique $K$ of $\Gph$.} Since $v'$ is adjacent to $v$ and to every neighbor of $v$, the set $K\cup\{v'\}$ is a clique of size $\omega+1$, and no clique can be larger, so $\omega(\Gph')=\omega+1$. Keep the $\omega$-coloring of $\Gph$ and give $v'$ one new color to obtain an $(\omega+1)$-coloring of $\Gph'$.

\emph{Case 2: $v$ lies in no maximum clique of $\Gph$.} Any clique of $\Gph'$ containing both $v$ and $v'$ comes from a clique of $\Gph$ containing $v$ plus $v'$, which has size at most $(\omega-1)+1=\omega$; hence $\omega(\Gph')=\omega$. Let $C$ be the color class of $v$ (the set of vertices receiving $v$'s color in our $\omega$-coloring). Every maximum clique of $\Gph$ has size $\omega$, so it uses all $\omega$ colors and meets $C$; because $v$ lies in no maximum clique, it meets $C\setminus\{v\}$. Deleting $C\setminus\{v\}$ destroys every maximum clique of $\Gph$, so $H=\Gph-(C\setminus\{v\})$ satisfies $\omega(H)\le\omega-1$. Because $H$ is an induced subgraph of $\Gph$, $H$ is perfect and admits a proper $(\omega-1)$-coloring. Finally, no vertex of $C\setminus\{v\}$ is adjacent to $v$ (they shared a color), so none is adjacent to its twin $v'$; thus $(C\setminus\{v\})\cup\{v'\}$ is an independent set in $\Gph'$. Giving that set the $\omega$-th color completes a proper $\omega$-coloring of $\Gph'$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{ch30:thm-pgt}]
Since $\overline{\overline{\Gph}}=\Gph$, it suffices to show that whenever $\Gph$ is perfect, $\overline{\Gph}$ is also perfect. Every induced subgraph of $\overline{\Gph}$ is the complement of an induced subgraph of $\Gph$, so we need only prove $\chi(\overline{\Gph})=\omega(\overline{\Gph})$, meaning $V$ can be covered by $\alpha=\alpha(\Gph)$ cliques of $\Gph$. Proceed by induction on $\abs{V}$; the empty graph ($\abs{V}=0$) is covered by $0$ cliques. Assume $\abs{V}\ge 1$.

\emph{Key claim: some nonempty clique of $\Gph$ meets every maximum independent set.} Suppose, for contradiction, that every nonempty clique $K$ is missed by at least one maximum independent set $A_K$ ($K\cap A_K=\emptyset$). Let $N\ge 1$ be the total number of nonempty cliques of $\Gph$. Replace each vertex $v\in V$ by $t_v$ replicated copies, where $t_v$ is the number of nonempty cliques $K$ for which $v\in A_K$ (if $t_v=0$, delete $v$). By repeated application of Lemma~\ref{ch30:lem-replication}, the blown-up graph $\Gph^\ast$ is perfect. Count three invariants of $\Gph^\ast$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Vertices:} summing copy counts over vertices is the same as summing $\abs{A_K}=\alpha$ over all $N$ nonempty cliques, so
  \[
    \abs{V(\Gph^\ast)} = \sum_{v\in V} t_v = \sum_{K} \abs{A_K} = \alpha N.
  \]
  \item \emph{Independence number:} the $t_v$ copies of a single vertex $v$ form a clique, so any independent set of $\Gph^\ast$ picks at most one copy per vertex and projects to an independent set of $\Gph$; hence $\alpha(\Gph^\ast)\le\alpha$.
  \item \emph{Clique number:} every nonempty clique of $\Gph^\ast$ lies inside the blown-up copies of some nonempty clique $K'$ of $\Gph$, so its size is at most
  \[
    \sum_{v\in K'} t_v = \sum_{K} \abs{K'\cap A_K}.
  \]
  Because a clique $K'$ and an independent set $A_K$ share at most one vertex, each of the $N$ terms is $0$ or $1$, and the term $K=K'$ equals $0$ by choice of $A_{K'}$. Therefore $\omega(\Gph^\ast)\le N-1$.
\end{itemize}
In any proper coloring of $\Gph^\ast$, each color class is an independent set of size at most $\alpha(\Gph^\ast)$, so at least $\abs{V(\Gph^\ast)}/\alpha(\Gph^\ast)$ colors are needed:
\[
  \chi(\Gph^\ast) \;\ge\; \frac{\abs{V(\Gph^\ast)}}{\alpha(\Gph^\ast)} \;\ge\; \frac{\alpha N}{\alpha} \;=\; N \;>\; N-1 \;\ge\; \omega(\Gph^\ast),
\]
contradicting that $\Gph^\ast$ is perfect.

\emph{Inductive step.} Choose a clique $K$ that meets every maximum independent set of $\Gph$. Removing $K$ drops the independence number by at least $1$, so $\alpha(\Gph-K)\le\alpha-1$. Since $\Gph-K$ is perfect, the induction hypothesis covers $\Gph-K$ with at most $\alpha-1$ cliques; adding $K$ covers $V$ with at most $\alpha$ cliques, giving $\bar\chi(\Gph)\le\alpha(\Gph)\le\bar\chi(\Gph)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{whybox}
Replication turns a qualitative failure into a counting contradiction. In any perfect graph $\Gph$, partitioning $V$ into $\chi(\Gph)=\omega(\Gph)$ independent color classes of size at most $\alpha(\Gph)$ gives the product bound $\abs{V}\le\omega(\Gph)\cdot\alpha(\Gph)$. Blowing up vertices by $t_v$ weights the graph toward its maximum independent sets until any hypothetical failure of $\bar\chi=\alpha$ forces $\abs{V(\Gph^\ast)}>\omega(\Gph^\ast)\cdot\alpha(\Gph^\ast)$.
\end{whybox}

\subsection*{The Strong Perfect Graph Theorem}

Berge's second conjecture characterizes perfect graphs directly by the induced subgraphs they forbid.

\begin{definition}[Holes and antiholes]\label{ch30:def-holes}
An \emph{odd hole} is an induced cycle $C_{2k+1}$ of odd length $2k+1\ge 5$. An \emph{odd antihole} is the complement $\overline{C_{2k+1}}$ of an odd hole ($2k+1\ge 5$). A graph containing neither an odd hole nor an odd antihole as an induced subgraph is called a \emph{Berge graph}.
\end{definition}

Neither family can appear inside a perfect graph (Figure~\ref{ch30:fig-holes-antiholes}): an odd hole $C_{2k+1}$ has $\omega=2$ and $\chi=3$, while an odd antihole $\overline{C_{2k+1}}$ has $\omega=k$ (the largest independent set of $C_{2k+1}$) and $\chi=k+1$ (each color class can cover at most $2$ adjacent cycle vertices, and $2k+1>2k$). Berge conjectured that these are the \emph{only} minimal obstructions.

\begin{theorem}[Strong Perfect Graph Theorem; Chudnovsky--Robertson--Seymour--Thomas 2006]\label{ch30:thm-spgt}
A graph is perfect if and only if it is a Berge graph: it contains no odd hole and no odd antihole as an induced subgraph.
\end{theorem}

\begin{connectionbox}
The Lov\'asz theta function $\vartheta(\Gph)$ links perfect graphs to Chapters~31, 32, and~38. Defined by a semidefinite program (SDP), $\vartheta$ evaluated on the complement is sandwiched between the clique and chromatic numbers:
\[
  \omega(\Gph) \;\le\; \vartheta(\overline{\Gph}) \;\le\; \chi(\Gph).
\]
The Gr\"otschel--Lov\'asz--Schrijver algorithm approximates $\vartheta(\overline{\Gph})$ to high precision via the ellipsoid method, and on a perfect graph $\vartheta(\overline{\Gph})=\omega(\Gph)=\chi(\Gph)$ is an exact integer. Here an exact discrete invariant is computed \emph{through} continuous optimization. On the structured subclasses in the rest of this chapter, vertex orderings avoid SDPs altogether and run in linear time.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Three structured families that are perfect}

General perfect-graph algorithms are polynomial-time, though slow in practice because they solve semidefinite programs. Most perfect graphs that arise in applications belong to one of three structured families that admit fast combinatorial algorithms.

\begin{definition}[Interval graph]\label{ch30:def-interval}
A graph $\Gph$ is an \emph{interval graph} if each vertex $v$ can be assigned a closed real interval $I_v=[a_v,b_v]$ on the number line so that $uv\in E$ if and only if $I_u\cap I_v\neq\emptyset$. The family $\{I_v\}_{v\in V}$ is an \emph{interval representation} of $\Gph$ (Figure~\ref{ch30:fig-interval}).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Comparability graph]\label{ch30:def-comparability}
A \emph{partial order} (or \emph{poset}) on $V$ is a relation $<$ that is irreflexive ($u\not< u$) and transitive ($u<v$ and $v<w$ imply $u<w$); two vertices $u\neq v$ are \emph{comparable} if $u<v$ or $v<u$, and \emph{incomparable} otherwise. An undirected graph $\Gph$ is a \emph{comparability graph} if its edges are the comparable pairs of some partial order on $V$. Equivalently, the edges of $\Gph$ can be given an acyclic direction (no directed cycles) that is transitive: whenever $u\to v$ and $v\to w$ are directed edges, $u\to w$ is also a directed edge.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Chordal graph]\label{ch30:def-chordal}
A graph $\Gph$ is \emph{chordal} (or \emph{triangulated}) if every cycle of length at least $4$ has a \emph{chord}: an edge joining two vertices that are not consecutive around the cycle. Equivalently, $\Gph$ contains no induced cycle $C_k$ for any $k\ge 4$.
\end{definition}

\end{proposition}

\begin{applicationbox}
Interval graphs model \emph{interval scheduling}: tasks occupy time intervals, two tasks conflict if and only if their intervals overlap, and a proper $\chi$-coloring assigns tasks to the fewest machines so no machine runs two overlapping tasks. Because interval graphs are perfect, $\chi(\Gph)=\omega(\Gph)$: the number of machines needed equals the peak number of tasks active at a single instant, and sorting tasks by start time while assigning each to the first free machine achieves that minimum. Chordal graphs model \emph{sparse Gaussian elimination}: when eliminating variables one by one from a symmetric linear system, eliminating a variable connects its remaining neighbors, creating new nonzero entries called \emph{fill-in} (Chapter~24). A graph is chordal if and only if its vertices admit an elimination order that creates zero fill-in. Comparability graphs model \emph{precedence orders}, where perfection equates longest chains with antichain partitions.
\end{applicationbox}

Why is every comparability graph perfect? Every induced subgraph of a comparability graph restricts the partial order to a subset, so it is again a comparability graph. On any partial order, assign each element $v$ the color
\[
  c(v) \;=\; \text{number of elements in a longest chain ending at } v.
\]
Whenever $u<v$, taking a longest chain ending at $u$ and appending $v$ produces a strictly longer chain ending at $v$, so $c(u)<c(v)$. Adjacent vertices in the comparability graph therefore receive distinct colors, and the largest color used equals the size $\omega$ of a longest chain. Hence $\chi=\omega$ on every induced subgraph (Mirsky's theorem: minimum antichain cover equals maximum chain size). Applying the Perfect Graph Theorem~\ref{ch30:thm-pgt} to the complement immediately gives the dual statement $\bar\chi=\alpha$: the minimum number of chains needed to cover the partial order equals the size of the largest antichain (Dilworth's theorem; see Exercise~\ref{ch30:ex-dilworth}).

\section{Chordal graphs: simplicial vertices and elimination orderings}\label{ch30:sec-chordal}

Chordal graphs include trees, interval graphs, and \emph{$k$-trees} (graphs built from a $(k+1)$-clique by repeatedly adding a new vertex adjacent to an existing $k$-clique), yet all four optimization problems run in linear time on them. Everything rests on one local property.

\begin{definition}[Simplicial vertex]\label{ch30:def-simplicial}
A vertex $v$ is \emph{simplicial} if its open neighborhood $N(v)$ is a clique --- every two neighbors of $v$ are adjacent to each other. Equivalently, $\{v\}\cup N(v)$ is a clique (Figure~\ref{ch30:fig-simplicial}).
\end{definition}

Dirac proved that every chordal graph has at least one simplicial vertex.

\begin{theorem}[Dirac 1961]\label{ch30:thm-dirac}
Every chordal graph with at least one vertex has a simplicial vertex. If the graph is not complete, it has \emph{two} non-adjacent simplicial vertices.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Induct on $\abs{V}$. A $1$-vertex graph is trivial, and in a complete graph every vertex is simplicial. Let $\Gph$ be chordal and not complete, and pick two non-adjacent vertices $a$ and $b$. Choose a \emph{minimal vertex separator} $S$ between $a$ and $b$: a vertex set $S\subseteq V\setminus\{a,b\}$ whose removal places $a$ and $b$ in different connected components, and from which no vertex of $S$ can be omitted without reconnecting $a$ and $b$. Let $A$ and $B$ be the connected components of $\Gph-S$ containing $a$ and $b$, respectively.

First, $S$ must be a clique (Figure~\ref{ch30:fig-dirac-separator}). Suppose two vertices $x,y\in S$ were not adjacent. Because $S$ is minimal, omitting $x$ reconnects $A$ and $B$, so $x$ has at least one neighbor in $A$ and at least one in $B$; the same holds for $y$. Let $P_A$ be a shortest path between $x$ and $y$ whose internal vertices lie in $A$, and let $P_B$ be a shortest path between $x$ and $y$ whose internal vertices lie in $B$. Because $A$ and $B$ have no edges between them and $xy\notin E$, concatenating $P_A$ and $P_B$ forms an induced cycle of length at least $4$ with no chord, contradicting chordality.

Now apply the induction hypothesis to the chordal induced subgraph $\Gph[A\cup S]$. If $\Gph[A\cup S]$ is complete, then $a\in A$ is simplicial in $\Gph[A\cup S]$; otherwise $\Gph[A\cup S]$ has two non-adjacent simplicial vertices, and since $S$ is a clique they cannot both lie in $S$, so at least one vertex $u\in A$ is simplicial in $\Gph[A\cup S]$. Because $u\in A$ has no neighbors outside $A\cup S$, $u$ is simplicial in all of $\Gph$. By the same argument on $\Gph[B\cup S]$, some vertex $w\in B$ is simplicial in $\Gph$, and $u,w$ are non-adjacent because they lie in distinct components of $\Gph-S$.
\end{proof}

Dirac's theorem lets us peel a chordal graph down one simplicial vertex at a time. Recording the removal order gives the central algorithmic structure of the chapter.

\begin{definition}[Perfect elimination ordering]\label{ch30:def-peo}
A \emph{perfect elimination ordering} (PEO) of $\Gph$ is an ordering $v_1,v_2,\dots,v_n$ of $V$ such that for each $i\in\{1,\dots,n\}$, vertex $v_i$ is simplicial in the remaining induced subgraph $\Gph[\{v_i,v_{i+1},\dots,v_n\}]$ --- that is, the \emph{later} neighbors of $v_i$ form a clique.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Fulkerson--Gross 1965]\label{ch30:thm-peo}
A graph is chordal if and only if it has a perfect elimination ordering.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
($\Rightarrow$) If $\Gph$ is chordal, Theorem~\ref{ch30:thm-dirac} gives a simplicial vertex $v_1$. Because $\Gph-v_1$ is an induced subgraph of $\Gph$, it is again chordal (removing a vertex cannot create an induced chordless cycle), so repeating the choice on $\Gph-v_1$ produces $v_1,v_2,\dots,v_n$, which is a PEO by construction.

($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $\Gph$ has a PEO $v_1,\dots,v_n$ and, for contradiction, contains an induced cycle $C$ of length at least $4$. Let $v_i$ be the vertex of $C$ with the smallest index $i$. Its two cycle neighbors $x$ and $y$ both have indices greater than $i$, so both belong to the later-neighbors of $v_i$. By the PEO property, $x$ and $y$ must be adjacent. Because $C$ has length at least $4$, $x$ and $y$ are non-consecutive on $C$, making $xy$ a chord of $C$ --- a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{whybox}
A PEO converts ``no chordless cycle anywhere'' --- a global property over exponentially many cycles --- into a \emph{local} guarantee: process vertices along $v_1,\dots,v_n$, and at the moment you touch $v_i$ its surviving neighbors already form a clique. Once you have the PEO, optimal coloring, maximum clique, maximum independent set, and minimum clique cover each reduce to a single linear pass.
\end{whybox}

\section{Finding a PEO in linear time: Lexicographic BFS}

How do we find a PEO fast? Testing vertices directly for simpliciality takes $\Oh(nm)$ time. Rose, Tarjan, and Lueker (1976) showed that \emph{Lexicographic Breadth-First Search} (Lex-BFS) produces a candidate ordering in $\Oh(n+m)$ time, where $n=\abs{V}$ and $m=\abs{E}$, and a second $\Oh(n+m)$ pass verifies whether that ordering is a genuine PEO (and thus whether $\Gph$ is chordal).

\begin{intuitionbox}
Ordinary BFS breaks ties on its frontier arbitrarily. Lex-BFS breaks them with a history rule: each unnumbered vertex carries a \emph{label} listing the numbers of its already-numbered neighbors in decreasing order. At each step, Lex-BFS picks an unnumbered vertex whose label is \emph{lexicographically largest} --- comparing two sequences entry by entry from left to right (dictionary order), so $(6,5)$ beats $(5,4)$ at the first entry, $(5,3)$ beats $(5)$ because a longer sequence wins when a prefix ties, and any nonempty sequence beats the empty sequence $\varepsilon$. Vertices adjacent to high-numbered cliques float to the front, grouping clique-mates together so that reversing the visit order produces a PEO whenever $\Gph$ is chordal.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Lex-BFS: produces a candidate PEO $v_1=\sigma(1),\dots,v_n=\sigma(n)$}
\label{ch30:alg-lexbfs}
\KwIn{Graph $\Gph=(V,E)$ with $n$ vertices and $m$ edges.}
\KwOut{Numbering $\sigma(n),\sigma(n-1),\dots,\sigma(1)$ of $V$ (candidate PEO eliminates $v_1=\sigma(1)$ first).}
Assign every vertex the empty sequence label $\varepsilon$\;
\For{$i \leftarrow n$ \textbf{downto} $1$}{
  Pick an unnumbered vertex $v$ of lexicographically \textbf{largest} label\;
  $\sigma(i)\leftarrow v$; mark $v$ numbered with index $i$\;
  \ForEach{unnumbered neighbor $w$ of $v$}{
    append $i$ to the end of $w$'s label\;
  }
}
\Return $v_1=\sigma(1),\ v_2=\sigma(2),\ \dots,\ v_n=\sigma(n)$\;
\end{algorithm}

Two facts make Lex-BFS run in linear time and decide chordality. First, labels never need entry-by-entry comparison if unnumbered vertices are stored in a \emph{partition refinement} structure: a doubly linked list (where each node links to both its predecessor and successor for $\Oh(1)$ updates) of \emph{classes}, each class holding all unnumbered vertices with identical labels so far, ordered from largest label to smallest. When vertex $v$ receives number $i$, each class containing neighbors of $v$ splits into (neighbors of $v$, non-neighbors of $v$), with the neighbor half placed immediately ahead of the non-neighbor half. Each edge triggers at most one move, so the entire run takes $\Oh(n+m)$ time. Second, Rose, Tarjan, and Lueker proved that $\Gph$ is chordal \emph{if and only if} the ordering $v_1=\sigma(1),\dots,v_n=\sigma(n)$ returned by Algorithm~\ref{ch30:alg-lexbfs} is a PEO.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Verify whether an ordering $v_1,\dots,v_n$ is a PEO in linear time}
\label{ch30:alg-verifypeo}
\KwIn{Ordering $v_1,\dots,v_n$ (eliminate $v_1$ first) of graph $\Gph=(V,E)$.}
\KwOut{\textsc{True} if $v_1,\dots,v_n$ is a PEO, else \textsc{False} with a non-adjacent pair in some $\mathrm{later}(v_k)$.}
\For{$i\leftarrow 1$ \KwTo $n$}{$A(v_i)\leftarrow\emptyset$\;}
\For{$i\leftarrow 1$ \KwTo $n$}{
  Let $\mathrm{later}(v_i)=\{\,v_j : v_iv_j\in E,\ j>i\,\}$\;
  \If{$\mathrm{later}(v_i)\neq\emptyset$}{
    Let $u$ be the \textbf{earliest} (smallest-index) vertex in $\mathrm{later}(v_i)$\;
    append every vertex of $\mathrm{later}(v_i)\setminus\{u\}$ to $A(u)$\;
  }
  \If{$A(v_i)\not\subseteq \mathrm{later}(v_i)$}{
    \Return \textsc{False} (a required edge between $v_i$ and some $w\in A(v_i)$ is missing)\;
  }
}
\Return \textsc{True}\;
\end{algorithm}

Why does Algorithm~\ref{ch30:alg-verifypeo} run in $\Oh(n+m)$ time without testing all pairs in $\mathrm{later}(v_i)$? Instead of checking every pair of later-neighbors of $v_i$ directly (which could take $\Oh(n^2)$ time), we delegate to the earliest later-neighbor $u$ of $v_i$: we append $\mathrm{later}(v_i)\setminus\{u\}$ to a bucket $A(u)$, requiring $u$ to be adjacent to all of them when the loop reaches $u$. Across the whole loop, at most $\sum_i \abs{\mathrm{later}(v_i)} = m$ entries are appended to buckets, and marking $\mathrm{later}(v_i)$ in a timestamp array checks $A(v_i)\subseteq \mathrm{later}(v_i)$ in $\Oh(\abs{A(v_i)}+\abs{\mathrm{later}(v_i)})$ time, totaling $\Oh(n+m)$. If every check passes, induction from $i=n$ down to $1$ confirms that $u$'s later-neighbors already form a clique, and since $u$ is adjacent to all other vertices of $\mathrm{later}(v_i)$, all of $\mathrm{later}(v_i)$ is a clique. Conversely, if Algorithm~\ref{ch30:alg-verifypeo} returns \textsc{False} on a Lex-BFS ordering, $\Gph$ is not chordal and the missing edge extends through higher-indexed vertices to an induced cycle of length at least $4$.

\begin{labbox}

Maintain the partition of unnumbered vertices as an ordered list of classes, splitting classes into neighbors and non-neighbors of each newly numbered vertex. Check your steps against Table~\ref{ch30:tab-trace}, then reverse the numbering to read off $v_1,\dots,v_6$ and confirm each $\mathrm{later}(v_i)$ is a clique.
\end{labbox}

\begin{whybox}
Partition refinement is what lowers Lex-BFS from $\Oh(n^2)$ to $\Oh(n+m)$. Picture the unnumbered vertices as a left-to-right row of buckets (classes), where vertices in the same bucket have identical labels so far and buckets are sorted from largest label (left) to smallest (right). Numbering $v$ scans only $v$'s adjacency list: for each bucket touched by a neighbor of $v$, those neighbors step into a new bucket immediately to the left of their old bucket. A vertex moves at most once per incident edge, so all bucket splits together cost $\Oh(m)$ time, and picking $n$ front vertices costs $\Oh(n)$ time. String comparison is replaced by incremental bucket splitting.
\end{whybox}

\section{Linear-time coloring and maximum clique on a PEO}

Given a PEO $v_1,\dots,v_n$, greedy coloring (assigning each vertex the smallest positive integer color absent from its already-colored neighbors) finds an optimal coloring in one pass --- provided we process vertices in \emph{reverse} elimination order $v_n,v_{n-1},\dots,v_1$. That way, when $v_i$ is colored, its already-colored neighbors are its later-neighbors $\mathrm{later}(v_i)$, which form a clique (Figure~\ref{ch30:fig-peo-coloring-step}).

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Optimal greedy coloring of a chordal graph from a PEO}
\label{ch30:alg-color}
\KwIn{Chordal graph $\Gph$, PEO $v_1,\dots,v_n$ (eliminate $v_1$ first).}
\KwOut{A proper coloring using $\omega(\Gph)$ colors.}
\For{$i\leftarrow n$ \textbf{downto} $1$}{
  $F\leftarrow\{\, \mathrm{color}(v_j) : v_iv_j\in E,\ j>i\,\}$ \tcp*{colors of already-colored later-neighbors}
  $\mathrm{color}(v_i)\leftarrow$ smallest positive integer not in $F$\;
}
\Return $\mathrm{color}$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[Chordal greedy is optimal]\label{ch30:thm-greedy-opt}
Algorithm~\ref{ch30:alg-color} produces a proper coloring with $\omega(\Gph)$ colors, and the largest color used equals $\omega(\Gph)$. Consequently every chordal graph is perfect, and both $\chi(\Gph)$ and a maximum clique are found in $\Oh(n+m)$ time.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Properness.} When $v_i$ is colored, its color avoids all already-colored neighbors ($j>i$), and each earlier neighbor ($j<i$) avoids $\mathrm{color}(v_i)$ when its turn arrives.

\emph{Color bound.} When $v_i$ is colored, its already-colored neighbors are $\mathrm{later}(v_i)$, which form a clique by the PEO property. Write $k=\abs{\mathrm{later}(v_i)}$. At most $k$ colors appear in $F$, so the smallest positive integer absent from $F$ is at most $k+1$. Meanwhile $\{v_i\}\cup\mathrm{later}(v_i)$ is itself a clique of size $k+1\le\omega(\Gph)$. Combining with Proposition~\ref{ch30:prop-trivial-bound} gives
\[
  \omega(\Gph) \;\le\; \chi(\Gph) \;\le\; \max_{1\le i\le n}\bigl(\abs{\mathrm{later}(v_i)}+1\bigr) \;\le\; \omega(\Gph),
\]
forcing equality throughout. Since every induced subgraph of a chordal graph is also chordal, $\chi(H)=\omega(H)$ holds for every induced subgraph $H$, proving chordal graphs are perfect.

\emph{Maximum clique and running time.} Any clique's lowest-indexed vertex $v_i$ has the rest of that clique inside $\mathrm{later}(v_i)$, so choosing $i$ to maximize $\abs{\mathrm{later}(v_i)}$ yields a maximum clique $\{v_i\}\cup\mathrm{later}(v_i)$ of size $\omega(\Gph)$. Both Lex-BFS and the coloring sweep scan each adjacency list a constant number of times, taking $\Oh(n+m)$ time in total.
\end{proof}

\begin{trapbox}
Direction matters. If you color greedily in the \emph{forward} elimination order ($f$ first), a vertex's already-colored neighbors are its \emph{earlier}-neighbors, which need not form a clique, so greedy can spend more than $\omega(\Gph)$ colors even on a chordal graph (Exercise~\ref{ch30:ex-forward-greedy}). Remember how the two passes pair up: Lex-BFS numbers from $n$ down to $1$, so elimination runs $1\to n$ while optimal coloring runs $n\to 1$ (the same order in which Lex-BFS visited the vertices).
\end{trapbox}

\section{Maximum independent set and minimum clique cover on a PEO}

Maximum independent set and minimum clique cover are solved together by a greedy pass in the \emph{forward} elimination order $v_1,\dots,v_n$: take the first remaining vertex into the independent set, group it with its surviving neighbors as one clique of the cover, delete that clique, and repeat.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Maximum independent set and minimum clique cover from a PEO}
\label{ch30:alg-mis}
\KwIn{Chordal graph $\Gph$, PEO $v_1,\dots,v_n$ (eliminate $v_1$ first).}
\KwOut{A maximum independent set $S$ and a clique cover $\mathcal{K}$ with $\abs{S}=\abs{\mathcal{K}}$.}
$S\leftarrow\emptyset$;\quad $\mathcal{K}\leftarrow\emptyset$;\quad mark all vertices \textit{alive}\;
\For{$i\leftarrow 1$ \KwTo $n$}{
  \If{$v_i$ is alive}{
    $S\leftarrow S\cup\{v_i\}$\;
    $K_i\leftarrow\{v_i\}\cup\{\text{alive later-neighbors of }v_i\}$\;
    $\mathcal{K}\leftarrow\mathcal{K}\cup\{K_i\}$\;
    mark every vertex in $K_i$ \textit{dead}\;
  }
}
\Return $S$ and $\mathcal{K}$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[Chordal MIS and clique cover]\label{ch30:thm-mis}
Algorithm~\ref{ch30:alg-mis} returns a maximum independent set $S$ and a minimum clique cover $\mathcal{K}$ of equal size $\abs{S}=\abs{\mathcal{K}}$. Hence on chordal graphs $\alpha(\Gph)=\bar\chi(\Gph)$, and both are computable in $\Oh(n+m)$ time.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{$S$ is independent, and $\mathcal{K}$ is a clique cover of size $\abs{S}$.} When an alive vertex $v_i$ is added to $S$, any earlier neighbor $v_j$ ($j<i$) cannot still be alive: if $v_j$ had been added to $S$ it would have marked $v_i$ dead, and if $v_j$ was skipped it was already dead. Thus all alive neighbors of $v_i$ lie in $\mathrm{later}(v_i)$. By the PEO property $\mathrm{later}(v_i)$ is a clique, so $K_i=\{v_i\}\cup(\text{alive later-neighbors of }v_i)$ is a clique containing $v_i$ and every alive neighbor of $v_i$. Marking $K_i$ dead ensures no future vertex added to $S$ is adjacent to $v_i$, so $S$ is independent, and because every vertex is marked dead in a unique $K_i$, the family $\mathcal{K}$ partitions $V$ into $\abs{S}$ cliques.

\emph{Optimality by duality.} No clique can contain two vertices of an independent set, so $\alpha(\Gph)\le\bar\chi(\Gph)$ holds on every graph. Because $S$ is an independent set and $\mathcal{K}$ is a clique cover with $\abs{S}=\abs{\mathcal{K}}$,
\[
  \abs{S} \;=\; \abs{\mathcal{K}} \;\ge\; \bar\chi(\Gph) \;\ge\; \alpha(\Gph) \;\ge\; \abs{S},
\]
forcing equality throughout: $S$ is maximum, $\mathcal{K}$ is minimum, and $\alpha(\Gph)=\bar\chi(\Gph)$. Scanning each vertex and its adjacency list once takes $\Oh(n+m)$ time.
\end{proof}

\begin{whybox}
Theorem~\ref{ch30:thm-mis} uses the same certificate pattern as max-flow/min-cut and linear-programming (LP) duality. Every independent set $S'$ and every clique cover $\mathcal{K}'$ satisfy the weak bound $\abs{S'}\le\abs{\mathcal{K}'}$ directly from the definitions. Producing a pair $(S,\mathcal{K})$ of equal size certifies in one step that neither can be improved.
\end{whybox}

\section{Why structure gives polynomial time}

\begin{aestheticbox}
Computing $\chi$, $\omega$, $\alpha$, or $\bar\chi$ is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard on general graphs because arbitrary edges can encode arbitrary logical constraints between choices, leaving no global order to exploit. \emph{Perfection} promises that the local clique lower bound is always tight on every induced subgraph, closing the gap where hardness hides. \emph{Chordality} adds a combinatorial handle: a linear vertex order in which each step faces a clique of surviving neighbors. Turning a global problem into a sequence of local decisions along an order is a recurring pattern across graph algorithms: topological order for dynamic programming on a directed acyclic graph (DAG), BFS layers for unweighted shortest paths, tree-decomposition postorder for bounded treewidth, and the PEO here.
\end{aestheticbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{Summary and Limitations:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Fragility under edge edits:} Perfection requires $\chi(H) = \omega(H)$
        on \emph{every} induced subgraph. Adding or deleting even one edge can create an
        induced $C_5$, breaking perfection so that greedy and SDP guarantees no longer apply.
  \item \emph{Gap between chordal and general perfect graphs:} Chordal, interval, and bipartite
        graphs have fast combinatorial solvers, whereas coloring or finding $\alpha(\Gph)$ on an
        arbitrary perfect graph still relies on optimizing the Lov{\'a}sz $\vartheta$ SDP, which
        is polynomial-time in theory but computationally expensive at scale.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Where We Go Next:}
Lov{\'a}sz's $\vartheta$ function showed that discrete graph invariants can be bounded and solved through matrices and eigenvalues. How do we represent a graph as a linear operator so that eigenvalues reveal its cuts, components, and bottlenecks? In \textbf{Chapter~31 (The Graph Laplacian)}, we build a matrix that measures how values vary across edges --- modeling edges as springs or resistors so linear algebra exposes graph structure.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{marginnotebox}
Keep the inclusion map of Figure~\ref{ch30:fig-hierarchy} in mind: $\text{trees} \subset \text{interval} \subset \text{chordal} \subset \text{perfect}$, with $\text{bipartite} \subset \text{comparability} \subset \text{perfect}$, and $\text{perfect} = \text{Berge}$ (no odd hole, no odd antihole) by Theorem~\ref{ch30:thm-spgt}. Algorithmically, always check for the strongest subclass your input satisfies: chordality gives $\Oh(n+m)$ passes, whereas general perfection falls back to the Lov\'asz $\vartheta$ SDP.
\end{marginnotebox}

\section*{Exercises}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Imperfection certificates]
Show directly that the odd antihole $\overline{C_7}$ has $\omega=3$ and $\chi=4$, certifying it is not perfect. (Hint: a clique in $\overline{C_7}$ is an independent set in $C_7$; the largest has size $3$. For $\chi$, a proper coloring of $\overline{C_7}$ partitions $C_7$ into cliques of $C_7$ --- that is, edges and single vertices --- so count how many you need to cover $7$ vertices.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The product bound]
Prove that for every perfect graph $\abs{V}\le\omega(\Gph)\cdot\alpha(\Gph)$, and show this can fail for imperfect graphs by exhibiting a graph with $\abs{V}>\omega\alpha$. (This inequality is the arithmetic core of the Perfect Graph Theorem; $C_5$ is the smallest counterexample on the imperfect side.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Replication, in full]
The proof of the Replication Lemma~\ref{ch30:lem-replication} handles one twin. Check its first step in full: every induced subgraph of $\Gph'$ is either an induced subgraph of $\Gph$ or a single replication of one. Then run both cases of the proof on $C_4$ with one vertex replicated, and conclude by induction that replicating any vertex any number of times preserves perfection.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Interval $\Rightarrow$ chordal]\label{ch30:ex-interval-chordal}
Prove that every interval graph is chordal. (Hint: suppose intervals realize a chordless cycle $v_1v_2\cdots v_k v_1$ with $k\ge4$. Consider the vertex whose interval has the leftmost right-endpoint and derive a contradiction from the cycle's non-adjacencies.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Trees are chordal, and more]
Show every tree is chordal and exhibit a PEO of a tree directly (no Lex-BFS): repeatedly remove a leaf. What are the maximal cliques of a tree, and what does Algorithm~\ref{ch30:alg-color} reduce to? Confirm $\chi=\omega=2$ for any tree with an edge.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Break the forward greedy]\label{ch30:ex-forward-greedy}
Find a chordal graph and a PEO for which coloring greedily in \emph{forward} elimination order (eliminate-first colored first) uses strictly more than $\omega$ colors. This shows the reverse-order requirement of Algorithm~\ref{ch30:alg-color} is essential, not a convenience.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Lex-BFS by hand]
Run Lex-BFS on the $5$-cycle $C_5$ and on $C_5$ plus one chord. For each, write the elimination order it returns and run the PEO verifier (Algorithm~\ref{ch30:alg-verifypeo}). The verifier should fail on $C_5$ (not chordal); determine whether a single chord makes $C_5$ chordal, and confirm the verifier's verdict.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Clique tree]
For the chordal graph of Figure~\ref{ch30:fig-tracegraph}, list the four maximal cliques and build a \emph{clique tree}: a tree whose nodes are the maximal cliques such that for every vertex $v$, the cliques containing $v$ form a connected subtree. Verify the ``running intersection'' property and explain why this tree is a tree decomposition of width $\omega-1=2$. (Figure~\ref{ch30:fig-cliquetree} shows one such tree; confirm it and decide whether any other clique tree exists here.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Comparability and Dilworth]\label{ch30:ex-dilworth}
A comparability graph comes from a partial order $P$. State Dilworth's theorem (minimum chain cover $=$ maximum antichain) and explain why it is the assertion $\bar\chi=\alpha$ for the comparability graph of $P$. Deduce, using the Perfect Graph Theorem~\ref{ch30:thm-pgt}, that comparability graphs are perfect.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Recognizing interval graphs]
Sketch how to combine a chordality test with a check that the maximal cliques can be linearly ordered so that the cliques containing each vertex are consecutive (the ``consecutive cliques'' property) to recognize interval graphs in polynomial time, and explain how a valid ordering of maximal cliques yields an interval representation. (Booth and Lueker gave a linear-time data structure for this consecutive-ones check; you need only describe the high-level reduction.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Divisibility order]
Consider the divisibility comparability graph of Figure~\ref{ch30:fig-divisibility} on the six-element set $\{1,2,3,4,6,12\}$. Exhibit an optimal coloring with $\chi=\omega=4$ colors and a minimum chain cover with $\bar\chi=\alpha=2$ chains. Then extend the ground set to $\{1,2,\dots,12\}$ under divisibility, and compute $\omega$ (longest chain) and $\alpha$ (largest antichain) for the enlarged order. (Hint for $\alpha$: the integers in $\{7,8,\dots,12\}$ are pairwise non-dividing.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Partition refinement by hand]
Implement, on paper, the partition-refinement view of Lex-BFS: maintain the unnumbered vertices as an ordered list of classes and, at each numbering step, perform the neighbor/non-neighbor split described in the text. Run it on the graph of Figure~\ref{ch30:fig-tracegraph} and confirm you reproduce Table~\ref{ch30:tab-trace} class-by-class. Then argue that the total number of split operations over the whole run is $\Oh(m)$, so the algorithm is $\Oh(n+m)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Perfection is not closed under everything]
Show that perfection is preserved by taking induced subgraphs and by complementation (the latter is Theorem~\ref{ch30:thm-pgt}), but is \emph{not} preserved by adding an arbitrary edge or by taking subgraphs that are not induced. Give a small concrete example for each failure. (Hint: a path $P_5$ is perfect; what single edge turns part of it into an induced $C_5$ inside a larger graph?)
\end{exercise}
