\chapter{Graph Coloring}

\providecommand{\degen}{\operatorname{degen}}

Color a map of the counties of
England so that no two counties sharing a border get the same color, and you will never
need more than four colors. Reframe the map as a graph $\Gph$ -- one vertex per
county, an edge between counties that touch -- and the puzzle becomes the central problem
of this chapter: assign a color to every vertex so that adjacent vertices differ, using as
few colors as possible. That minimum number is the \emph{chromatic number} $\chi(\Gph)$.

In coloring problems an edge no longer
means ``you can travel here,'' but ``these two endpoints cannot make the same choice.''
Compilers mapping variables to CPU registers, universities assigning exams to timeslots,
and mobile towers sharing frequencies are all coloring problems. The central algorithmic question is when a graph can be colored with few colors quickly, and why finding $\chi(\Gph)$ exactly is hard on general graphs.

\begin{historybox}
The mathematical theory of graph coloring began with a cartographic observation. In October 1852,
Francis Guthrie, an undergraduate student at University College London, noticed while coloring
a map of England's counties that four colors always seemed sufficient. Guthrie asked his brother
Frederick, who consulted Augustus De Morgan. In 1879, Alfred Kempe published a claimed proof
introducing \emph{Kempe chains} -- paths alternating between two colors that allow swapping
colors locally. In 1890, Percy Heawood discovered that Kempe's swap could accidentally intertwine
two chains around a vertex of degree $5$, breaking the proof, though Heawood salvaged the
idea to prove the Five Color Theorem (Chapter~26).

Progress on general graphs soon followed. In 1941, R.~Leonard Brooks proved that every connected
graph $\Gph$ has $\chi(\Gph) \le \Delta(\Gph)$, where $\Delta(\Gph)$ is the maximum vertex degree in $\Gph$, unless $\Gph$ is a complete graph (every pair of vertices adjacent) or an odd cycle (L{\'a}szl{\'o} Lov{\'a}sz
later gave an ordering proof, the ancestor of modern algorithmic recoloring). In 1964,
Vadim Vizing proved that edges can always be colored using either $\Delta(\Gph)$ or $\Delta(\Gph)+1$ colors.
The Four Color Theorem itself was finally settled in 1976 by Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken,
who used $1{,}200$ hours of computer time to verify $1{,}936$ local subgraphs (called reducible configurations) that could not appear in a smallest counterexample. The result
provoked debate over whether computer-assisted proofs that no human could
check by hand count as mathematical proof.

On the computational side, Richard Karp placed \textsc{Chromatic Number} on his original 1972
list of 21 NP-complete problems. In the late 1990s, Uriel Feige, Joe Kilian, and Johan H{\aa}stad
used the probabilistically checkable proofs (PCP) theorem -- which encodes proofs so a verifier need inspect only a few random bits -- to show that on an $n$-vertex graph, $\chi(\Gph)$ cannot be approximated within a factor of $n^{1-\eps}$ (for any constant $\eps>0$) in polynomial
time unless $\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}$. Today, whether a $3$-colorable graph can be colored with a constant number of colors ($\Oh(1)$)
in polynomial time remains open.
\end{historybox}

\section{Proper Colorings and the Chromatic Number}
\label{ch28:basics}

\begin{definition}[Proper coloring]
\label{ch28:def-coloring}
Let $\Gph=(V,E)$ be an undirected graph with no self-loops. A \emph{$k$-coloring}
is a function $c:V\to\set{1,2,\dots,k}$. It is \emph{proper} if $c(u)\neq c(v)$ for
every edge $uv\in E$ joining $u$ and $v$. The graph is \emph{$k$-colorable} if a proper $k$-coloring
exists. The \emph{chromatic number} $\chi(\Gph)$ is the least $k$ for which $\Gph$
is $k$-colorable.
\end{definition}

The colors $1,\dots,k$ are unordered labels; nothing about them is numeric.
A proper $k$-coloring carries the same information as a partition of $V$ into $k$ (possibly
empty) \emph{color classes} $V_1,\dots,V_k$, where each $V_i=c^{-1}(i)=\set{v\in V : c(v)=i}$ is an
\emph{independent set} -- a set of vertices with no edge between any two of them (Figure~\ref{ch28:fig-color-classes}). Thus $\chi(\Gph)$ is the minimum
number of independent sets needed to partition $V$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Three small examples show what forces extra colors. A path or any tree with at least one edge needs $2$ colors -- walk
along it alternating between $1$ and $2$. A triangle needs $3$: every pair of its vertices is
adjacent, so no two may share a color. And an odd cycle $C_{2k+1}$ (on $2k+1$ vertices) needs $3$ even
though it looks locally like a path: try to alternate two colors around it and the first and last vertices collide when the cycle closes. That parity clash is why bipartiteness (two-colorability) requires avoiding odd cycles.
\end{intuitionbox}

Figure~\ref{ch28:fig-small} illustrates four basic families. The complete graph $K_n$ has every
pair of vertices adjacent, so all $n$ vertices need distinct colors and $\chi(K_n)=n$. A graph
with no edges has $\chi(\Gph)=1$. Any tree with at least one edge has $\chi(\Gph)=2$. A cycle $C_n$ on $n\ge 3$ vertices has $\chi(C_n)=2$ if $n$ is even and $3$
if $n$ is odd.

Two structural lower bounds on the chromatic number come from cliques and independent sets.

\begin{proposition}[Clique and independent-set bounds]
\label{ch28:prop-bounds}
For every graph $\Gph$ on $n$ vertices,
\[
  \omega(\Gph) \le \chi(\Gph) \le n
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \chi(\Gph) \ge \frac{n}{\alpha(\Gph)},
\]
where $\omega(\Gph)$ is the \emph{clique number} (the size of a largest clique, a set of mutually adjacent vertices) and $\alpha(\Gph)$ is the \emph{independence number} (the size
of a largest independent set).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A clique of size $\omega(\Gph)$ has all pairs adjacent, so its vertices receive
$\omega(\Gph)$ distinct colors; hence $\chi(\Gph)\ge\omega(\Gph)$. Assigning each of the $n$ vertices its own color gives $\chi(\Gph)\le n$. For the ratio bound, every color
class in a proper coloring is an independent set, so each holds at most $\alpha(\Gph)$ vertices; $\chi(\Gph)$ color classes cover
at most $\chi(\Gph)\cdot\alpha(\Gph)$ vertices, which must be at least $n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{trapbox}
Do not mistake $\omega(\Gph)\le\chi(\Gph)$ for an equality. It holds for many small examples (such as Figure~\ref{ch28:fig-color-classes}, where $\omega(\Gph)=3$ and $n/\alpha(\Gph)=6/2=3$ both match $\chi(\Gph)=3$), but the gap can be arbitrarily large. The odd
cycle $C_5$ already separates them: $\omega(C_5)=2$ (no triangle) yet $\chi(C_5)=3$.
Worse, there exist \emph{triangle-free} graphs ($\omega(\Gph)=2$) with chromatic number
as large as you like -- Mycielski's construction builds them (Exercise~\ref{ch28:ex-mycielski}). Graphs where
$\omega(H)=\chi(H)$ holds for the graph \emph{and all its induced subgraphs} $H$ (Definition~\ref{ch01:def-subgraph}) are the \emph{perfect graphs}, the subject of Chapter~30; most graphs are not
perfect.
\end{trapbox}

\section{The Greedy Algorithm and Degeneracy}
\label{ch28:greedy}

A \emph{greedy} algorithm commits to the locally cheapest valid choice one step at a time and never revisits earlier decisions. For coloring, take the vertices in some fixed order $v_1,\dots,v_n$ and assign each $v_i$ the smallest positive integer not yet used on any neighbor $u\in N(v_i)$ (where $N(v_i)$ denotes the set of neighbors of $v_i$). It always outputs a proper coloring, though the number of colors used depends heavily on the vertex order.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{GreedyColor}$(\Gph, \pi)$ -- greedy coloring in a fixed order}
\label{ch28:alg-greedy}
\KwIn{Graph $\Gph=(V,E)$; an ordering $\pi=(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ of $V$.}
\KwOut{A proper coloring $c:V\to\set{1,2,\dots}$.}
\For{$i \leftarrow 1$ \KwTo $n$}{
  $S \leftarrow \set{\, c(u) : u \in N(v_i),\ u \text{ already colored}\,}$\;
  $c(v_i) \leftarrow$ smallest positive integer not in $S$\;
}
\Return $c$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{whybox}
Why is the output always proper? When we color $v_i$, we explicitly forbid every
color currently assigned to a neighbor of $v_i$. A neighbor $v_j$ either is colored
already (we avoid its color now) or comes later (it will avoid \emph{our} color
when its turn arrives). Either way the two endpoints of edge $v_iv_j$ receive different colors. Correctness is automatic; the only question is how many colors get used.
\end{whybox}

An upper bound comes from counting how many earlier neighbors each vertex has.

\begin{proposition}[Greedy degree bound]
\label{ch28:prop-degree}
Running Algorithm~\ref{ch28:alg-greedy} uses at most $\Delta(\Gph)+1$ colors, where
$\Delta(\Gph) = \max_{v\in V}\deg(v)$ is the maximum degree. More sharply, if every vertex $v_i$ has at most $d$
neighbors among $v_1,\dots,v_{i-1}$ (its \emph{back-degree}), then at most $d+1$
colors are used.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
When $v_i$ is colored, the forbidden set $S$ contains at most one color per
already-colored neighbor of $v_i$, so $\abs{S}$ is at most the back-degree of $v_i$, which is at most $d$. Among the $d+1$ integers $\set{1,\dots,d+1}$, at least one is missing from $S$, so $c(v_i)\le d+1$. Since back-degree never
exceeds total degree $\deg(v_i)\le\Delta(\Gph)$, the bound $\Delta(\Gph)+1$ follows.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{ch28:prop-degree} shows we want an ordering that keeps
every vertex's back-degree small. That goal defines a graph parameter.

\begin{definition}[Degeneracy]
\label{ch28:def-degen}
A graph $\Gph$ is \emph{$d$-degenerate} if every nonempty subgraph $H\subseteq\Gph$ has a vertex of degree at most
$d$ in $H$. The \emph{degeneracy} $\degen(\Gph)$ is the smallest such $d$.
\end{definition}

Equivalently, a graph is $d$-degenerate if and only if we can repeatedly delete a vertex of
current degree at most $d$ until no vertices remain. Reversing that deletion sequence gives a
\emph{degeneracy ordering} in which every vertex has back-degree at most $d$ (Figure~\ref{ch28:fig-degen-peeling}); feeding that ordering to
Algorithm~\ref{ch28:alg-greedy} bounds the color count by $d+1$.

To compute this ordering in linear time on an $n$-vertex, $m$-edge graph, track each remaining vertex's \emph{residual degree} -- its number of neighbors still in the remaining graph -- in an array of degree-indexed doubly linked lists (\emph{buckets}) $B_0, B_1, \dots, B_{n-1}$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{DegeneracyOrder}$(\Gph)$ -- smallest-last ordering}
\label{ch28:alg-degen}
\KwIn{Graph $\Gph=(V,E)$.}
\KwOut{Ordering $v_1,\dots,v_n$ with back-degrees $\le \degen(\Gph)$, and $d=\degen(\Gph)$.}
Maintain buckets $B_0,B_1,\dots,B_{n-1}$ where $B_j$ holds remaining vertices of residual degree $j$\;
$d \leftarrow 0$;\quad let $\mathrm{deg}[v]$ be the residual degree of each $v$\;
\For{$i \leftarrow n,\,n-1,\,\dots,\,1$}{
  let $j^\star$ be the smallest nonempty bucket index; remove some $v$ from $B_{j^\star}$\;
  $d \leftarrow \max(d, j^\star)$;\quad $v_i \leftarrow v$\;
  \ForEach{still-present neighbor $u$ of $v$}{
    move $u$ from $B_{\mathrm{deg}[u]}$ to $B_{\mathrm{deg}[u]-1}$;\quad $\mathrm{deg}[u] \leftarrow \mathrm{deg}[u]-1$\;
  }
}
\Return $(v_1,\dots,v_n),\ d$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{whybox}
The smallest-last rule -- repeatedly peel off a minimum-degree vertex of the
remaining graph and place it into the rightmost open slot $v_i$ -- realizes Definition~\ref{ch28:def-degen}
directly. When vertex $v$ is peeled at residual degree $j^\star$, the vertices still remaining are $v_1,\dots,v_{i-1}$, so $v$'s
back-neighbors are its still-present neighbors, of which there are
$j^\star\le d$. The maximum $j^\star$ over the whole peel equals the largest minimum degree $\delta(H)$ among all nonempty subgraphs $H\subseteq\Gph$:
\[
  \degen(\Gph) = \max_{\emptyset\neq H\subseteq \Gph} \delta(H).
\]
Notice $j^\star$ can drop by at most $1$ in a step (when a neighbor's degree decreases by $1$), so finding the next nonempty bucket scans at most one step down before advancing, and moving a neighbor between doubly linked buckets takes $\Oh(1)$ time. Touching each of the $m$ edges once gives $\Oh(n+m)$ total time.
\end{whybox}

\begin{corollary}[Degeneracy coloring bound]
\label{ch28:cor-degen}
Every graph satisfies $\chi(\Gph)\le \degen(\Gph)+1$, and greedy coloring on the
degeneracy ordering achieves $\degen(\Gph)+1$ colors in $\Oh(n+m)$ time.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Order the vertices by Algorithm~\ref{ch28:alg-degen} so every back-degree is at most $\degen(\Gph)$, and apply
Proposition~\ref{ch28:prop-degree}. The bucket peel takes $\Oh(n+m)$ time, and in the greedy pass each edge is inspected once from its later endpoint, taking $\Oh(n+m)$ total time.
\end{proof}

Degeneracy is much smaller than $\Delta(\Gph)$ whenever a graph has high-degree hubs but is globally sparse. A star $K_{1,t}$ (one central hub joined to $t$ degree-$1$ leaves) has $\Delta(K_{1,t})=t$ yet degeneracy $1$ (peel all $t$
leaves first), so Corollary~\ref{ch28:cor-degen} gives $\chi(K_{1,t})\le 2$, matching its true value of $2$. Simple planar graphs are $5$-degenerate (Chapter~26 proved every simple planar graph has a vertex of degree at most $5$, and every subgraph of a planar graph is planar), giving a linear-time $6$-coloring of any planar map in one line.

\end{center}
This $4$-cycle is too small to trip greedy up. The standard worst case is the
\emph{crown graph} $S_k^0$: take the complete bipartite graph $K_{k,k}$ (two sides $\set{u_1,\dots,u_k}$ and $\set{v_1,\dots,v_k}$ with every $u_i$ joined to every $v_j$) and delete the perfect matching $\set{u_1v_1,\dots,u_kv_k}$. It remains bipartite
($\chi(S_k^0)=2$), yet the alternating order $u_1,v_1,u_2,v_2,\dots,u_k,v_k$ forces greedy to spend
$k$ colors on $2k$ vertices. Trace $k=3$: with each pair $u_iv_i$ non-adjacent, $u_i$ is joined to every $v_j$ with $j\neq i$. When greedy reaches $u_i$ (and then $v_i$), their already-colored neighbors are $v_1,\dots,v_{i-1}$
(and $u_1,\dots,u_{i-1}$), which already hold colors $1,\dots,i-1$, forcing both $u_i$ and $v_i$ onto a fresh color $i$. Greedy's performance depends on the ordering, not the graph alone.

In $S_3^0$, each $u_i$ misses only its partner $v_i$; feeding greedy the order

\begin{trapbox}
There \emph{always} exists an ordering on which greedy uses $\chi(\Gph)$
colors: take any optimal coloring and list color class $1$ first, then class $2$,
and so on (greedy never introduces a $(k+1)$-st color because no vertex in class $i$ has a neighbor in class $i$, so it always gets color at most $i$). This observation does not give an algorithm, because finding that ordering is as hard as finding an optimal coloring in the first place.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Bipartite Graphs: Two Colors and a Certificate}
\label{ch28:bipartite}

The simplest case is $\chi(\Gph)\le 2$. As Chapter~1 showed, a graph is $2$-colorable
if and only if it is \emph{bipartite}, with a clean, checkable obstruction:
the odd cycle.

\begin{theorem}[K\H{o}nig's characterization]
\label{ch28:thm-bipartite}
For a graph $\Gph$ the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\arabic*)}]
  \item $\chi(\Gph)\le 2$;
  \item $\Gph$ is bipartite (its vertices split into two independent sets);
  \item $\Gph$ contains no cycle of odd length.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(1)$\Leftrightarrow$(2) is the definition of a $2$-coloring read as a bipartition $(X,Y)$.
For (2)$\Rightarrow$(3): in a bipartition $(X,Y)$ every edge crosses between $X$ and $Y$, so any closed
walk alternates sides and must have even length; an odd cycle cannot exist. For (3)$\Rightarrow$(2): assume $\Gph$ is connected (otherwise treat
each connected component separately). Pick a root $r$, run BFS, and color each vertex $v$ by the parity of its shortest-path
distance $\dist(r,v)$: even distances go to $X$, odd distances to $Y$. Suppose some edge $uv$ joins two
vertices of equal distance parity, and let $z$ be the last shared vertex along the BFS tree paths $r\to u$ and $r\to v$. Those tree subpaths from $z$ to $u$ and $z$ to $v$ meet only at $z$, so together with $uv$ they form a simple cycle of length
\[
  \bigl(\dist(r,u)-\dist(r,z)\bigr) + \bigl(\dist(r,v)-\dist(r,z)\bigr) + 1
  = \dist(r,u) + \dist(r,v) - 2\,\dist(r,z) + 1,
\]
which is odd because $\dist(r,u)+\dist(r,v)$ is even. That contradicts (3), so every edge crosses between $X$ and $Y$.
\end{proof}

The proof is constructive: as Figure~\ref{ch28:fig-bfs-bipartite} shows, BFS two-colors a bipartite graph and, when it fails,
returns an odd cycle as a \emph{certificate} of non-bipartiteness. That
certificate makes ``is this graph $2$-colorable?'' decidable in linear time, whereas Section~\ref{ch28:nphard} shows that testing $3$-colorability is NP-complete.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{TwoColor}$(\Gph)$ -- BFS bipartiteness test and coloring}
\label{ch28:alg-twocolor}
\KwIn{Connected graph $\Gph=(V,E)$.}
\KwOut{A proper $2$-coloring, or an odd cycle.}
$c[v]\leftarrow \bot$ (uncolored) for all $v\in V$; pick a start $s$; $c[s]\leftarrow 0$; queue $Q\leftarrow\set{s}$\;
\While{$Q$ nonempty}{
  $u\leftarrow$ dequeue $Q$\;
  \ForEach{neighbor $w$ of $u$}{
    \uIf{$c[w]=\bot$}{$c[w]\leftarrow 1-c[u]$; enqueue $w$\;}
    \ElseIf{$c[w]=c[u]$}{\Return odd cycle through $u,w$ via BFS-tree paths\;}
  }
}
\Return coloring $c$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{complexitybox}
\textsc{TwoColor} is one BFS: $\Oh(n+m)$ time and $\Oh(n)$ space. It both decides
bipartiteness and outputs a witness either way -- a proper $2$-coloring or an
odd cycle. Beyond two colors, no comparably compact ``no'' witness is known to exist.
\end{complexitybox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Bipartite graphs connect coloring, matching, and spectral graph theory. Theorem~\ref{ch28:thm-bipartite} is the coloring counterpart of \emph{K\H{o}nig's matching theorem} -- in a
bipartite graph the size of a maximum matching equals the size of a minimum vertex cover (a smallest set of vertices touching every edge), proved via max-flow in
Chapter~15. Two-colorability also appears in the eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix $\Adjm$ (the numbers $\lambda$ satisfying $\Adjm x = \lambda x$ for a nonzero vector $x$):
a connected graph is bipartite if and only if its largest adjacency
eigenvalue $\lambda_{\max}$ is mirrored by $-\lambda_{\max}$ as the smallest, with a matching symmetry in the eigenvalues of the Laplacian matrix $\Lap$ (Chapter~32).
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Brooks's Theorem}
\label{ch28:brooks}

The greedy bound $\chi(\Gph)\le\Delta(\Gph)+1$ is tight for complete graphs ($\Delta=n-1$, $\chi=n$) and odd cycles ($\Delta=2$, $\chi=3$). Brooks's
theorem shows these two families are the only connected graphs that require $\Delta(\Gph)+1$ colors;
every other connected graph needs at most $\Delta(\Gph)$.

\begin{theorem}[Brooks, 1941]
\label{ch28:thm-brooks}
Let $\Gph$ be a connected graph with maximum degree $\Delta$. Then
$\chi(\Gph)\le\Delta$ unless $\Gph$ is a complete graph $K_{\Delta+1}$ or an odd
cycle, in which case $\chi(\Gph)=\Delta+1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Why does avoiding those two families save a color? Greedy spends
$\Delta+1$ colors only if some vertex sees all $\Delta$ colors already used among its
neighbors at the moment it is colored. Every earlier vertex can avoid that fate if it still has at least one \emph{uncolored} neighbor saved for later. As for the final vertex $v_n$, if \emph{two} of its neighbors already share a color, its $\Delta$ neighbors occupy at most $\Delta-1$ colors, leaving one free. Only cliques (every pair of neighbors is adjacent, preventing any repeat) and odd cycles (too thin to arrange a repeat) block this plan.
\end{intuitionbox}

Recall from Chapter~10 that a \emph{cut vertex} (articulation point) disconnects a connected graph when removed, and the maximal $2$-connected subgraphs (pieces with no internal cut vertex) are its \emph{blocks}, arranged in a \emph{block-cut tree}. More generally, a graph on more than $k$ vertices is \emph{$k$-connected} if removing any set of fewer than $k$ vertices leaves it connected; a set of $2$ vertices whose removal disconnects a $2$-connected graph is a \emph{$2$-vertex cut}. Finally, a graph is \emph{$\Delta$-regular} if every vertex has degree $\Delta$.

\begin{proof}[Proof (Lov\'asz's ordering argument)]
If $\Delta=0$ or $\Delta=1$, then $\Gph$ is $K_1$ or $K_2$, both complete ($K_{\Delta+1}$). If $\Delta=2$, a connected graph is a path ($\chi\le 2=\Delta$) or a cycle (even cycles have $\chi=2=\Delta$; odd cycles are excluded). Assume from now on that $\Gph$ is connected, not complete, not an odd cycle, and $\Delta\ge 3$.

\textbf{Case A: $\Gph$ is not $\Delta$-regular.} Some vertex $v_n$ has $\deg(v_n)\le\Delta-1$. Build a BFS spanning tree rooted at $v_n$ and order the vertices by
\emph{decreasing} BFS distance from $v_n$, breaking ties arbitrarily, so $v_n$ comes last. Every
$v_i$ with $i<n$ has its BFS parent closer to $v_n$, so that parent comes \emph{later} in the order; hence $v_i$ has at most
$\deg(v_i)-1\le\Delta-1$ earlier neighbors. Vertex $v_n$ itself has at most $\Delta-1$ neighbors in total. Greedy coloring in this
order uses at most $\Delta$ colors.

\textbf{Case B: $\Gph$ is $\Delta$-regular.} Every vertex has degree $\Delta$, so we force two neighbors of the last vertex $v_n$ to share a color. Specifically, we seek a vertex $v_n$ with two non-adjacent
neighbors $a=v_1$ and $b=v_2$ such that $\Gph-\set{a,b}$ stays connected:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{$\Gph$ has a cut vertex.} Then we do not even need $a$ and $b$. In each
        block $B$ of $\Gph$, any cut vertex of $\Gph$ lying in $B$ has neighbors outside $B$ as well, so its degree inside $B$ is strictly less than $\Delta$. Case~A therefore colors every
        block with at most $\Delta$ colors, and we permute color labels block by block along the block-cut tree
        so they agree at each shared cut vertex.
  \item \emph{$\Gph$ is $3$-connected.} Because $\Gph$ is connected and not complete, some
        vertex $v_n$ has two non-adjacent neighbors $a$ and $b$ (otherwise every neighborhood would be a clique, forcing $\Gph$ itself to be complete). Deleting two vertices
        cannot disconnect a $3$-connected graph, so $\Gph-\set{a,b}$ is connected.
  \item \emph{$\Gph$ is $2$-connected but not $3$-connected.} Choose $v_n$ inside a
        $2$-vertex cut, so $\Gph-v_n$ is connected but has at least one cut vertex. Its block-cut
        tree therefore has at least two leaf blocks (blocks containing only one cut vertex of $\Gph-v_n$). Each leaf block must contain a neighbor of
        $v_n$ that is not a cut vertex of $\Gph-v_n$, or else deleting that single cut
        vertex would separate the rest of the leaf block from $\Gph$ even with $v_n$ present, contradicting $2$-connectivity of $\Gph$. Pick $a$ and $b$ as such neighbors of $v_n$ in two different leaf
        blocks; being in distinct blocks away from the cut vertices, $a$ and $b$ are non-adjacent. Neither leaf block can be a single edge, because its non-cut endpoint in $\Gph-v_n$ would have at most one neighbor in the block plus $v_n$, giving degree at most $2<\Delta$ in $\Gph$. Hence each leaf block stays connected after losing $a$ or $b$, keeping $\Gph-v_n-\set{a,b}$ connected. Since $\deg(v_n)=\Delta\ge 3$, vertex $v_n$ has at least one neighbor besides $a$ and $b$, so
        $\Gph-\set{a,b}$ is connected.
\end{itemize}
Inside the connected graph $\Gph-\set{a,b}$, run BFS from $v_n$ and list the remaining vertices $v_3,\dots,v_{n-1}$ in decreasing order of BFS distance from $v_n$, ending with $v_n$.

Now run Algorithm~\ref{ch28:alg-greedy} on $v_1,v_2,v_3,\dots,v_n$. Because $a=v_1$ and $b=v_2$ are non-adjacent, greedy gives both color $1$.
Every intermediate vertex $v_i$ ($3\le i\le n-1$) still has its BFS parent in $\Gph-\set{a,b}$ ahead of it toward $v_n$, so its back-degree in $\Gph$ is at most $\deg(v_i)-1=\Delta-1$ and greedy gives it a color in $\set{1,\dots,\Delta}$. Finally, $v_n$ has
$\Delta$ neighbors, two of which ($a$ and $b$) share color $1$, so at most $\Delta-1$ distinct colors appear on $N(v_n)$ and a color in
$\set{1,\dots,\Delta}$ remains free for $v_n$ (Figure~\ref{ch28:fig-brooks}). Thus $\chi(\Gph)\le\Delta$.
\end{proof}

\section{Edge Coloring and Vizing's Theorem}
\label{ch28:vizing}

Now suppose we color edges instead of vertices. A proper \emph{edge coloring} assigns colors to
edges so that any two edges sharing an endpoint receive different colors; the minimum number of colors needed is the
\emph{chromatic index} $\chi'(\Gph)$. Think of scheduling matches in a
tournament: edges meeting at a vertex are games involving the same player and
cannot run simultaneously, so each color class is a round of non-conflicting games (a \emph{matching}).

\begin{intuitionbox}
Edge coloring a graph $\Gph$ is the same as vertex coloring its \emph{line graph}
$L(\Gph)$, in which each edge of $\Gph$ becomes a vertex and two vertices of $L(\Gph)$ are adjacent if and only if the corresponding edges of $\Gph$
share an endpoint. A vertex of degree $\Delta(\Gph)$ in $\Gph$ has $\Delta(\Gph)$
incident edges, all mutually sharing that endpoint -- a clique of size $\Delta(\Gph)$ in $L(\Gph)$.
That forces $\chi'(\Gph)\ge\Delta(\Gph)$. Vizing's theorem says a simple graph never needs more than
one additional color.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{theorem}[Vizing, 1964]
\label{ch28:thm-vizing}
For every simple graph $\Gph$ (with no self-loops or parallel edges),
\[
  \Delta(\Gph) \le \chi'(\Gph) \le \Delta(\Gph)+1.
\]
\end{theorem}

Simplicity matters: a triangle with each edge doubled has $\Delta=4$ yet $6$ pairwise-touching edges, forcing $\chi'=6$. For simple graphs, the lower bound comes from the $\Delta(\Gph)$ edges meeting at a maximum-degree vertex, and the upper bound is proved by local fan rotations and two-color \emph{Kempe chain} swaps. Graphs achieving $\chi'(\Gph)=\Delta(\Gph)$ are called \emph{Class~1} and those requiring $\Delta(\Gph)+1$ are \emph{Class~2}. Deciding between Class~1 and Class~2 is NP-complete (Holyer, 1981), so Vizing's bound pins $\chi'(\Gph)$ down to two consecutive integers even though telling which one holds is hard.

\begin{proof}[Proof of the upper bound]
We color edges one at a time, maintaining a partial proper edge coloring from the
palette $\set{1,\dots,\Delta+1}$, and show how to color any remaining uncolored edge $xy_0$.

For a vertex $v$, call a color \emph{missing at $v$} if no currently colored edge incident to $v$
carries that color. Because $\deg(v)\le\Delta$ while the palette has $\Delta+1$ colors, every
vertex misses at least one color at every stage.

Starting from the uncolored edge $xy_0$, build a \emph{Vizing fan} at $x$: a maximal sequence of distinct
neighbors $y_0,y_1,\dots,y_k$ of $x$ such that, for each $i\ge 1$, the edge $xy_i$ carries
a color that is \emph{missing at} $y_{i-1}$ (Figure~\ref{ch28:fig-vizing-fan}). Let $\beta$ be a color
missing at $x$ and $\alpha$ a color missing at $y_k$.

\emph{Easy case: $\alpha$ is also missing at $x$.} Rotate the fan by shifting each edge's color one step back, setting
$c(xy_i) \leftarrow c(xy_{i+1})$ for $i=0,\dots,k-1$, and finally setting $c(xy_k)\leftarrow \alpha$ (Figure~\ref{ch28:fig-vizing-fan}). Each shift assigns $xy_i$ a color that was missing at $y_i$ (and newly vacated at $x$), and $\alpha$ was missing at both $x$ and $y_k$, so the extended coloring is proper.

\emph{Hard case: $\alpha$ is already used at $x$}, say on some edge $xu$. If $u$ were outside
the fan, we could append $u$ as $y_{k+1}$ because $\alpha$ is missing at $y_k$, contradicting maximality of the fan. Thus
$u=y_j$ for some $1\le j<k$ ($j\neq k$ since $\alpha$ is missing at $y_k$). By the fan rule,
$\alpha=c(xy_j)$ is missing at $y_{j-1}$. Hence \emph{two} fan vertices,
$y_{j-1}$ and $y_k$, both miss $\alpha$.

Let $P$ be the maximal walk starting at $x$ whose edges alternate between colors $\alpha$ and $\beta$ (a Kempe chain in $\set{\alpha,\beta}$). Because each vertex meets at most one $\alpha$-edge and one $\beta$-edge, $P$ is a simple path; because $\beta$ is missing at $x$, $P$ starts with the $\alpha$-edge $xy_j$. Any vertex missing $\alpha$ meets at most one edge of $P$ (a $\beta$-edge), so it can appear on $P$ only as its far endpoint. A path has only one far endpoint, so $P$ cannot end at both $y_{j-1}$ and $y_k$. Swap colors $\alpha\leftrightarrow\beta$ along $P$ (Figure~\ref{ch28:fig-kempe-swap}). This preserves properness everywhere, frees $\alpha$ at $x$, and alters missing-color sets only at $x$ and the far endpoint of $P$. Moreover, no fan edge before $xy_j$ has color $\alpha$ (already on $xy_j$) or $\beta$ (missing at $x$).
\begin{itemize}
  \item If $P$ does not end at $y_{j-1}$, then $\alpha$ remains missing at
        $y_{j-1}$, and $y_0,\dots,y_{j-1}$ is still a valid fan at $x$ with $\alpha$ now missing at both $x$ and $y_{j-1}$. Rotate $y_0,\dots,y_{j-1}$ and set $c(xy_{j-1})\leftarrow\alpha$.
  \item If $P$ ends at $y_{j-1}$, then $P$ does not reach $y_k$, so $\alpha$ remains missing
        at $y_k$. Along $P$, edge $xy_j$ flipped from $\alpha$ to $\beta$, and the last edge of $P$ at $y_{j-1}$ flipped from $\beta$ to $\alpha$, leaving $\beta$ missing at
        $y_{j-1}$. Thus $y_0,\dots,y_k$ is still a valid fan at $x$, with $\alpha$ now missing at both $x$ and $y_k$. Rotate the full fan and set $c(xy_k)\leftarrow\alpha$.
\end{itemize}
In every case $xy_0$ becomes properly colored. Repeating over all $m$ edges completes the proof.
\end{proof}

That local swap is the edge-coloring cousin of an augmenting path in bipartite matching (Chapter~15).
\end{whybox}

Vizing's proof translates directly into an algorithm: building the fan and tracing one Kempe chain per edge each cost
$\Oh(n)$ time, giving an $\Oh(nm)$ edge-coloring algorithm over $m$ edges.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{VizingEdgeColor}$(\Gph)$ -- $(\Delta+1)$-edge-coloring}
\label{ch28:alg-vizing}
\KwIn{Simple graph $\Gph=(V,E)$ with maximum degree $\Delta$.}
\KwOut{Proper edge coloring with palette $\set{1,\dots,\Delta+1}$.}
\ForEach{edge $e=xy_0\in E$ (in any order)}{
  build a maximal fan $y_0,\dots,y_k$ at $x$\;
  $\beta\leftarrow$ a color missing at $x$;\quad $\alpha\leftarrow$ a color missing at $y_k$\;
  \uIf{$\alpha$ missing at $x$}{rotate the fan and set $c(xy_k)\leftarrow\alpha$\;}
  \Else{
    $y_j\leftarrow$ the fan vertex with $c(xy_j)=\alpha$\;
    swap colors $\alpha,\beta$ along the Kempe chain from $x$\;
    \uIf{the chain ended at $y_{j-1}$}{rotate the whole fan and set $c(xy_k)\leftarrow\alpha$\;}
    \Else{rotate $y_0,\dots,y_{j-1}$ and set $c(xy_{j-1})\leftarrow\alpha$\;}
  }
}
\Return $c$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{complexitybox}
The fan has length at most $\deg(x)\le\Delta$, and the Kempe chain visits at most $n$ vertices.
With a $\Delta+1$-slot color table per vertex, each edge takes $\Oh(n)$ work, so Algorithm~\ref{ch28:alg-vizing} runs in $\Oh(nm)$ time
and uses at most $\Delta+1$ colors -- within one of $\chi'(\Gph)$. On
bipartite graphs the extra color is never needed: \emph{K\H{o}nig's edge-coloring theorem} gives
$\chi'(\Gph)=\Delta(\Gph)$ (every bipartite graph is Class~1), proved either by avoiding odd cycles during two-color swaps or by repeatedly extracting a matching that saturates (includes an incident edge at) every vertex of current maximum degree.
\end{complexitybox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Round-robin scheduling.} Scheduling a round-robin tournament among $n$ players (every
pair plays once, no player plays twice in the same round) in the fewest rounds is edge
coloring $K_n$. For even $n$, $\Delta(K_n)=n-1$ and $K_n$ is Class~1, so $n-1$ rounds
suffice, each round a perfect matching. Organizers build it with the \emph{circle method}: hold player~$n$ fixed and arrange players $1,\dots,n-1$ around a regular polygon, pairing $n$ with the apex and pairing opposite vertices in parallel, then rotating the polygon one step per round. For odd $n$, a matching covers at most $(n-1)/2$ pairs while $K_n$ has $n(n-1)/2$ edges, forcing $\chi'(K_n)=n$ (Class~2, with one player sitting out each round).
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Chordal and Interval Graphs: Optimal Coloring}
\label{ch28:chordal}

Two structured graph families can be colored optimally in
linear time: interval graphs and chordal graphs. On every graph in both families, the chromatic number equals the clique number ($\chi(\Gph)=\omega(\Gph)$).

\begin{definition}[Interval graph]
\label{ch28:def-interval}
A graph is an \emph{interval graph} if each vertex can be represented by a closed
interval on the real line so that two vertices are adjacent if and only if their
intervals overlap.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Chordal graph]
\label{ch28:def-chordal}
A graph is \emph{chordal} (or \emph{triangulated}) if every cycle of length at least $4$ has a
\emph{chord} -- an edge joining two non-consecutive vertices of the cycle. Equivalently,
it contains no induced cycle $C_k$ for $k\ge 4$ (Figure~\ref{ch28:fig-chordal-peo}).
\end{definition}

Every interval graph is chordal: if $I_1,I_2,\dots,I_k,I_1$ were a chordless cycle of length $k\ge 4$, consecutive intervals would advance monotonically along the real line, preventing $I_k$ from reaching back to $I_1$ without overlapping an intermediate interval. Chordal graphs form a strictly larger class -- for example, subdividing all three edges of a star $K_{1,3}$ produces a tree (hence chordal) whose three arms cannot fit along a single line around a hub interval without meeting an outer tip. Chordal graphs can be colored optimally in one greedy pass because they admit an elimination order.

\begin{theorem}[Perfect elimination ordering]
\label{ch28:thm-peo}
A graph $\Gph$ is chordal if and only if it has a \emph{perfect elimination ordering} (PEO): an
ordering $v_1,\dots,v_n$ such that for each $i$, the \emph{later} neighbors of
$v_i$ -- those among $v_{i+1},\dots,v_n$ -- form a clique. A PEO can be computed
in $\Oh(n+m)$ time by \emph{Lexicographic BFS} (Lex-BFS), a variant of BFS that repeatedly visits next an unvisited vertex whose already-visited neighbors came earliest in the visit sequence (breaking ties by the second-earliest visited neighbor, and so on) and then reverses the visit order.
\end{theorem}

\begin{intuitionbox}
A vertex whose neighbors are all mutually adjacent is called \emph{simplicial}.
Every chordal graph has a simplicial vertex, and deleting it leaves a chordal graph. Peeling off simplicial vertices one by one gives the PEO $v_1,\dots,v_n$ directly. In an interval graph, the interval that starts \emph{latest} (largest left endpoint $l_v$) overlaps only remaining intervals that contain the point $x=l_v$, and all such intervals overlap each other at $x=l_v$ -- so it is simplicial. Sorting intervals by decreasing start time is therefore a PEO, and its reverse -- sorting by \emph{increasing start time} -- is the greedy coloring order (symmetrically, increasing finish time is also a PEO, and decreasing finish time is its coloring order).
\end{intuitionbox}

Greedy coloring in the \emph{reverse} of a PEO is always optimal (coloring forward in PEO order can fail; see Chapter~30).

\begin{theorem}[Optimal chordal coloring]
\label{ch28:thm-chordal-color}
Let $v_1,\dots,v_n$ be a PEO of a chordal graph $\Gph$. Running Algorithm~\ref{ch28:alg-greedy} in the reverse
order $v_n,v_{n-1},\dots,v_1$ uses $\omega(\Gph)=\chi(\Gph)$ colors.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Color the vertices in the order $v_n,v_{n-1},\dots,v_1$. When $v_i$ is colored, its
already-colored neighbors are precisely its neighbors in $\set{v_{i+1},\dots,v_n}$ --
its \emph{later} neighbors in the PEO. By Theorem~\ref{ch28:thm-peo}, those later neighbors form a clique $K_i$. Because $K_i\cup\set{v_i}$ is a clique in $\Gph$, we have $\abs{K_i}+1\le\omega(\Gph)$, so $\abs{K_i}\le\omega(\Gph)-1$. At most $\omega(\Gph)-1$ colors are therefore forbidden at $v_i$, and greedy assigns $v_i$ a color in $\set{1,\dots,\omega(\Gph)}$. Thus $\chi(\Gph)\le\omega(\Gph)$; combined with Proposition~\ref{ch28:prop-bounds} ($\chi(\Gph)\ge\omega(\Gph)$), equality $\chi(\Gph)=\omega(\Gph)$ holds and the coloring is optimal.
\end{proof}

\begin{whybox}
When $v_i$ is colored in reverse PEO order, its already-colored neighbors $K_i$ are pairwise adjacent, so they carry $\abs{K_i}$ distinct colors and together with $v_i$ form a clique of size $\abs{K_i}+1$. Every new color $t$ that greedy introduces is immediately certified by a clique of size $t$. Having the clique lower bound and chromatic number coincide on every induced subgraph is the defining property of \emph{perfect graphs} (Chapter~30), of which chordal graphs are a prime example.
\end{whybox}

\end{labbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Interval scheduling / room assignment.} Tasks are intervals in time; two
tasks conflict if and only if they overlap; the minimum number of rooms (or machines) needed
is $\chi(\Gph)$ of the interval graph, which equals the maximum number of tasks
active at any single instant ($\omega(\Gph)$). The rule ``sort tasks by start time and assign each to the lowest-numbered free room'' is reverse-PEO greedy coloring. That is why airport gate
assignment, conference room booking, and contiguous time-slot allocation admit
linear- or $\Oh(n\log n)$-time optimal greedy algorithms, whereas two-dimensional variants (whose conflict graphs are not chordal) become NP-hard.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Coloring Is NP-Hard}
\label{ch28:nphard}

Without chordality, bipartiteness, or low treewidth, graph coloring becomes computationally intractable. Recall that a decision problem lies in $\mathsf{NP}$ if a ``yes'' answer has a certificate checkable in polynomial time, is \emph{NP-hard} if every problem in $\mathsf{NP}$ can be translated to it in polynomial time (a polynomial-time reduction, written $A\le_p B$), and is \emph{NP-complete} if it is both in $\mathsf{NP}$ and NP-hard.

\begin{theorem}[Hardness of coloring]
\label{ch28:thm-nphard}
Deciding whether $\chi(\Gph)\le k$ is NP-complete for every fixed $k\ge 3$. In
particular, \textsc{3-Coloring} is NP-complete. Computing $\chi(\Gph)$ is
NP-hard, and unless $\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}$ no polynomial-time algorithm approximates
$\chi(\Gph)$ on an $n$-vertex graph within a factor of $n^{1-\eps}$ for any constant $\eps>0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Testing $2$-colorability takes linear time, whereas testing $3$-colorability is NP-complete. Once a third color is available, small triangles can pin down ``true,'' ``false,'' and ``neutral'' palette roles, giving enough room to wire Boolean gates and encode \textsc{3-SAT} formulas (conjunctions of three-literal OR clauses such as $(x_1\lor \bar x_2\lor x_3)$).
\end{intuitionbox}

We sketch the polynomial-time reduction $\textsc{3-SAT}\le_p\textsc{3-Coloring}$ (illustrated in Figure~\ref{ch28:fig-3col-reduction}); the complete proof appears in Chapter~37.

\begin{proof}[Proof idea: \textsc{3-SAT}\,$\le_p$\,\textsc{3-Coloring}]
Fix three palette colors named $\mathsf{T}$, $\mathsf{F}$, and $\mathsf{N}$ (true,
false, neutral). Create a base triangle on three vertices $T,F,N$; in any proper $3$-coloring they receive distinct colors, which we name $\mathsf{T},\mathsf{F},\mathsf{N}$. For each Boolean variable $x_i$, add two literal vertices $x_i$ and $\bar x_i$
joined to each other and to $N$. Because $x_i,\bar x_i,N$ form a triangle and $c(N)=\mathsf{N}$, every proper $3$-coloring satisfies
\[
  \set{c(x_i),\,c(\bar x_i)} = \set{\mathsf{T},\,\mathsf{F}},
\]
choosing a truth value for $x_i$ and the opposite value for its negation $\bar x_i$. For each clause $(\ell_1\lor\ell_2\lor\ell_3)$, attach a six-vertex ``OR gadget'' wired to $\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_3$ and to the base triangle so that the gadget extends to a proper $3$-coloring if and only if at least one of $\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_3$ carries color $\mathsf{T}$ (Exercise~\ref{ch28:ex-gadget}). The whole graph is $3$-colorable if and only if the \textsc{3-SAT} formula has a satisfying assignment. Building the graph takes polynomial time, and \textsc{3-Coloring} lies in $\mathsf{NP}$ because a proposed coloring can be checked across all $m$ edges in $\Oh(n+m)$ time.
\end{proof}

\begin{connectionbox}
Three strategies for coping with NP-hard graph problems recur throughout this book.
\emph{Structure:} Graphs of bounded treewidth $\tw(\Gph)$ (measuring how closely $\Gph$ resembles a tree; Chapter~24) admit exact $k$-coloring by dynamic
programming over a tree decomposition in $\Oh^\star(k^{\tw(\Gph)})$ time, where $\Oh^\star$ hides polynomial factors in $n$ (Chapter~25). \emph{Geometry and sparsity:} Every planar graph is
$4$-colorable, though deciding $3$-colorability remains NP-complete even on planar graphs of maximum degree $4$.
\emph{Relaxation:} The \emph{Lov\'asz theta function} $\vartheta(\bar\Gph)$, computed in polynomial time by a semidefinite program (a convex optimization over symmetric matrices with nonnegative eigenvalues), sandwiches $\omega(\Gph)\le\vartheta(\bar\Gph)\le\chi(\Gph)$ (here $\bar\Gph$ is the complement graph, swapping edges and non-edges) and powers approximation algorithms via \emph{vector colorings} that place unit vectors far apart across edges (Chapters~32 and~38).
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Register allocation.} A compiler must map the many temporary
\emph{variables} of a program onto the fixed number $k$ of physical \emph{registers} on the CPU.
Two variables that are simultaneously \emph{live} -- both holding values still needed at a later instruction -- cannot occupy the same register. Build the \emph{interference graph}: one vertex
per variable, with an edge between any two variables whose live ranges overlap. A proper
$k$-coloring assigns every variable to one of $k$ registers without conflict.

Gregory Chaitin (1981) turned degeneracy peeling into a register allocator. Repeatedly remove a
vertex of current degree strictly less than $k$ (fewer than $k$ neighbors guarantee a free register later) and push it onto a stack. If every remaining vertex has degree at least $k$, \emph{spill} one variable to main memory -- delete its vertex from the graph and insert load/store instructions around its uses -- and continue peeling. Finally, pop vertices off the stack and color greedily. When the compiler first puts the program into \emph{Static Single Assignment} (SSA) form -- an intermediate representation where every variable is assigned at only one point in the code -- the live ranges form subtrees of a tree, making the interference graph chordal. On chordal interference graphs, popping the stack is reverse-PEO coloring (Theorem~\ref{ch28:thm-chordal-color}): spilling is needed if and only if more than $k$ variables are live at the exact same program point ($\omega>k$), and register assignment runs optimally in linear time.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Timetabling and frequency assignment.} University exams that share an enrolled student cannot run
in the same time slot: vertices are exams, edges represent shared students, colors are slots, and $\chi(\Gph)$ is the shortest conflict-free exam schedule. Radio transmitters close enough to interfere cannot reuse a channel: vertices are transmitters, edges mark interfering
pairs, and colors are frequencies. Both problems are NP-hard in general, and practical solvers combine the tools of this chapter: greedy coloring on a degeneracy ordering or on a \emph{saturation-degree} (\textsc{DSATUR}, short for \emph{degree of saturation}) ordering -- which dynamically colors next the uncolored vertex adjacent to the greatest number of distinct colors already placed -- together with Brooks-style recoloring and exact chordal coloring along linear corridors.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{What This Made Possible:}
Graph coloring models pairwise conflicts across scheduling, frequency assignment, and compilation.
Brooks's and Vizing's theorems tie vertex and edge colorings tightly to maximum degree $\Delta(\Gph)$,
while Lex-BFS and perfect elimination orderings solve chordal and interval coloring optimally in
linear time. Chaitin's interference-graph allocator put degeneracy peeling directly inside production compilers, and SSA form later connected register allocation to chordal graph theory.

\textbf{Limitations:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Hardness of approximation:} Deciding whether a planar graph of maximum degree $4$
        is $3$-colorable is NP-complete (Garey, Johnson, and Stockmeyer, 1976). On general $n$-vertex graphs,
        $\chi(\Gph)$ cannot be approximated within $n^{1-\eps}$ for any $\eps > 0$ unless
        $\mathsf{P} = \mathsf{NP}$ (Feige--Kilian 1998; Zuckerman 2007).
  \item \emph{Order-sensitivity of greedy heuristics:} As Exercise~\ref{ch28:ex-crown} shows,
        an adversarial ordering forces the greedy algorithm to spend $k$ colors on the $2k$-vertex crown graph $S_k^0$ (half as many colors as vertices) even though $\chi(S_k^0) = 2$.
  \item \emph{Local vs.\ global gap:} A triangle-free graph ($\omega(\Gph) = 2$) can still
        have an arbitrarily large chromatic number $\chi(\Gph)$ (Mycielski's construction, Exercise~\ref{ch28:ex-mycielski}),
        so checking local cliques cannot bound $\chi(\Gph)$ in general.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Where We Go Next:}
A proper coloring partitions $V$ into independent sets. How do we find a single
largest independent set, or a largest clique? \textbf{Chapter~29
(Independent Sets, Cliques, and Vertex Covers)} develops exact branching and pivoting algorithms for those problems.
\textbf{Chapter~30 (Perfect Graphs)} then characterizes the graphs on which $\chi(H)=\omega(H)$ holds for every induced subgraph $H$.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch28:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Greedy adversary]
\label{ch28:ex-crown}
The \emph{crown graph} $S_k^0$ is $K_{k,k}$ with a perfect matching removed ($2k$ vertices in total). Prove
$\chi(S_k^0)=2$, then exhibit a vertex order on which Algorithm~\ref{ch28:alg-greedy} uses
$k$ colors. Conclude that the gap between greedy's worst order and $\chi(\Gph)$ is
unbounded.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Degeneracy versus max degree]
\label{ch28:ex-degen}
Show $\degen(\Gph)\le\Delta(\Gph)$ always, and construct, for each $d\ge 1$, a graph with
$\degen(\Gph)=d$ and $\Delta(\Gph)$ arbitrarily large. Then prove every simple planar graph is
$5$-degenerate (use Euler's edge bound $m\le 3n-6$ for $n\ge 3$, which implies average degree strictly below $6$), and
deduce the six-color theorem for planar graphs.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Odd-cycle certificate]
\label{ch28:ex-oddcycle}
Modify Algorithm~\ref{ch28:alg-twocolor} so that, on failure, it outputs an explicit odd cycle
(not solely the conflicting edge). Prove your reconstruction is correct and runs in
$\Oh(n+m)$ time. Why does this short ``no'' witness place non-$2$-colorability in $\mathsf{NP}$ (equivalently, $2$-colorability in $\mathsf{co\text{-}NP}$, the class of problems whose ``no'' instances have polynomial-time checkable certificates)?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Brooks by hand]
\label{ch28:ex-brooks}
Take the $3$-cube graph $Q_3$ (whose $8$ vertices are binary strings of length $3$, with edges between strings differing in one bit; $\Delta=3$, $3$-regular, bipartite). Brooks's theorem
guarantees $\chi(Q_3)\le 3$; bipartiteness gives $\chi(Q_3)=2$. Reconcile these two statements and
exhibit the $2$-coloring. Then find a connected $3$-regular graph other than the prism of Figure~\ref{ch28:fig-brooks-example} that is \emph{not}
bipartite and color it with $3$ colors using the ordering from the proof of
Theorem~\ref{ch28:thm-brooks}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Vizing classes]
\label{ch28:ex-vizing}
Compute $\chi'(\Gph)$ for $K_4$, $K_5$, $C_5$, and the Petersen graph (the $10$-vertex, $3$-regular graph consisting of an outer $5$-cycle $u_0\dots u_4$, an inner pentagram joining $v_i$ to $v_{(i+2)\bmod 5}$, and five spoke edges $u_iv_i$). Which are Class~1
($\chi'=\Delta$) and which are Class~2 ($\chi'=\Delta+1$)? Prove that every connected
$\Delta$-regular graph on an \emph{odd} number of vertices is Class~2. (Hint: each
color class is a matching, which saturates an even number of vertices.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Line graphs and edge coloring]
\label{ch28:ex-linegraph}
Prove $\chi'(\Gph)=\chi(L(\Gph))$ where $L(\Gph)$ is the line graph of $\Gph$. Using Vizing's theorem, deduce
$\chi(L(\Gph))\le\Delta(\Gph)+1$. Give an example where $L(\Gph)$ contains an induced odd cycle of length at least $5$ (so $\omega(H)<\chi(H)$ on that induced subgraph $H$, meaning $L(\Gph)$ is not perfect), showing that the tight bound on $\chi(L(\Gph))$ is specific to line graphs rather than a consequence of perfection.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Simplicial vertices]
\label{ch28:ex-simplicial}
Prove that every chordal graph with at least two vertices has at least two
simplicial vertices, and that if the graph is not complete the two can be chosen
non-adjacent. Use this fact to give an inductive proof of
Theorem~\ref{ch28:thm-chordal-color} directly, without invoking Lex-BFS.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Interval coloring is room scheduling]
\label{ch28:ex-interval}
Given $n$ closed intervals on the real line, design an $\Oh(n\log n)$-time algorithm that assigns each interval a color
(room) using $\omega$ colors, where $\omega$ is the maximum number of intervals overlapping at a single point. Prove
optimality by outputting a point stabbed by $\omega$ intervals. Where does this
argument break down if the intervals are arcs around a circle rather than segments on a
line?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Mycielski's triangle-free tower]
\label{ch28:ex-mycielski}
For a graph $\Gph=(V,E)$, the \emph{Mycielskian} $M(\Gph)$ keeps $\Gph$, adds for each vertex $v\in V$ a twin vertex $v'$ joined to every neighbor of $v$ in $\Gph$ (with no edge $vv'$ and no edges among twins), and adds one apex vertex $w$ joined to all twins $v'$. Prove that $M(\Gph)$ is triangle-free
whenever $\Gph$ is, and that $\chi(M(\Gph))=\chi(\Gph)+1$. Starting from $K_2$, apply $M$ twice to build a
triangle-free graph with $\chi=4$ and report its vertex count.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[3-coloring gadget]
\label{ch28:ex-gadget}
Design the six-vertex OR gadget used in Theorem~\ref{ch28:thm-nphard}: a graph wired to the
palette triangle $(T,F,N)$ and to three literal vertices $\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_3$ that extends to a proper $3$-coloring if and only if at least one of $\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_3$ is colored $\mathsf{T}$. Verify your gadget across all $8$ truth
assignments to the three literals, and check that the full reduction runs in time polynomial in the formula size.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Register allocation with spilling]
\label{ch28:ex-regalloc}
Implement Chaitin-style allocation: given an interference graph and $k$ registers,
repeatedly remove a vertex of degree strictly less than $k$ and push it onto a stack; if every remaining vertex has degree at least $k$, spill a
highest-degree vertex; finally pop the stack and color greedily. Prove that if the interference graph
is chordal, the stuck case never arises when $k\ge\omega(\Gph)$, so no spilling occurs
and setting $k=\omega(\Gph)$ colors the graph optimally. Connect this stack order to Theorem~\ref{ch28:thm-chordal-color}.
\end{exercise}
