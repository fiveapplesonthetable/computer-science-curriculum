\chapter{Planar Graphs and Planarity Testing}

In the preceding chapters, bounding solution size or treewidth made NP-hard problems
tractable. Geometry can restrict a graph's structure too. Some graphs can be drawn on a flat
sheet of paper without any two edges crossing, and some cannot. Graphs that admit a
crossing-free drawing (\emph{planar} graphs) have at most $3n - 6$ edges on $n$ vertices,
can have their vertices colored with four colors so no edge joins matching colors, and can
be split into balanced pieces by deleting only $\Oh(\sqrt{n})$ vertices. All of these
properties trace back to a single counting identity discovered on three-dimensional solids:
in any connected flat drawing, the number of vertices minus the number of edges plus the
number of enclosed regions equals $2$.

\begin{historybox}
The mathematics of planar graphs originated in polyhedral geometry. In 1750, Leonhard
Euler wrote to Christian Goldbach stating his polyhedral formula: every convex polyhedron
(a flat-faced solid with no dents, such as a cube or pyramid) satisfies $V - E + F = 2$,
where $V$, $E$, and $F$ count its vertices, edges, and faces. Euler checked it on pyramids,
prisms, and the five regular Platonic solids, though he lacked formal definitions of faces
and surfaces. In 1813, Augustin-Louis Cauchy published a proof by removing one face and
flattening the remaining surface into the plane. Cauchy tacitly assumed faces are simple
flat patches without holes and that removing them preserves connectivity. Beginning in 1812,
Simon Lhuilier found counterexamples: polyhedra with tunnels (such as a hollow picture frame)
satisfy $V - E + F = 0$, establishing that the alternating sum depends on the \emph{genus}
(the number of donut-like holes in the surface). This historical struggle inspired Imre
Lakatos's philosophical study \emph{Proofs and Refutations}.

Map coloring opened a second line of inquiry. In 1852, Francis Guthrie conjectured that the
regions of any planar map can be colored with four colors so neighboring regions receive
different colors. Alfred Kempe claimed a proof in 1879 using \emph{Kempe chains} (paths
alternating between two colors to swap color choices locally). In 1890, Percy Heawood found
a flaw in Kempe's two-chain swap, while salvaging a single-chain swap to prove the Five
Color Theorem. The Four Color Theorem was finally proved in 1976 by Kenneth Appel and
Wolfgang Haken, who showed that every planar graph contains at least one of $1{,}936$ local
subgraphs (\emph{reducible configurations}) around which any coloring of the remaining graph
can be patched, verified using $1{,}200$ hours of computer time. Because no human could
check the computer output by hand, the proof sparked debate over mathematical proof itself.
Neil Robertson, Daniel Sanders, Paul Seymour, and Robin Thomas trimmed the list to $633$
configurations in 1997, and Georges Gonthier checked the full proof inside the Coq
proof-checking software in 2005, though a short hand-checkable proof is still unknown.

Algorithmic planarity developed in parallel. In 1930, Kazimierz Kuratowski characterized
planar graphs by forbidden subgraphs: no subdivisions of the five-vertex complete graph
$K_5$ or the $3$-by-$3$ complete bipartite graph $K_{3,3}$, where a subdivision replaces
edges with internally vertex-disjoint paths. Klaus Wagner proved the edge-contraction
analogue in 1937, a precursor to Robertson and Seymour's Graph Minor Theorem (1983--2004;
see Chapter~24). Searching directly for Kuratowski subgraphs was exponential. In 1974, John
Hopcroft and Robert Tarjan gave the first $\Oh(n)$ planarity test using depth-first search
(DFS) to embed paths incrementally, work cited in their 1986 Association for Computing
Machinery (ACM) Turing Award. John Boyer and Wendy Myrvold (2004) later designed an
edge-addition method that is simpler to implement and extracts an explicit $K_5$ or
$K_{3,3}$ subdivision whenever a graph is not planar.
\end{historybox}

\section{Drawings, embeddings, and faces}
\label{ch26:setup}

Throughout, $\Gph=(V,E)$ is a finite undirected graph with $n=\abs{V}$ vertices and
$m=\abs{E}$ edges. We forbid self-loops and, unless stated otherwise, parallel edges (two
or more edges between the same pair of vertices); adding either cannot make a non-planar
graph planar and complicates the edge counts.

\begin{definition}[Planar drawing]
\label{ch26:def-drawing}
A \emph{drawing} of $\Gph$ in the plane $\R^2$ maps each vertex to a distinct point and
each edge $\{u,v\}$ to a simple (non-self-intersecting) curve joining the images of $u$
and $v$, such that the curve meets no other vertex and any two curves meet only at
shared endpoints. A graph is \emph{planar} if it admits such a drawing; a fixed crossing-free
drawing is a \emph{plane graph}, or a \emph{planar embedding}.
\end{definition}

\begin{intuitionbox}
``Planar'' is a property of the abstract graph: \emph{can it be drawn flat without
crossings?} A ``plane graph'' is one chosen flat drawing. The same planar graph can have
different embeddings, much as a cardboard box can be flattened in different ways. Crossings
in a hasty sketch belong to \emph{that} drawing, not to the graph. Figure~\ref{fig:ch26-k4-embeddings}
shows the four-vertex complete graph $K_4$ (every pair of vertices joined by an edge): drawn
as a square with two diagonals it has a crossing, yet moving vertex~$4$ inside triangle
$1\text{--}2\text{--}3$ removes the crossing.
\end{intuitionbox}

A plane graph cuts the plane into connected open regions called \emph{faces}. Exactly one
region extends to infinity in all directions: the unbounded \emph{outer} (or infinite) face.
The boundary of a face is a closed walk along edges. Most edges separate two distinct faces,
whereas a \emph{bridge} (an edge whose deletion increases the number of connected components;
see Chapter~10) has the same face on both sides and is traversed twice along that face's
boundary walk, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ch26-faces-degrees}.

\begin{definition}[Face and its degree]
\label{ch26:def-face}
Let $H$ be a plane graph. A \emph{face} is a connected component of $\R^2\setminus H$
(the plane minus all drawn vertices and edges). The \emph{degree} $\deg(f)$ of a face
$f$ is the number of edge traversals on its boundary walk, counting an edge twice when both
of its sides touch $f$.
\end{definition}

\begin{focusbox}
Three counts describe every connected plane graph: $n$ vertices, $m$ edges, and $f$ faces.
Euler's formula links them by $n - m + f = 2$, so any two of these counts fix the third.
\end{focusbox}

Counting the two sides of every edge gives a face-degree identity. As you walk along an
edge, one face lies on your left and one on your right (possibly the same face twice when
the edge is a bridge), so summing face degrees counts every edge twice:
\[
  \sum_{f} \deg(f) \;=\; 2m. \tag{$\star$}
\]
This is the face-counting counterpart of the handshake lemma $\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v)=2m$
from Chapter~1.

\section{Euler's formula}
\label{ch26:euler}

\begin{theorem}[Euler's formula]
\label{ch26:thm-euler}
For any connected plane graph with $n$ vertices, $m$ edges, and $f$ faces,
\[
  n - m + f = 2.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Grow the graph one edge at a time and track $n - m + f$, as illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:ch26-euler-induction}. Start from a single vertex: $n=1$, $m=0$, $f=1$
(the outer face alone), so $1-0+1=2$. Now add edges one by one. Every new edge either
reaches a brand-new vertex (a \emph{tree} edge) or joins two existing vertices and closes
a cycle (a \emph{cycle} edge). A tree edge increases $n$ and $m$ by $1$ each, leaving
$n-m+f$ unchanged. A cycle edge splits one face into two, increasing $m$ and $f$ by $1$
each and again leaving $n-m+f$ unchanged. The alternating sum starts at $2$ and stays $2$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{proof}
Induct on $m$. \emph{Base.} If $m=0$, connectedness forces $\Gph$ to be a single vertex, so
$n=1$, $f=1$, and $1-0+1=2$.

\emph{Step.} Take a connected plane graph with $m\ge 1$ edges and pick any edge $e$.

\emph{Case 1: $e$ lies on a cycle.} Together with the rest of that cycle, $e$ forms a
simple closed curve in $\R^2$. By the \emph{Jordan curve theorem} (every simple closed
curve in the plane partitions the remaining plane into one bounded interior region and one
unbounded exterior region), the two sides of $e$ lie in distinct faces. Deleting $e$ merges
those two faces into one and keeps the graph connected. The resulting plane graph has $n$
vertices, $m-1$ edges, and $f-1$ faces; by the induction hypothesis,
$n-(m-1)+(f-1)=2$, so $n-m+f=2$.

\emph{Case 2: $e$ lies on no cycle (a bridge).} Then $e$ has the same face on both sides,
so deleting $e$ leaves the face count unchanged in $\Gph$, while splitting $\Gph$ into two
connected plane components with $n_1+n_2=n$ vertices, $m_1+m_2=m-1$ edges, and $f_1,f_2$
faces when each component is viewed in the plane on its own. Because both components share
the single unbounded outer face of $\Gph-e$, that outer face is counted once in $f$ and
once in each of $f_1$ and $f_2$, giving $f_1+f_2 = f+1$. Applying the induction hypothesis
$n_i - m_i + f_i = 2$ to each component ($i=1,2$) and summing gives
\begin{align*}
  (n_1 + n_2) - (m_1 + m_2) + (f_1 + f_2) &= 2 + 2 \\
  n - (m - 1) + (f + 1) &= 4 \\
  n - m + f &= 2. \qedhere
\end{align*}
\end{proof}

\begin{whybox}
The induction runs on \emph{a chosen} drawing, yet solving for $f$ gives $f = 2 - n + m$,
which depends only on $n$ and $m$. Thus the number of faces is an \emph{invariant} of a
connected planar graph (a quantity that takes the same value in every valid planar
embedding).
\end{whybox}

\subsection{The sparsity bounds}

Combined with face-degree counting, Euler's formula caps how many edges a simple planar
graph can have.

\begin{theorem}[Edge bound for simple planar graphs]
\label{ch26:thm-edgebound}
If $\Gph$ is simple, planar, and connected with $n\ge 3$, then
\[
  m \le 3n - 6.
\]
If in addition $\Gph$ is bipartite (or more generally triangle-free, meaning its
\emph{girth}, the length of its shortest cycle, is at least $4$), then
\[
  m \le 2n - 4.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix a plane embedding with $f$ faces. Because $\Gph$ is simple, connected, and has $n\ge 3$
vertices, every face $f$ satisfies $\deg(f)\ge 3$: if the boundary of $f$ contains a cycle,
that cycle has at least $3$ edges because $\Gph$ has no self-loops or parallel edges; if
the boundary contains no cycle, $\Gph$ is a tree with $m = n - 1 \ge 2$ bridges each
walked twice around the unique face, giving $\deg(f) = 2m \ge 4$. Summing $\deg(f)\ge 3$
over all $f$ faces and applying $(\star)$ yields
\[
  2m \;=\; \sum_{f} \deg(f) \;\ge\; 3f,
\]
so $f \le \frac{2}{3}m$. Substituting this into Euler's formula $f = 2 - n + m$ gives
\begin{align*}
  2 - n + m &\le \frac{2}{3}m \\
  \frac{1}{3}m &\le n - 2 \\
  m &\le 3n - 6.
\end{align*}
If $\Gph$ is bipartite (or triangle-free), it has no $3$-cycles, so every face boundary
either contains a cycle of length at least $4$ or traverses at least two bridges twice;
either way, $\deg(f)\ge 4$ for every face. Then $2m \ge 4f$, so $f \le \frac{1}{2}m$, and
Euler's formula gives
\[
  2 - n + m \;\le\; \frac{1}{2}m \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m \;\le\; 2n - 4. \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Two non-planar graphs]
\label{ch26:cor-k5k33}
The complete graph $K_5$ ($5$ vertices with all $\binom{5}{2}=10$ edges) and the complete
bipartite graph $K_{3,3}$ (two vertex sets of size $3$ with all $3\cdot 3 = 9$ cross-edges)
are not planar.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ch26-k5-k33}, $K_5$ is simple and connected with $n=5$ and
$m=10$, violating $m \le 3n-6 = 9$. Likewise, $K_{3,3}$ is simple, connected, and bipartite
with $n=6$ and $m=9$, violating the bipartite bound $m \le 2n-4 = 8$.
\end{proof}

\begin{trapbox}
The bound $m\le 3n-6$ is necessary, not sufficient. A graph can satisfy it and still be
non-planar: attach a long path to $K_5$ and the average degree drops well below the
threshold while the $K_5$ subgraph keeps the graph non-planar. Sparsity rules graphs
\emph{out}; it never rules them \emph{in}. Also, $m\le 3n-6$ requires a \emph{simple}
graph with $n\ge 3$ (for $n=2$, $m=1$ gives $3n-6=0 < 1$). Finally, Euler's count assumes
connectivity; for a plane graph with $c$ connected components, $n-m+f = 1+c$ (Exercise~2).
\end{trapbox}

\begin{corollary}[A low-degree vertex always exists]
\label{ch26:cor-degree5}
Every simple planar graph has a vertex of degree at most $5$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For $n < 3$ every vertex has degree at most $1$. For $n \ge 3$, apply
Theorem~\ref{ch26:thm-edgebound} to each connected component (or to $\Gph$ after adding
edges to connect components while preserving planarity) to obtain $m \le 3n - 6$. If every
vertex had $\deg(v) \ge 6$, the handshake lemma would give
\[
  2m \;=\; \sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) \;\ge\; 6n \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m \;\ge\; 3n \;>\; 3n - 6,
\]
contradicting $m \le 3n - 6$.
\end{proof}

This guarantee drives both the five-color theorem and linear-time planar algorithms: every
planar graph has a vertex of degree at most $5$ to remove, so recursive algorithms that
peel off one low-degree vertex at a time shrink the graph at constant cost per step.

\begin{labbox}
Check Euler's counts on the cube graph $Q_3$ (the $8$ corners and $12$ edges of a
three-dimensional cube, so $n=8$ and $m=12$). Euler's formula gives
$f = 2 - 8 + 12 = 6$ faces: five bounded $4$-cycles plus one square face stretched out as
the unbounded outer face when the cube is flattened into the plane. The edge bound holds
($m = 12 \le 3n-6 = 18$), and $(\star)$ checks out because all $6$ faces are $4$-cycles:
$\sum_f\deg(f) = 6\cdot 4 = 24 = 2m$.
\end{labbox}

\section{Duality}
\label{ch26:duality}

Placing a vertex inside every face of a plane graph and connecting faces across shared
edges produces a companion plane graph called the \emph{dual}. To tell the two apart, we
call the starting plane graph $H$ the \emph{primal} graph.

\begin{definition}[Dual graph]
\label{ch26:def-dual}
Let $H$ be a connected plane graph. Its \emph{planar dual} $H^\ast$ has one vertex $f^\ast$
for each face $f$ of $H$, and for each edge $e$ of $H$ a dual edge $e^\ast$ joining the
vertices of the two faces that $e$ borders (forming a self-loop at $f^\ast$ when $e$ is a
bridge with the same face $f$ on both sides).
\end{definition}

planar dual $H^\ast$.

Each primal edge crosses only its own dual edge, so $H^\ast$ is itself a connected plane
multigraph with $m^\ast = m$ edges, $n^\ast = f$ vertices, and $f^\ast = n$ faces. Euler's
formula $n - m + f = 2$ is unchanged when $n$ and $f$ swap places. Repeating the
construction returns the original plane graph, $(H^\ast)^\ast = H$ (an operation that is
its own inverse is called an \emph{involution}). Even when $H$ is simple, $H^\ast$ can
contain self-loops and parallel edges: a bridge of $H$ becomes a self-loop of $H^\ast$, and
two faces of $H$ that share $k \ge 2$ boundary edges produce $k$ parallel edges in $H^\ast$.

\begin{connectionbox}
Duality links planar graphs to the cut and flow algorithms of Chapters~11--12. When $H$ and
$H^\ast$ are drawn together as in Figure~\ref{ch26:fig-dual-graph}, each primal vertex $v$
sits inside a dual face $v^\ast$. A minimal set of primal edges whose removal disconnects
$s$ from $t$ corresponds to a simple cycle of dual edges in $H^\ast$ separating the dual
face $s^\ast$ from the dual face $t^\ast$ (Exercise~12). Finding a minimum-weight $s$--$t$
cut in a planar graph therefore reduces to finding a shortest separating cycle in $H^\ast$
(Reif; Hassin), which explains why planar max-flow and min-cut run in near-linear time
rather than the $\Oh(nm)$ bound for general graphs.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{remark}
The dual depends on the chosen planar embedding rather than the abstract graph alone. A
graph is \emph{$3$-connected} if it has at least four vertices and remains connected after
deleting any one or two vertices. By a theorem of Hassler Whitney (1932), every
$3$-connected planar graph has a unique planar embedding (up to choosing which face is the
outer face and reflecting the plane) and therefore a unique dual. Planar graphs of lower
connectivity can admit embeddings with different face structures and non-isomorphic duals.
\end{remark}

\section{The forbidden minors: Kuratowski and Wagner}
\label{ch26:kuratowski}

Corollary~\ref{ch26:cor-k5k33} shows that $K_5$ and $K_{3,3}$ are non-planar. The converse
also holds: every non-planar graph contains a copy of $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$ hidden inside it.
Two standard notions make ``hidden inside'' precise, and for planarity they coincide.

\begin{definition}[Subdivision]
A \emph{subdivision} of a graph $H$ replaces one or more edges of $H$ by paths that share
no internal vertices (inserting new degree-$2$ vertices along those edges). The original
vertices of $H$ are the \emph{branch vertices} of the subdivision. Subdividing edges never
changes whether a graph is planar.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Minor]
A graph $H$ is a \emph{minor} of $\Gph$ if $H$ can be obtained from a subgraph of $\Gph$ by
contracting edges (deleting an edge $\{x,y\}$ and merging $x$ and $y$ into one vertex,
discarding any resulting self-loops or duplicate parallel edges; see Chapter~24).
Equivalently, $H$ is a minor of $\Gph$ if and only if $\Gph$ contains $\abs{V(H)}$
pairwise disjoint connected vertex sets (called \emph{branch sets}), one per vertex of $H$,
with at least one edge of $\Gph$ between two branch sets whenever $H$ has the corresponding
edge.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Kuratowski 1930; Wagner 1937]
\label{ch26:thm-kuratowski}
A graph $\Gph$ is planar if and only if it contains \textbf{no subgraph that is a
subdivision} of $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$ (Kuratowski). Equivalently, $\Gph$ is planar if and
only if it has \textbf{no minor} isomorphic to $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$, meaning identical to
$K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$ up to relabeling vertices (Wagner).
\end{theorem}

an edge into a path of degree-$2$ vertices, whereas taking a minor contracts connected
branch sets back into single vertices.

\begin{intuitionbox}
A \emph{subdivision} embeds $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$ as $5$ or $6$ branch vertices joined by
internally disjoint paths. A \emph{minor} is looser: each vertex of $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$ can
expand into a connected subgraph (a branch set) rather than a single vertex. Every
subdivision of $H$ is automatically an $H$-minor (contract the internal path edges). For
general $H$ the reverse can fail, because a branch set may split high-degree connections
across several hubs; for $K_{3,3}$ (maximum degree $3$) any minimal branch set shrinks to
a single hub vertex, and for $K_5$ (degree $4$) a split hub produces a $K_{3,3}$
subdivision instead. Thus avoiding $K_5$ and $K_{3,3}$ minors is equivalent to avoiding
$K_5$ and $K_{3,3}$ subdivisions.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{whybox}
\emph{Why necessity is easy and sufficiency is hard.} The ``only if'' direction follows from
Corollary~\ref{ch26:cor-k5k33} together with the fact that deleting vertices or edges,
subdividing edges, and contracting edges all preserve planarity (contracting an edge in a
plane drawing pulls its two endpoints together along the drawn curve without creating
crossings). Hence no planar graph can contain a $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$ subdivision or minor.
The ``if'' direction --- proving that avoiding these two patterns \emph{guarantees} a
crossing-free drawing --- does the real work. Modern proofs reduce to $3$-connected graphs:
by a theorem of William Tutte (1963), every $3$-connected planar graph admits a convex
straight-line drawing (every edge is a straight segment and every bounded face is a convex
polygon), and induction on edge contractions shows that every $3$-connected graph with no
$K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$ minor reduces to $K_4$ and is therefore planar. Graphs of lower
connectivity are handled by gluing planar embeddings across cut vertices and separating
vertex pairs (pairs of vertices whose deletion disconnects a $2$-connected block).
\end{whybox}

We omit the $3$-connected contraction case analysis, and record the connectivity reduction
because planarity-testing algorithms use the same decomposition.

\begin{lemma}[Reduction to $3$-connected pieces]
\label{ch26:lem-3conn}
A graph $\Gph$ is planar if and only if each of its biconnected blocks (maximal
$2$-connected subgraphs; see Chapter~10) is planar, and a $2$-connected graph is planar if
and only if each $3$-connected component in its triconnected decomposition is planar.
\end{lemma}

Planarity is therefore local to the connectivity pieces: you can test each block
independently, and within a block test each $3$-connected component. A $2$-connected
graph's decomposition along separating pairs of vertices is often stored in an \emph{SPQR
tree} (a tree of Series, Parallel, single-edge Q, and Rigid $3$-connected skeletons);
planar embeddings of the pieces glue back together across cut vertices and across
separating pairs (where each piece can flip across its two attachment vertices).

\section{Planarity testing}
\label{ch26:testing}

Could we test planarity by searching directly for a $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$ subdivision? Brute
force over all candidate branch vertices and paths takes exponential time. Three
linear-time families exist; we develop the path-addition strategy in detail and summarize
how its linear-time refinements work.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Goal.} Decide in $\Oh(n)$ time whether $\Gph$ is planar and, if so, output a
\emph{combinatorial embedding}: the clockwise cyclic order of incident edges around each
vertex (which uniquely fixes all face boundaries of each connected component). By
Theorem~\ref{ch26:thm-edgebound}, any simple $n$-vertex planar graph with $n\ge 3$ satisfies
$m\le 3n-6$, so if $m > 3n-6$ we immediately reject. Thereafter $m = \Oh(n)$, making
$\Oh(n)$ and $\Oh(m)$ interchangeable.
\end{focusbox}

\subsection{Intuition: add one path at a time}

The path-addition method (Auslander and Parter, refined to $\Oh(n)$ time by Hopcroft and
Tarjan) starts by running DFS to get a spanning tree and back edges. Any back edge together
with its tree path forms a cycle $C$. Once $C$ is drawn as a simple closed curve in the
plane, every remaining connected piece of $\Gph - C$ must go either completely inside $C$
or completely outside $C$.

Two pieces conflict when they cannot lie on the same side of $C$ without crossing. Recording
each piece as a vertex and adding an edge between every pair of conflicting pieces gives an
auxiliary \emph{interlacement graph} $I$, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:ch26-interlacement}.
Assigning pieces to the inside or outside of $C$ is then a proper $2$-coloring of $I$
(Chapter~1): conflicting pieces must receive opposite sides. The pieces fit around $C$
without crossings if and only if $I$ is bipartite (contains no odd cycle).

\subsection{A clean recursive scheme}

Algorithm~\ref{ch26:alg-planartest} states the Auslander--Parter--Goldstein recursion. It
runs in $\Oh(n^3)$ time as written; Hopcroft--Tarjan's linear-time algorithm executes the
same conflicts in a single DFS pass. Relative to a cycle $C$, a \emph{$C$-piece}
(traditionally called a \emph{bridge of $C$}, distinct from a single cut-edge of Chapter~10)
is either a \emph{chord} (one edge outside $C$ with both endpoints on $C$) or a connected
component of $\Gph - V(C)$ together with all edges joining that component to $C$. One corner
case needs care: if $C$ has only a single $C$-piece $B_1$ and $B_1$ is not a single chord,
then $B_1 \cup C$ is the entire graph $\Gph$, so recursing directly would not shrink the
input. Because $\Gph$ is biconnected, $B_1$ contains a path between two attachment vertices
on $C$; swapping one arc of $C$ for that path produces a new cycle $C'$ that splits $B_1$
into at least two smaller pieces, ensuring every recursive call gets a strictly smaller
graph.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{PlanarTest}($\Gph$) -- path-addition skeleton}
\label{ch26:alg-planartest}
\KwIn{a biconnected simple graph $\Gph$ (run separately on each biconnected block).}
\KwOut{\textsc{Planar} or \textsc{NonPlanar}.}
\If{$n \ge 3$ \textbf{and} $m > 3n-6$}{\Return \textsc{NonPlanar}\tcp*{sparsity gate, Thm~\ref{ch26:thm-edgebound}}}
\If{$\Gph$ is a single cycle or has $\le 2$ edges}{\Return \textsc{Planar}}
Find a cycle $C$ in $\Gph$ (rerouting $C$ if needed so non-chord pieces shrink)\;
Let $B_1,\dots,B_k$ be the $C$-pieces of $\Gph$ outside $C$\;
\ForEach{non-chord piece $B_i$}{
  \lIf{\textup{\textsc{PlanarTest}}$(B_i \cup C) = \textup{\textsc{NonPlanar}}$}{\Return \textsc{NonPlanar}}
}
Build the \emph{interlacement graph} $I$: one vertex per piece $B_i$, and an edge $\{B_i,B_j\}$ whenever $B_i$ and $B_j$ interlace on $C$\;
\eIf{$I$ is bipartite}{
  \Return \textsc{Planar}\tcp*{$2$-coloring gives inside/outside sides}
}{
  \Return \textsc{NonPlanar}\tcp*{odd cycle in $I$ yields $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$}
}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{whybox}
\emph{Why bipartiteness of $I$ captures planarity around $C$.}
Each piece $B_i$ is connected outside $C$, so in any crossing-free drawing of $\Gph$ all
of $B_i$ must lie on one side of the simple closed curve $C$. Testing $B_i \cup C$
recursively verifies that $B_i$ can be drawn on a single side of $C$ without internal
crossings. Two pieces placed on the same side can be nested without crossing if and only if
their attachment vertices around $C$ do not interlace. Therefore a crossing-free placement
of all pieces exists if and only if $I$ admits a proper $2$-coloring, which by Chapter~1
holds if and only if $I$ is bipartite. When $I$ has an odd cycle, minimal conflicting pieces
around $C$ trace out a subdivision of $K_{3,3}$ or $K_5$, giving an algorithmic proof of
Kuratowski's theorem.
\end{whybox}

\begin{complexitybox}
The direct recursion runs in $\Oh(n^3)$ time (or $\Oh(n^2)$ with careful graph reuse):
there are $\Oh(n)$ recursive calls, each constructing and $2$-coloring an interlacement
graph of up to $\Oh(n)$ pieces. The \textbf{Hopcroft--Tarjan} algorithm (1974) processes
paths in DFS order while maintaining conflicting inside/outside blocks on a stack, running
in $\Oh(n)$ time. The \textbf{vertex-addition} method of Lempel, Even, and Cederbaum,
accelerated with Booth and Lueker's \emph{PQ trees} (trees of P-nodes that allow arbitrary
child permutations and Q-nodes that allow only reversals, compactly representing valid
frontier vertex orders), also achieves $\Oh(n)$ time. The \textbf{Boyer--Myrvold} algorithm
(2004) embeds DFS back-edges directly into adjacency lists in $\Oh(n)$ time and, on failure,
extracts a $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$ subdivision as a \emph{certificate} (an independently
checkable witness of non-planarity).
\end{complexitybox}

\begin{trapbox}
Two implementation pitfalls stand out. (1) \emph{Skipping the $m \le 3n-6$ check.} On a
dense graph with $m = \Th(n^2)$ edges, building interlacement graphs without first rejecting
$m > 3n-6$ turns an $\Oh(n)$ algorithm into $\Om(n^2)$ or worse; check the edge count
before touching the edges. (2) \emph{Skipping biconnected decomposition.} If $\Gph$ has a
cut vertex, pieces attached at a single vertex of $C$ do not behave like $2$-attached
chords. Decompose $\Gph$ into biconnected blocks in $\Oh(n+m)$ time first (Chapter~10) and
test each block independently.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{labbox}
Trace Algorithm~\ref{ch26:alg-planartest} on $K_4$. Pick the $4$-cycle
$C = 1\text{--}2\text{--}4\text{--}3\text{--}1$. The remaining edges are two chords:
$B_1 = \{1,4\}$ and $B_2 = \{2,3\}$. Around $C$, their attachment vertices alternate
($1, 2, 4, 3$), so $B_1$ and $B_2$ interlace and $I$ consists of two vertices joined by a
single edge. That graph is bipartite: put $\{1,4\}$ inside $C$ and $\{2,3\}$ outside $C$,
and the algorithm returns \textsc{Planar}. Now run it on $K_5$ with
$C = 1\text{--}2\text{--}3\text{--}4\text{--}5\text{--}1$, as in the right panel of
Figure~\ref{fig:ch26-interlacement}. The five chords $\{1,3\}$, $\{2,4\}$, $\{3,5\}$,
$\{4,1\}$, $\{5,2\}$ interlace around a $5$-cycle in $I$, which is an odd cycle and not
bipartite, so the algorithm returns \textsc{NonPlanar}.
\end{labbox}

\section{Coloring planar graphs}
\label{ch26:coloring}

A \emph{proper $k$-coloring} of $\Gph$ assigns each vertex a color in $\{1,\dots,k\}$ so
that no edge joins two vertices of the same color; if such an assignment exists, $\Gph$ is
\emph{properly $k$-colorable}. The smallest $k$ for which $\Gph$ is properly $k$-colorable
is its \emph{chromatic number}, written $\chi(\Gph)$. Every planar graph has
$\chi(\Gph) \le 4$.

\begin{theorem}[Four Color Theorem; Appel--Haken 1976]
\label{ch26:thm-4ct}
Every simple planar graph is properly $4$-colorable: its vertices can be colored with four
colors so that adjacent vertices receive distinct colors.
\end{theorem}

Every known proof of the Four Color Theorem relies on checking hundreds of local
configurations by computer. Allowing a fifth color, by contrast, yields a short proof and a
fast algorithm built from Corollary~\ref{ch26:cor-degree5} and \emph{Kempe chains}.

\begin{theorem}[Five Color Theorem; Heawood 1890]
\label{ch26:thm-5ct}
Every simple planar graph is properly $5$-colorable, and a proper $5$-coloring can be
computed in $\Oh(n)$ time.
\end{theorem}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Remove a vertex $v$ of degree at most $5$ (guaranteed by Corollary~\ref{ch26:cor-degree5}),
recursively $5$-color the remaining graph, and put $v$ back. If $\deg(v)\le 4$, or if two
neighbors of $v$ share a color, at most $4$ colors appear around $v$ and the unused fifth
color goes to $v$. The only case that blocks $v$ is $\deg(v)=5$ with all five neighbors
carrying distinct colors. Figure~\ref{fig:ch26-kempe-chain} shows how we free one of those
colors by swapping two colors along an alternating component, using planarity so that at
least one of two candidate swaps succeeds.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{proof}
Induct on $n$. \emph{Base.} If $n\le 5$, give each vertex a distinct color from
$\{1,\dots,5\}$.

\emph{Step.} For $n>5$, fix a plane embedding of $\Gph$ and choose a vertex $v$ with
$\deg(v)\le 5$ (Corollary~\ref{ch26:cor-degree5}). By the induction hypothesis, $\Gph-v$
has a proper $5$-coloring.

If $\deg(v)\le 4$, or if $\deg(v)=5$ and two neighbors of $v$ share a color, at least one
color in $\{1,2,3,4,5\}$ is absent among $v$'s neighbors; assign that color to $v$.
Otherwise $\deg(v)=5$ and its neighbors $v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4,v_5$ in clockwise order around $v$
carry five distinct colors, which we relabel $1,2,3,4,5$ respectively.

\emph{Kempe chain.} Let $H_{1,3}$ be the subgraph of $\Gph - v$ induced by colors $1$ and
$3$ (all vertices currently colored $1$ or $3$ together with all edges of $\Gph-v$ between
them), and let $K$ be the connected component of $H_{1,3}$ containing $v_1$.

\emph{Case A: $v_3 \notin K$.} Swap colors $1$ and $3$ on every vertex of $K$. Because no
edge leaves $K$ to another vertex colored $1$ or $3$, the coloring of $\Gph - v$ remains
proper. Now $v_1$ has color $3$ while $v_3$ still has color $3$, so no neighbor of $v$ has
color $1$; assign color $1$ to $v$.

\emph{Case B: $v_3 \in K$.} Then $H_{1,3}$ contains a simple path $P_{1,3}$ from $v_1$ to
$v_3$ whose vertices alternate between colors $1$ and $3$. Together with the edges
$\{v,v_1\}$ and $\{v_3,v\}$, the path $P_{1,3}$ forms a simple closed curve in the plane.
Because $v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4,v_5$ appear in clockwise order around $v$, vertex $v_2$ lies on
one side of this closed curve and $v_4$ lies on the other (Figure~\ref{fig:ch26-kempe-chain},
right). Consider the subgraph $H_{2,4}$ of $\Gph - v$ induced by colors $2$ and $4$. Any
path in $H_{2,4}$ from $v_2$ to $v_4$ would have to cross the closed curve by the Jordan
curve theorem: it cannot pass through a vertex of $P_{1,3}\cup\{v\}$ because $\{2,4\}$ is
disjoint from $\{1,3\}$ and $v \notin H_{2,4}$, and it cannot cross an edge because the
embedding is planar. Thus $v_2$ and $v_4$ belong to different connected components of
$H_{2,4}$. Swapping colors $2$ and $4$ in $v_2$'s component recolors $v_2$ to $4$ while
leaving $v_4$ colored $4$, freeing color $2$ for $v$.
\end{proof}

\begin{whybox}
The Jordan-curve barrier in Case~B is where planarity enters the proof. In a non-planar
graph, a $(2,4)$-path from $v_2$ to $v_4$ can jump over $P_{1,3}$ via a crossing edge,
which is why $K_6$ requires $6$ colors. Kempe's 1879 attempt at the \emph{Four} Color
Theorem tried to handle a degree-$5$ vertex using only four colors by performing \emph{two}
Kempe swaps simultaneously; Heawood's 1890 counterexample showed that the first swap can
rewire the second chain so the second swap reintroduces a clash (Exercise~9). With five
colors, a single swap always suffices.
\end{whybox}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{FiveColor}($\Gph$) -- recursive Kempe-chain coloring}
\label{ch26:alg-fivecolor}
\KwIn{a simple plane graph $\Gph$.}
\KwOut{a proper coloring $c:V\to\{1,2,3,4,5\}$.}
\If{$\abs{V}\le 5$}{assign each vertex a distinct color in $\{1,\dots,5\}$; \Return $c$}
Find $v$ with $\deg(v)\le 5$\tcp*{exists by Cor.~\ref{ch26:cor-degree5}}
$c \gets \textsc{FiveColor}(\Gph - v)$\;
\eIf{some color in $\{1,\dots,5\}$ is missing among $v$'s neighbors}{
  assign $v$ that missing color\;
}{
  $v_1,\dots,v_5 \gets$ neighbors of $v$ in clockwise embedding order\;
  \eIf{$v_3$ is not reachable from $v_1$ in $H_{c(v_1),c(v_3)}$}{
    swap $c(v_1) \leftrightarrow c(v_3)$ in $v_1$'s component; $c(v) \gets$ old $c(v_1)$\;
  }{
    swap $c(v_2) \leftrightarrow c(v_4)$ in $v_2$'s component of $H_{c(v_2),c(v_4)}$; $c(v) \gets$ old $c(v_2)$\;
  }
}
\Return $c$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{complexitybox}
Each recursive call deletes one vertex, giving $n$ levels. Maintaining vertices in buckets
grouped by current degree finds a vertex of degree at most $5$ in $\Oh(1)$ time per step,
and each Kempe swap is a BFS over a two-colored subgraph taking $\Oh(n)$ time, so
Algorithm~\ref{ch26:alg-fivecolor} runs in $\Oh(n^2)$ time as written. Tightening the total
time to $\Oh(n)$ either runs BFS simultaneously from $v_1$ and $v_3$ in lockstep so the work
is proportional to the smaller component, or merges two non-adjacent neighbors of each
degree-$5$ vertex during reduction (Matula, Shiloach, and Tarjan). Finding a \emph{four}-coloring
is also polynomial: the 1997 Robertson--Sanders--Seymour--Thomas proof yields an $\Oh(n^2)$
algorithm, though implementing its $633$ reducible configurations is a major undertaking.
\end{complexitybox}

\begin{trapbox}
A tempting shortcut fails: ``every planar graph is $4$-colorable, so run greedy coloring.''
A \emph{greedy coloring} visits vertices in a fixed order and gives each vertex the smallest
color not already taken by its colored neighbors. On an adversarial vertex order, greedy
coloring can spend up to $\Delta + 1$ colors even on a tree or bipartite planar graph, where
$\Delta = \max_{v \in V} \deg(v)$ is the maximum vertex degree. Even peeling vertices of
degree at most $5$ greedily without Kempe swaps only guarantees $6$ colors; getting $5$
requires both the low-degree elimination order and the Kempe-chain repair.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
Planar graph coloring models radio frequency assignment across neighboring broadcast zones
(adjacent transmitters need different channels to avoid interference), compiler register
allocation (variables live at the same program point cannot share a CPU register, and
structured code without arbitrary jumps produces conflict graphs close to planar or
bounded-treewidth), and map coloring itself, which is coloring the planar dual whose
vertices are regions and whose edges are shared borders.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Planar separators}
\label{ch26:separators}

Planarity also guarantees small balanced vertex cuts: any $n$-vertex planar graph can be
split into balanced pieces by deleting $\Oh(\sqrt n)$ vertices, as illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:ch26-planar-separator}. Chapter~27 proves this theorem in full; here we
state the bound and examine its algorithmic consequences.

\begin{theorem}[Lipton--Tarjan Planar Separator Theorem, 1979]
\label{ch26:thm-separator}
Let $\Gph$ be an $n$-vertex planar graph. There is a partition of $V$ into three disjoint
sets $V = A \cup S \cup B$ such that
\begin{itemize}
  \item no edge joins a vertex in $A$ to a vertex in $B$ (so deleting $S$ \emph{separates} $A$ from $B$),
  \item $\abs{A} \le \frac{2}{3}n$ and $\abs{B} \le \frac{2}{3}n$ (the two sides are balanced), and
  \item $\abs{S} \le 2\sqrt{2}\,\sqrt n = \Oh(\sqrt n)$ (the separator $S$ is small),
\end{itemize}
and the partition $(A,S,B)$ can be found in $\Oh(n)$ time.
\end{theorem}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Run BFS from an arbitrary root to partition the vertices into distance levels
$L_0,L_1,\dots$ Two observations combine. First, because $\sum_i \abs{L_i}=n$, there cannot
be more than $\sqrt{n}$ consecutive levels that all exceed $\sqrt{n}$ vertices: near the
median distance (where half the vertices lie closer and half lie farther), two levels of
size $\Oh(\sqrt n)$ lie within $\Oh(\sqrt n)$ steps of each other, and deleting a BFS level
separates shallower levels from deeper ones. Second, collapsing the levels before the inner
cut into a single root leaves a planar graph of \emph{radius} (maximum distance from the
root) $d = \Oh(\sqrt n)$. Adding one non-tree edge to a depth-$d$ spanning tree forms a
\emph{fundamental cycle} of at most $2d + 1$ vertices that splits the plane into an inside
and an outside, and choosing the right non-tree edge balances the two sides with a separator
of size $\Oh(d) = \Oh(\sqrt n)$. Geometrically, $\sqrt{n}$ reflects two-dimensional
perimeter-to-area scaling: a flat patch of $n$ unit squares has boundary length of order
$\sqrt{n}$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{whybox}
\emph{Why $\sqrt n$ cannot be improved.} On the $\sqrt n\times\sqrt n$ grid
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch26-planar-separator}), any balanced separator must contain $\Om(\sqrt n)$
vertices (Exercise~10): a straight cut across the grid uses $\sqrt{n}$ vertices, and any
curved cut separating at least $n/3$ vertices on each side still hits $\Om(\sqrt n)$ rows or
columns. Thus Lipton--Tarjan is tight up to the leading constant. Compare the three regimes:
every tree has a $1$-vertex centroid separator (Chapter~20), planar graphs have
$\Th(\sqrt n)$ separators, and sparse high-connectivity graphs (\emph{expander graphs},
Chapter~34) require $\Om(n)$ vertices to split.
\end{whybox}

\begin{corollary}[Divide and conquer via planar separators]
\label{ch26:cor-sep-divide}
Applying Theorem~\ref{ch26:thm-separator} recursively builds a balanced separator hierarchy,
yielding:
\begin{itemize}
  \item subexponential $2^{\Oh(\sqrt n)}$-time exact algorithms for problems that take
        $2^{\Oh(n)}$ time on general graphs, including the planar traveling salesman problem
        (TSP, finding a shortest tour visiting every vertex once), planar maximum
        independent set, and planar Hamiltonian cycle (the ``square-root phenomenon'');
  \item \emph{nested dissection}: solving an $n$-variable system of linear equations whose
        nonzero pattern is a planar graph (such as the graph Laplacian systems of
        Chapter~31) in $\Oh(n^{3/2})$ time and $\Oh(n\log n)$ \emph{fill} (new nonzero
        entries created during Gaussian elimination), by eliminating variables in $A$ and $B$
        before eliminating their shared separator $S$;
  \item \emph{polynomial-time approximation schemes} (PTASs): algorithms that come within a
        $(1\pm\eps)$ factor of optimal in polynomial time for any fixed $\eps>0$, via Brenda
        Baker's layering method (deleting every $k$-th BFS level for $k = \ceil{1/\eps}$
        drops at most an $\eps$-fraction of a solution and leaves planar slices of treewidth
        $\Oh(1/\eps)$, solvable exactly by Chapter~24).
\end{itemize}
\end{corollary}

\begin{connectionbox}
Separators tie planar geometry to the spectral and linear-system chapters ahead
(Chapters~31--35 and~41). Nested dissection (Corollary~\ref{ch26:cor-sep-divide}) is the
fastest direct solver for planar linear systems and complements the near-linear iterative
electrical-flow solvers of Chapters~35 and~41. In Chapter~32 we also measure connectivity
algebraically via the smallest positive eigenvalue of the graph Laplacian: bounded-degree
planar graphs have a value of $\Oh(1/n)$, matching the existence of $\Oh(\sqrt n)$ cuts.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
Planar separators power road-network route planners (road maps have few bridges/overpasses
and behave like planar graphs; preprocessing shortcuts across small separators answers
continent-scale shortest-path queries in microseconds), very-large-scale integration (VLSI)
microchip layout (Lipton and Tarjan's original application was proving chip-area lower
bounds), and parallel mesh partitioning for finite-element physical simulations, where
putting $A$ and $B$ on separate processors limits inter-processor communication to the
$\Oh(\sqrt n)$ boundary vertices in $S$.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Summary of planar graph properties}
\label{ch26:summary}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Quantity / problem & General simple graphs & Simple planar graphs \\
\midrule
Edges ($n \ge 3$) & up to $\binom{n}{2}$ & $\le 3n-6$ ($\le 2n-4$ if bipartite) \\
Minimum vertex degree & up to $n-1$ & $\le 5$ \\
Chromatic number $\chi(\Gph)$ & up to $n$ & $\le 4$ ($5$-coloring in $\Oh(n)$) \\
Balanced vertex separator & $\Om(n)$ (expanders) & $\Oh(\sqrt n)$ \\
Planarity test + embedding & --- & $\Oh(n)$ \\
Max-flow / min-cut & $\Oh(nm)$ (Orlin) & $\Oh(n\log\log n)$ / $\Oh(n\log n)$ via dual \\
Shortest paths (negative weights) & $\Oh(nm)$ (Bellman--Ford) & near-linear, $\Oh(n\log^2 n/\log\log n)$ \\
Exact Independent Set, TSP & $2^{\Oh(n)}$ & $2^{\Oh(\sqrt n)}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Every row of this comparison traces back to Euler's formula $n - m + f = 2$: face counting
gives $m \le 3n - 6$ and a vertex of degree at most $5$, which drive linear-time testing and
coloring, while two-dimensional boundary scaling gives duality and $\Oh(\sqrt n)$ separators.

\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{What This Made Possible:}
Restricting inputs to planar graphs turns $\Oh(m)$-time traversals into $\Oh(n)$-time passes,
reduces minimum cuts to shortest dual cycles, guarantees five-colorability by local
Kempe-chain swaps, and supports $\Oh(n^{3/2})$ nested dissection and $(1\pm\eps)$
approximations on geometric networks and meshes.

\textbf{Limitations:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Fragility under added edges:} Planarity breaks as soon as a single $K_5$ or
        $K_{3,3}$ subdivision appears (for instance, a few highway overpasses or long-range
        links), invalidating $n - m + f = 2$ and planar face duality.
  \item \emph{Non-uniqueness of embeddings:} A $2$-connected planar graph can admit
        exponentially many embeddings with different face structures, so dual-graph
        algorithms must compute and fix one embedding first. Only $3$-connected planar
        graphs have a unique embedding (Whitney 1932).
  \item \emph{NP-hardness persists:} Exact $3$-Coloring, Maximum Independent Set,
        Hamiltonian Cycle, and Minimum Vertex Cover remain NP-complete even when restricted
        to planar graphs of bounded degree.
\end{itemize}

\end{connectionbox}

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Girth bound]
Generalize Theorem~\ref{ch26:thm-edgebound}: if a connected simple planar graph has girth
$g \ge 3$ (length of its shortest cycle) and contains at least one cycle, prove that
\[
  m \le \frac{g}{g-2}(n-2).
\]
Recover $m \le 3n-6$ and $m \le 2n-4$ as special cases, and use the bound to prove in one
line that the Petersen graph ($n=10$, $m=15$, girth $g=5$) is non-planar.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Both obstructions are needed]
Exhibit a graph that contains a $K_5$ subdivision and no $K_{3,3}$ subdivision, and another
that contains a $K_{3,3}$ subdivision and no $K_5$ subdivision. Conclude that neither
forbidden graph alone characterizes planarity.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Maximal planar = triangulation]
A simple planar graph is \emph{maximal planar} if adding any missing edge destroys
planarity. Prove that a maximal planar graph on $n\ge 3$ vertices has $m = 3n-6$ edges and
that every face in any planar embedding (including the outer face) is bounded by a
$3$-cycle. (Hint: if a face has degree at least $4$, show you can add an edge across it
inside the face or outside it without creating parallel edges.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Self-dual graphs]
A connected plane graph $H$ is \emph{self-dual} if it is isomorphic to its dual $H^\ast$
(written $H \cong H^\ast$). Prove that every self-dual connected plane graph satisfies
$m = 2n - 2$, and construct an infinite family of self-dual simple plane graphs. (Hint:
isomorphism forces $n = n^\ast = f$; combine with Euler's formula, and look at wheels like
Figure~\ref{ch26:fig-dual-graph}.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Implement five-coloring]
Implement Algorithm~\ref{ch26:alg-fivecolor}. Maintain an array of degree buckets ($0$
through $n-1$) so that extracting a vertex of degree at most $5$ takes $\Oh(1)$ time per
step, and implement the Kempe swap as a BFS over the relevant two-color subgraph. Test your
implementation on maximal planar graphs built by starting from $K_3$ and repeatedly
inserting a new vertex inside a triangle and connecting it to the triangle's three corners,
verifying that every edge has differently colored endpoints.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Break a naive tester]
Consider the bogus ``planarity tester'' that returns \textsc{Planar} whenever $m \le 3n - 6$.
Construct an infinite family of connected simple non-planar graphs with $m \le 2n$ that
this tester wrongly accepts. Explain what the recursive cycle-and-interlacement steps in
Algorithm~\ref{ch26:alg-planartest} catch that the $m \le 3n - 6$ gate misses.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Kempe's mistake]
Look up Heawood's counterexample to Kempe's four-color argument (or construct a smaller
planar configuration around a degree-$5$ vertex colored with four colors $1,2,3,4$, say
neighbors colored $1,2,3,4,2$ in clockwise order where both $P_{1,3}$ and $P_{1,4}$ block
single swaps). Show how swapping $(3,4)$ in $v_5$'s component and $(2,3)$ or $(2,4)$
simultaneously can cause the two Kempe chains to cross and create a monochromatic edge, and
explain why a single swap in the five-color proof avoids this interference.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Separator on the grid]
For the $\sqrt n \times \sqrt n$ grid (assuming $\sqrt n$ is an integer), prove that any
partition $V = A\cup S\cup B$ with $\abs{A},\abs{B}\ge n/3$ and no edge between $A$ and $B$
satisfies $\abs{S} = \Om(\sqrt n)$. (Hint: if fewer than $\frac{1}{2}\sqrt n$ rows contain a
vertex of $S$, every clean row lies wholly in $A$ or wholly in $B$; check columns connecting
clean rows.) Conclude that Theorem~\ref{ch26:thm-separator} is tight up to a constant factor.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Outerplanar warm-up]
A graph is \emph{outerplanar} if it admits a planar embedding in which every vertex lies on
the boundary of the outer face. Prove that every simple outerplanar graph with $n \ge 2$
satisfies $m \le 2n - 3$, and prove that a graph is outerplanar if and only if it has no
$K_4$ or $K_{2,3}$ minor. (Hint: add an apex vertex $z$ adjacent to every vertex of $\Gph$,
show $\Gph$ is outerplanar if and only if $\Gph + z$ is planar, and apply Theorem~\ref{ch26:thm-kuratowski}.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Dual of a cut]
Let $H$ be a connected plane graph and $H^\ast$ its planar dual. Prove that a set of edges
$F \subseteq E(H)$ is a minimal edge cut (an edge cut no proper subset of which disconnects
$H$, also called a \emph{bond}) if and only if the dual edge set $F^\ast$ forms a simple
cycle in $H^\ast$. Explain how this correspondence reduces minimum $s$--$t$ cut in a planar
graph to shortest-cycle computations in $H^\ast$.
\end{exercise}
